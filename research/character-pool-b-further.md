# Character Architecture — Pool B, further specimens

**Document type:** Observational specimens. Not a selection and not a recommendation.  
**What this file is:** A second pass at relational and ordinary interpersonal conduct, gathered after Pool A and after a first Pool B pass that stayed close to a short list of leads.  
**Rule used:** Describe what the person does before interpreting what it means. Interpretation, where it appears, is marked.

The first Pool B files already hold Pierre Bezukhov, Margaret Schlegel, Elinor Dashwood, Fred Vincy, and others in novels; Uncle Iroh, Kim Wexler, Tami Taylor, and others on screen; Samuel Pepys, John Keats, Dorothy Wordsworth, James Baldwin, and others in documented lives. Those names are not repeated here.

Three researchers worked separate source domains. Each was told not to rank, not to predict a desired personality, and not to include someone mainly because people flock to them or because they hold the room.

## Novels

### 1. Lila Cerullo
- **Source:** Elena Ferrante, *My Brilliant Friend*; *The Story of a New Name*; *Those Who Leave and Those Who Stay*; *The Story of the Lost Child*. Novel series. Fictional.
- **Posture:** In the neighborhood she starts tasks, corrects, and rearranges plans at close range. With Elena she alternates shared work and sudden exclusion. In the marriage and the shop she often goes quiet and then changes an object, a bargain, or a living arrangement.
- **What she does:** She spends time in other people’s rooms: kitchens, the shoe shop, a house on Ischia, a factory, an apartment with Enzo. She reads faster than the person beside her, rewrites a sentence, redesigns a shoe, or tells a brother or a husband what the deal was. Speech with Elena and the neighborhood is fast and specific. She names who paid whom. When a room turns against her she often stops explaining and works with her hands. With Stefano she is present, then withholds sex, talk, and the story of their child. With Enzo the talk is mostly the next practical step. With Elena the contact runs for decades: arm in arm, homework, mockery, and being called back. Interpretation: the mockery and the collaboration are one continuous tie.
- **Ordinary social life:** *My Brilliant Friend*, childhood. She and Elena walk the neighborhood and sit with schoolwork. Lila finishes first and corrects Elena. In the Cerullo shop she draws shoes and argues with Rino about making them.
- **Social uncertainty:** *My Brilliant Friend*, the wedding. Marcello Solara appears in the Cerullo shoes. She stays through the reception and does not give a speech about it. Afterward, with Elena, she gives a precise account of who sold what.
- **Intimate friction:** *The Story of a New Name*, the shop photograph. She lays the wedding panel on the floor, cuts black paper, and pins it over the figure until the shoes remain. In the marriage she is beaten, goes cold at home, and later tells Stefano that Gennaro is not his son.
- **Peer friction:** Ischia, then the nights after the Soccavo factory. On Ischia she talks with Nino and asks Elena to cover for her. With Stefano present she is short. Later she and Enzo sit over programming manuals. She corrects errors, including her own, and stays on the task.

### 2. Mohun Biswas
- **Source:** V. S. Naipaul, *A House for Mr Biswas*. Novel. Fictional.
- **Posture:** Inside the Tulsi household he talks more sharply than his position allows, then goes quiet or leaves the room. With Shama he quarrels, depends, and returns. With Anand he can drop the sarcasm and speak about his own embarrassment.
- **What he does:** As a sign-painter he moves through Hanuman House as a worker among people who already know one another. He stares, looks away, and shifts paint tins to have something to do with his hands. At meals he is present and extra. He makes remarks the brothers-in-law do not take up. With Shama the ordinary register is nagging, answering back, and, at times, blows. She goes to Hanuman House to give birth and to get away; he stays, then receives her again. With Anand he is first irritated by the boy’s shyness, then, finding him punished, tells a story against himself.
- **Ordinary social life:** Part 1, Chapter 4, “The Chase.” He and Shama run a grocery. She proposes a house-blessing; he rages; after nights of her sighing he agrees. The Tulsis come and barely speak to him. He leaves the ceremony and finds children opening tins.
- **Social uncertainty:** Part 1, Chapter 3, “The Tulsis.” He puts a note under cloth for Shama saying he loves her and wants to talk. She smiles in a way he reads as mockery. He tries to recover the note. Mrs Tulsi takes it. The marriage is arranged around a conversation that has not happened.
- **Intimate friction:** Part 1, Chapter 5, “Green Vale.” Anand has been made to kneel. Mr Biswas tells Shama to stop it and tells Anand about his own trouble in Pundit Jairam’s house. He asks if the boy wants to come with him. Anand is silent. Mr Biswas is irritated and still takes him.
- **Peer friction:** Among the brothers-in-law he offers jokes and insults. The table does not move onto his side. When Owad returns from England, Mr Biswas puts opinions forward, meets scorn, answers with sarcasm, and then has less to say.

### 3. Janie Crawford
- **Source:** Zora Neale Hurston, *Their Eyes Were Watching God*. Novel. Fictional.
- **Posture:** On the store porch she is kept from the talk and stands at the counter. With a husband who assigns her a public role, she answers, then leaves or speaks in front of other people. With Pheoby, and later with Tea Cake, she talks for a long time and takes part in games and field work.
- **What she does:** The amount of speech changes with the person and the room. With Logan she states what she will not do, hears the insult, and walks out to the road. In Eatonville, Joe stops her from addressing the town. She works in the store. When he insults her body in front of customers, she answers at length about his age and his voice. After he dies she runs the store, turns suitors away, and tells Pheoby she is not going to perform grief she does not feel. With Tea Cake she plays checkers and stays after closing. On the muck she picks beans and sits in the evening noise. The frame of the novel is her telling the story to Pheoby inside, while the town stays on the porch.
- **Ordinary social life:** Chapter 10 and the Everglades chapters. Tea Cake asks her to play checkers. She plays. They talk until the people from the ball game come and go. On the muck she works beside him and talks with the crew.
- **Social uncertainty:** Chapters 5–6. The town asks her to speak. Joe says she knows better than to make a speech. She is silent then. She stands behind the counter while men talk on the porch. Her sentences in that room are mostly replies.
- **Intimate friction:** Chapter 4 and Chapter 8. Logan wants her in the field. She says she will cut potatoes and not worship him. He insults her. She leaves through the gate. Years later, with Joe dying, she tells him what the marriage did to her talk. He does not want the account. She continues it.
- **Peer friction:** Chapter 7, the store. She cuts tobacco wrong. Joe recuts it and insults her looks. She answers so the room can hear. A different pattern appears on the muck, where the evening group treats her as one of the players rather than as the mayor’s wife.

### 4. Botchan
- **Source:** Natsume Sōseki, *Botchan*. Novel. Fictional. The narrator is unnamed; Kiyo calls him Botchan.
- **Posture:** He states the offense as soon as he thinks he sees it, gives people insulting nicknames, and treats a slight as something to answer in person. He is loyal, in a blunt way, to Kiyo and, once he decides he was wrong, to Hotta. He leaves the town rather than learn its indirections.
- **What he does:** He arrives from Tokyo as a new mathematics teacher and sorts the staff into nicknames the day he starts. In the staff room he says what he thinks of flattery. With students he reports tricks and gets angry in class. He keeps going to the bath and to dumpling shops after he is told a teacher should not. On a fishing trip he dozes, hears an insinuation against Porcupine, and turns on the colleague who had helped him. When he is told otherwise, he switches. They expose Red Shirt and beat him, then take the boat out. With Kiyo he speaks roughly, writes plain letters, comes back to Tokyo, lives with her, and buries her. Interpretation: affection here shows up as return and upkeep, not as a change in how abruptly he speaks.
- **Ordinary social life:** The first letter to Kiyo. He lists the nicknames, says the place is dull, and says he will be back next summer. Students write up how much tempura he eats. He keeps eating it.
- **Social uncertainty:** The dormitory night-duty. Grasshoppers are put in his bedding. He treats this as a clear wrong and expects the school to punish it. He talks more, not less, when he is unsure. He believes the fishing-boat conversation because it was said in his hearing, and he does not ask Porcupine first.
- **Intimate friction:** The frame with Kiyo. She feeds him and calls him Botchan. He takes the care and speaks roughly. He still writes from Shikoku and comes back to her. He does not stay in the profession. He announces he will not leave Tokyo again and handles her last years and burial.
- **Peer friction:** The quarrel with Porcupine, then the brothel and the beating. He falls out with Porcupine on an overheard insinuation. After he is told otherwise, they align in public, resign, hit Red Shirt and Clown, and leave on the boat. Disagreement is loud and settled by exit.

### 5. Fermina Daza
- **Source:** Gabriel García Márquez, *Love in the Time of Cholera*. Novel. Fictional.
- **Posture:** In public she is formal, brief, and hard to approach. Inside the long marriage she quarrels over household details, stops speaking, and still runs the day. At two moments, decades apart, she ends a courtship in a sentence and refuses the man at the door.
- **What she does:** As a girl she conducts a secret correspondence. When she sees Florentino in the Arcade of the Scribes after her return, she stops the relationship on the spot, writes two lines, returns his gifts, and will not let his mother into the house. In the marriage she keeps a large household and argues with Urbino about soap, smell, and schedule. During one fight she shouts, offers to move to her father’s house, and then lives with Urbino in silence, sending remarks through the children. In old age she bathes him, powders him, and dresses him item by item. When Florentino repeats his vow at the wake, she orders him out. Interpretation: the public sentence is short; the domestic tie is made of repeated physical tasks and of quarrels that go dormant.
- **Ordinary social life:** Chapter 1, the old-age mornings. She wakes, bathes Urbino, and keeps the bathroom usable after years of complaint about how he urinates. They still prod each other over small morning advantages.
- **Social uncertainty:** Chapter 2, the Arcade of the Scribes. He speaks to her in the language of their letters. She tells him to forget it and walks off. That afternoon she sends the two-line letter. She returns every object and refuses his visits.
- **Intimate friction:** Chapter 1, the soap quarrel, and Chapter 5, Miss Barbara Lynch. There is no soap. He piles on other grievances. She shouts and says she will go back to her father’s house. They sleep apart and speak through the children. Fifty years later they still treat that fight as unfinished. Later she tracks a smell on his clothes, asks what is happening, and leaves the city for about two years.
- **Peer friction:** The wake. She receives condolences and controls the house. When Florentino declares that he has kept faith, she throws him out in one burst of speech. At the cinema, earlier, she gives him a courteous smile and does not reopen the old claim.

### 6. Ifemelu
- **Source:** Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, *Americanah*. Novel. Fictional.
- **Posture:** She talks readily with friends and lovers, and she will say the uncomfortable observation in the room. When a relationship or a country stops fitting, she goes quiet toward that person, or she leaves. With acquaintances who need something from her, she is impatient and then sometimes agrees.
- **What she does:** In Nsukka she spends long hours with Obinze, reading and joking. With Aunty Uju she argues about money, Bartholomew, and Dike. In America she says what she thinks a room is doing, first in conversation and then on the blog. With Curt, after she sleeps with someone else, the relationship ends without the remorse he wants. With Blaine she misses a protest he treats as a test and lives through a stretch of his silence. In the Trenton salon she is short with Aisha, then, when Aisha cries, promises to talk to one of the boyfriends. She leaves wondering why she promised. Interpretation: exit and silence are ordinary tools for her, and so is a sudden practical promise once someone stops being an abstraction.
- **Ordinary social life:** The Nsukka chapters. She is in Obinze’s room and among school friends, talking for hours. She is not waiting to see what the group wants her to say.
- **Social uncertainty:** Chapter 1, Mariama’s salon. This is not her usual salon. She tries to read and is short with Aisha. Friends and family doubt she can live in Lagos again. She has not told her parents the plain version of the break with Blaine. Uncertainty here sounds like irritation, half-answers, and one email to Obinze.
- **Intimate friction:** After the assault by the tennis coach she stops writing to Obinze and does not explain. With Blaine, after the missed protest, he stops ordinary talk at home. She stays through that coldness. When Uju calls about Dike, Ifemelu says she will come. Pattern: few words when she is ashamed or finished; argument when she thinks the other person is evading; short logistical sentences in a crisis.
- **Peer friction:** New Haven, with Blaine’s circle, and Chapter 41 with Aisha. She does not attend the protest. The quarrel is withdrawal of warmth. She neither mimics his position on the spot nor leaves the house that day. With Aisha she moves from rudeness to a promise she did not plan.

### 7. Jia Baoyu
- **Source:** Cao Xueqin, *Honglou meng* (*Dream of the Red Chamber*), first eighty chapters. Novel. Fictional.
- **Posture:** With the girls and maids of the household he moves close, cries, quarrels, and comes back the same day with an object or an apology. In formal male company, especially under his father, he speaks when he should wait, then shakes and goes silent when he is shouted at.
- **What he does:** He changes into informal clothes as soon as he can and sits with Daiyu, Baochai, the cousins, and the maids. He cries when people are hurt. He gives away sashes and handkerchiefs. When Daiyu will not see him he stays near her door. With Jia Zheng and visiting scholars he offers names and couplets without waiting, is shouted at, trembles, and still produces the next line when ordered. With Aroma he listens to conditions about study because he does not want her to leave. Interpretation: rank gives him access to these rooms; the conduct inside them is clinging and apology, not command.
- **Ordinary social life:** Chapters 23 and the poetry gatherings. He reads with Daiyu among falling petals and gathers petals so they will not be stepped on. At the poetry meetings he writes when it is his turn, hears the girls tease weak lines, and remains in the group.
- **Social uncertainty:** Chapter 17, the garden inspection, and Chapter 3, the first meeting with Daiyu. In Chapter 3 he asks about her jade, hears she has none, and dashes his own to the floor. In Chapter 17 he walks behind his father and cannot keep from proposing inscriptions. He either overflows or freezes. He has no easy small talk for the scholars.
- **Intimate friction:** Chapter 29, after the temple, and Chapter 34, the handkerchiefs. He and Daiyu fight, cry, and are ill afterward. The content is circular: who cares, the jade, the gift. After the beating in Chapter 33 he sends Daiyu two used handkerchiefs. She writes poems on them. With Aroma in Chapter 19, Aroma threatens to leave and lists how he must behave. He agrees in order to keep her.
- **Peer friction:** Chapter 30. Baochai rebukes him for rudeness, using a fan and a quotation so the correction arrives sideways. He is embarrassed and does not win the exchange. In the poetry club he stays when a cousin says a line is weak. When the critic is Jia Zheng, the same talent becomes a scolding.

## Film and television

### 8. Daniel Holden
- **Source:** *Rectify* (SundanceTV), seasons 1–4. Television. Fictional.
- **Posture:** He enters rooms slowly, handles objects, and speaks in long, oddly formal stretches after silences. Casual back-and-forth is where his timing most often fails.
- **What he does:** With family he often stands aside while other people fill the talk. He answers late, sometimes with a concrete memory and sometimes with “I don’t remember.” He will start a practical task and carry it past what the other person asked for. With strangers he can use a borrowed name and a polite gallery voice, then stop when the exchange asks for his actual history. With Teddy he delivers prison detail in the middle of an ordinary outing. With Tawney he asks for closeness where she is already offering talk or a ride. When an insult lands after a long stillness, he sometimes moves all at once. Interpretation: the pauses read as lag, not as a strategy of withholding.
- **Ordinary social life:** S01E02, “Sexual Peeling.” Teddy takes him to a buffet and a driving range and talks about the tire store. Daniel tells him, in direct sequence, about being gang-raped in prison. He does not scale the disclosure to the outing.
- **Social uncertainty:** S02E04, “Donald the Normal.” In an Atlanta gallery he introduces himself as Donald and talks with a group of women as a visitor with no local history. Later a couple recognizes him. When he says he does not want to keep talking, they turn hostile. He uses a short refusal and does not explain.
- **Intimate friction:** S01E05, “Drip, Drip,” and S02E09, “Until You’re Blue.” After a baptism he asks Tawney if he can kiss her. A season later, at a motel, she asks if he is a bad person. He answers “Yes.” They dance.
- **Peer friction:** S03E01, “Hoorah,” and S03E02, “Thrill Ride.” Janet serves a barbecue she believes is his favorite. It is Teddy’s order. Daniel says he cannot remember the restaurant. Teddy tells him to stay away from Tawney. The next episode Daniel cooks for Amantha. She says the meal is thoughtful and that she cannot pretend she gives a shit. He has no counter-speech in that scene.

### 9. Lee Ji-an
- **Source:** *My Mister* (*Naui Ajeossi*), tvN, 16 episodes, 2018. Television. Fictional.
- **Posture:** She uses very little speech, stays at the edge of rooms, and learns other people’s private talk by listening rather than by asking.
- **What she does:** At the office she does not join standing conversations. She answers in short pieces. The sustained contact in the middle of the series is one-way: a microphone in Park Dong-hoon’s phone. She listens to his subway rides and to the drinking talk with his brothers. She does this alone. When she initiates, the message is narrow. She sends a short text — good luck, and that it is no big deal — because she has already heard him say he wanted someone to tell him that. She does not then explain how she knows. With her grandmother she is physical first: stairs, care, presence. Interpretation: the listening is a substitute for asking, and also a way of being in a social life she does not enter as a member.
- **Ordinary social life:** From about episode 5, she listens to Dong-hoon with his brothers. She is not at the table. Her response, when it shows, is staying on the line, eating, or crying later.
- **Social uncertainty:** Episode 3. He takes her to dinner to thank her. She accepts the setting and uses the proximity to plant the bug. The friendliness of the invitation and what she does inside it do not match.
- **Intimate friction:** Episode 13, and sequences around episodes 8 and 11. She texts him the phrase she overheard him wanting. Separately she cries outdoors while he describes her hardship to people who do not know she is listening. She tells her grandmother that his being among the people close to her is why she is happy.
- **Peer friction:** Episode 5. He helps carry her grandmother up the stairs. Ji-an lets him take part of the weight. There is almost no discussion of the help.

### 10. Nora Durst
- **Source:** *The Leftovers* (HBO), seasons 1–3. Television. Fictional. The series is the source, not the novel.
- **Posture:** She asks precise, invasive questions in an even voice, refuses the comfort people try to hand her, and proposes or ends contact abruptly.
- **What she does:** In work she reads a questionnaire and waits. Strangers who recognize her as the woman whose family departed often soften. She does not soften back. She repeats grocery purchases from the week her family disappeared. She hires someone to shoot her in a vest. Those are private arrangements with a script she controls. With Kevin she can skip the gradual sequence: she proposes leaving for Miami immediately, and when he cites his daughter she answers with “fuck your daughter.” She also stays, and later leaves. With Erika she opens with the questionnaire, then cannot stay once Erika turns the same question back. Interpretation: the questionnaire voice keeps a conversation inside questions she already knows how to ask. When someone else takes the questioning role, she leaves the room.
- **Ordinary social life:** S01E06, “Guest.” She buys the same cereals she bought the week of the Departure. She pays a sex worker three thousand dollars to shoot her in a vest. She keeps the appointment as a repeat service, not as a confession.
- **Social uncertainty:** The same episode, the conference. Her badge is missing. She argues that she wants the orange lanyard, then takes the “Guest” badge. She drinks with people she just met, tells off a speaker, and walks out.
- **Intimate friction:** The Miami proposal, and S03E08, “The Book of Nora.” Years later, in Australia, she listens to a long story from Kevin and then tells one of her own at similar length. She cries. They remain at her table. She does not use a middle style of short check-ins in either scene.
- **Peer friction:** S02E06, “Lens.” She throws a rock through Erika’s window, then administers the questionnaire at Erika’s house. Erika asks what Nora’s children’s last words were. Nora cries, picks up her things, and leaves without answering. Erika later throws a rock through Nora’s window.

### 11. Reginald “Bubbles” Cousins
- **Source:** *The Wire* (HBO), seasons 1–5. Television. Fictional.
- **Posture:** He starts contact, explains what he is doing while he does it, and keeps talking to people who may not answer. When a room asks for a confession, he often tells a joke first.
- **What he does:** On the street with Johnny he narrates the scam as they run it. He warns, repeats himself, and laughs. When Johnny finds out Bubbles is informing, they quarrel. Bubbles stops arguing and leaves him behind. At a meeting he stands, takes the hug and the key tag, and sits back down. With Sherrod the talk is prices, arithmetic, and repeated attempts to send him to school. With Walon the pattern reverses: Walon asks him to say what happened to Sherrod, and Bubbles uses humor and does not tell it. The sister’s house has a rule he follows for a long time: basement, and not in the house alone. The last scene is the rule changing. He goes upstairs and eats with her and her child.
- **Ordinary social life:** Season 1, with Johnny, and S01E07, “One Arrest.” Bubbles assigns roles in the scam. In the meeting he stands, is hugged, takes the key ring, and sits down. He does not make a speech.
- **Social uncertainty:** Season 5, before the finale. He lives in the basement and may not stay in the house when his sister is out. At meetings he makes the room laugh instead of describing Sherrod’s death. After a negative HIV test he talks about having escaped punishment again. Walon tells him he cannot live inside that accounting.
- **Intimate friction:** S04E13, “Final Grades.” Sherrod dies from a poisoned vial meant for someone else. Bubbles goes to homicide, tries to hang himself in the interview room, and asks to be locked up. When Walon arrives, Bubbles turns his face away and cries. Walon puts a hand on him.
- **Peer friction:** Season 4 with Sherrod, and S05E10, “-30-.” Bubbles has Sherrod count stock. They fall out. Sherrod returns and they sell together again, without a formal apology scene. In the finale Bubbles lets Walon read the newspaper profile and then eats upstairs.

### 12. Cathy
- **Source:** *Mum* (BBC Two), three series, 2016–2019. Television. Fictional. No surname is used on screen.
- **Posture:** She hosts, answers, and smooths. The sentences are short. Disagreement usually shows up as a pause, a sigh, or a change of task rather than a counter-attack.
- **What she does:** People arrive and talk at length. Cathy makes tea, looks for glasses, and checks the next practical thing. Her side of the talk is “yeah,” “oh no,” and small follow-up questions. When Pauline criticizes the house, Cathy does not return an insult. She goes quiet or steps out to smoke. Michael is the person she talks to once the room thins. With him the pauses are mutual. She will cry there and not in front of Pauline. In series 2 she asks him, in the middle of a barbecue, why he has not replied to an email. Interpretation: the agreeableness is what she does with volume. It is not the same action as having nothing to say.
- **Ordinary social life:** Series 1, Episode 1, “January.” The morning of Dave’s funeral. Kelly arrives in a bright red dress and talks about Jason. Cathy supplies “yeah,” “good,” and short praise, and keeps the schedule moving.
- **Social uncertainty:** The same episode. Kelly asks whether Jason has talked about marrying her. Cathy says no, and notes that they are still young. Kelly also tells her the previous relationship became physical. Cathy says she is sorry. Pauline mocks the house. Cathy’s response is a suppressed sigh.
- **Intimate friction:** With Michael, after the pressure of that morning, she cries. He stays. She does not turn that into a speech about the marriage. In series 2, “June,” the unanswered email is a rare repeated ask. She does not raise her voice. She keeps hosting around it.
- **Peer friction:** Series 3, “Monday” and “Wednesday,” at Pauline’s house. Michael’s arrival bothers Jason. Cathy and Michael get ready to walk. She does not take the floor to define the relationship for the group. She continues in the same short register while the walk is delayed.

### 13. David Fisher
- **Source:** *Six Feet Under* (HBO), seasons 1–5. Television. Fictional.
- **Posture:** In public rooms he uses complete, polite sentences and a straight back. In private with Keith he explains, retracts, pleads, and snaps, and he usually stays for the rest of the argument.
- **What he does:** At the funeral home and at church he takes roles: deacon, arranger, the person who knows the order of service. When Ruth circles the subject of his sexuality, he does not answer until a later night. When he tells Nate, and Keith wants to be present at that church, David refuses. Keith breaks up with him. David goes back to Nate and denies what he just said. With Keith the fights repeat: one of them states a thing they had agreed; David hesitates or adds a condition; Keith names the hesitation; David produces a longer justification. They do not resolve it in the moment. Years later, with Durrell and Anthony, David is the one saying they should stay. Keith wants to stop. David keeps the argument on the placement.
- **Ordinary social life:** S01E05, “An Open Book.” He goes to church with Ruth and is offered his father’s old deacon seat. At a wake he runs the service in his usual register. The discomfort he does not show there comes out later, about who is allowed to stand next to him in church.
- **Social uncertainty:** The same episode, and S01E12, “A Private Life.” He comes out to Nate, then will not have Keith come to that church. After Keith leaves, David tells Nate he is not gay. While arranging a funeral he hears the dead man’s father speak about gay people and does not correct him in the arrangement room. That night he tells Ruth. The public sentence and the private sentence contradict each other inside a few hours.
- **Intimate friction:** S01E05. Keith says David is a coward for hiding him and ends the relationship. David does not win him back inside the scene. He undoes the disclosure instead. At Nate and Lisa’s dinner he is in a couple argument that spills into someone else’s evening.
- **Peer friction:** S05E06, “The Rainbow of Her Reasons.” They have had the boys for about two weeks. Keith is ready to end the placement. David argues to keep them. Both are parenting. Neither hands the decision off with a polite close.

### 14. Toshiko Yokoyama
- **Source:** Hirokazu Kore-eda, *Still Walking* (*Aruitemo aruitemo*), 2008. Film. Fictional. Kore-eda has said the mother resembles his own; the day depicted is invented.
- **Posture:** She runs the day from the kitchen. She feeds people, starts topics, and makes cutting remarks in the same light voice she uses to offer another helping.
- **What she does:** The visit is the anniversary of her elder son’s death. She peels, fries, and talks the whole time. She does not wait to be asked how she is. When Yukari is new in the house, Toshiko serves her and also comments on her being a widow. She invites the man her son saved. After he leaves, she tells Ryota that once a year she makes that man feel awful, because not having someone to hate is worse. She says this without raising the volume. After most of the family has gone, she eats leftovers with her husband and puts on a pop song he tries to dismiss. She leaves it on.
- **Ordinary social life:** The kitchen before lunch. She scraps, salts, and fries while Chinami is with her. She talks while her hands work. She does not hand the meal off and sit as a guest in her own house.
- **Social uncertainty:** Yukari and Atsushi’s arrival. Toshiko serves them and makes her view of the marriage audible in side remarks. She does not look unsure in the room. The uncertainty sits on the newcomers.
- **Intimate friction:** The annual lunch, then the conversation with Ryota. She serves the man and does not help him relax. Later she states the reason in plain sentences. Ryota argues. She does not revise the plan. She does not cry while explaining it.
- **Peer friction:** The leftover dinner with Kyohei. She puts the song on. He belittles it. She does not take it off. He has standards about what she should cook. She serves the corn tempura anyway, and he eats it. Disagreement is continued activity, not a raised-voice fight.

## Documented lives

Pepys and Keats are already in [real-lives-pool-b.md](real-lives-pool-b.md). The five below are additional. Famous scenes of uncertain provenance were left out: the old Cézanne–Zola rupture anecdote, Haydon’s “Immortal Dinner,” and Darwin at the 1860 Oxford meeting, which he did not attend.

### 15. Glückel of Hameln
- **Source:** Her Yiddish memoir, begun about 1690/91, English in Marvin Lowenthal, *The Memoirs of Glückel of Hameln*. Retrospective family memoir, written for her children. Real. Natalie Zemon Davis, *Women on the Margins*, is a caution that the book is shaped.
- **Posture:** She consults husband, children, or siblings first, then drafts terms, travels, or keeps a betrothal quiet. When a plan fails, she writes the failure as both a money problem and a shame problem, and she states the family tie while describing the damage.
- **What she does:** In the first marriage she describes Hayim as doing nothing of importance without her knowledge. She enters trade talk, drafts a partnership at night, and travels to weddings with children and servants. Matchmaking is face-to-face bargaining. After his death she runs the business and the marriages. She agrees to a second marriage only after telling children and siblings, while hiding the betrothal from the Hamburg council because of a departure tax. With Cerf Lévy she does not describe leaving. She writes that he is still her husband. Interpretation: the moralizing voice makes some scenes neater than a diary would. The recurring acts — consult, write terms, travel, stay — are still visible.
- **Ordinary social life:** The wedding journey for Zipporah. Rumors run on the Hamburg Bourse about whether the match is real. When the wedding comes, Glückel travels with a baby at the breast, the bride, a rabbi, a maid, and a manservant, in company with other families. She calls the voyage merry. The social unit is the traveling household.
- **Social uncertainty:** The second betrothal, about 1699–1700. She keeps it secret because leaving Hamburg would trigger a tax, and because merchants thought she was worth thousands. The people inside the secret are her children, her brother and sisters, and close friends. She writes that they approved, and that it still went wrong.
- **Intimate friction:** The later books, after Cerf’s losses. She had consented because she feared that struggling alone would harm creditors and leave her dependent on her children. She then describes the reverse: she is unsure of a roof or bread, and the children may yet have to take her in. She does not record a public denunciation or a departure. The friction is written afterward, to the children, as shame and miscalculation.
- **Peer friction:** Judah Berlin proposes a partnership while Hayim is ill at the Leipzig fair. Hayim will not decide until they are home, where Glückel can advise. She says they can try it for a year and drafts an agreement at night. The partnership later fails. Separately, a match at Baiersdorf stops because they cannot agree over a thousand marks.

### 16. Sofia Tolstaya
- **Source:** *The Diaries of Sofia Tolstoy*, translated by Cathy Porter. Diary, often written to be read by her husband. Real. The 1910 sequence is taken from her dated entries.
- **Posture:** She argues in the room, then writes the argument in a diary he may read. She runs the house and the visitors, including people she does not want there, and she says when she has been too hard.
- **What she does:** For the first two weeks of the marriage she stops the diary. When he says he does not trust her love, she starts it again and also refuses to cry in front of him. She states the method directly: she always writes the diary when they quarrel. In company she will contradict him, raise her voice, and afterward write that she was too hard. She receives callers in sequence. At the end of October 1910, after he leaves Yasnaya Polyana, her entries are about waiting, not eating, and being kept outside the house at Astapovo until he is dying. Interpretation: the diaries are a second conversation. She says as much.
- **Ordinary social life:** A January entry during Ivan’s childhood illness. She gives him medicine, sits with him and reads, then receives Chicherin, Lopatin, Petrovskaya, and Tsurikova. Tsurikova dines and spends the night. The day is medicine, reading aloud, and a string of callers.
- **Social uncertainty:** 8 October 1862, two weeks after the wedding. Since he said he did not trust her love, she has felt terrible. She does not think she will be able to say what she really thinks. She has too much self-respect to cry in front of him. She goes downstairs to see him. The moment to go up to him passes. She writes instead.
- **Intimate friction:** 31 July 1868. She rereads the diary and writes that she always writes it when they quarrel. She records his claim that what she feels is use, not love. The late sequence is 29 October to 7 November 1910. She has been searching his study for a new will. He hears her and leaves. On 4 November she waits outside the little house. On 7 November she is allowed in only as he draws his last breath.
- **Peer friction:** 3 June 1891. A German merchant spends the day, come to look at Tolstoy and to ask for an article. In the evening she, her sister Tanya, and Lev argue. He shouts. She says she cannot talk to him when he shouts any more than to a barking dog. She writes that she was far too hard on him. In another January entry a follower keeps proposing to Tanya. Sofia writes that it is the family who have to send such people packing.

### 17. Charles Darwin
- **Source:** The July 1838 marriage memorandum (CUL-DAR210.8.2); Emma Wedgwood to Darwin, 21–22 November 1838; Darwin to Emma, November 1838, in Henrietta Litchfield, *Emma Darwin*, vol. 2; Darwin to J. D. Hooker, 11 January 1844; Francis Darwin, “Reminiscences of My Father’s Everyday Life,” in *The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin*, vol. 1. Real.
- **Posture:** He states a difficult fact once, to his wife or to one friend, with an apology for the length. In the house he keeps a short, repeatable evening. In a group after dinner he leaves early and treats more than about half an hour of talk as too much.
- **What he does:** Before the engagement he writes a private balance sheet. Morning calls, receiving relations, and awkwardness sit on the side against marriage. A wife, a sofa, a fire, books, and music sit on the other. He proposes at Maer on 11 November 1838 and tells Emma his religious doubts. Her reply thanks him for the openness and says she would dread concealment. At Down, Francis describes a day that barely changes: letters read aloud, walks, Emma reading while he rests, and, if no guests are there, two games of backgammon. If people stay to dinner, he leaves the dining room with the women, calling himself an old woman, because another half hour of talk can cost him sleep. Interpretation: the apology and the joke are how he stays in contact while limiting it. That is a reading, not something he announces.
- **Ordinary social life:** Francis’s reminiscence, checked against the correspondence project’s account of the routine. After the backgammon he becomes animated, laments his luck, and mocks anger at her score. In 1876 he writes Asa Gray the tally: she has won 2,490 games; he has won 2,795. Emma reads aloud. She plays the piano.
- **Social uncertainty:** The 1838 memorandum, and the November letter to Emma. He lists an infinity of trouble and the picture of living alone in London, then writes “Marry — Marry — Marry.” After the engagement he tells her about dinner with the Lyells and tea with the Carlyles. He likes Thomas Carlyle in the moment. Jane Carlyle’s civil remarks to Emma he cannot make out through what he calls a hysterical giggle. He reports the awkwardness to Emma as a joke. He does not pretend the visit was smooth. The memorandum also contains a racial phrase about slavery, written as a note about lost freedom, then partly corrected on the page. It is included because it is his wording, not because it is a social scene.
- **Intimate friction:** Emma’s letter of 21–22 November 1838. She says that since he left, she fears their opinions may differ widely. She thanks him for his openness. She asks him to read the farewell discourse in John, and says she does not wish him to give her his opinion of it. His documented part is the telling. The long household version of intimacy is the backgammon and the reading aloud, not a recorded series of quarrels.
- **Peer friction:** To Hooker, 11 January 1844. He rewrites Hooker’s self-criticism into a compliment, then says he is almost convinced that species are not immutable, “like confessing a murder.” He apologizes for the length. The cooperation is one letter to one friend, carrying a conclusion he does not yet take into a room.

### 18. Carolina Maria de Jesus
- **Source:** *Quarto de despejo* (1960), English *Child of the Dark*, translated by David St. Clair. Diary kept in the Canindé favela, 1955 entries used here. Real. Audálio Dantas states that he cut repetitions and changed punctuation. The scenes below are the published 15–21 July 1955 entries, not later stories about her fame.
- **Posture:** At the water tap and in the street she answers, argues, and leaves when her can is full or her sack is sold. At home she sets a different meal for each child, slaps a son to get him out of a crowd, and, when she is nervous, says she would rather write than discuss. She tells neighbors that the diary will name them.
- **What she does:** The July days repeat a circuit: fetch water, sell paper and cans, cook, write, go out again with Vera on her arm. At the tap she will join an argument and then walk away when her can is full while the other women are still talking. When women come into the shack, she tells them she is writing a book. Silvia asks to have her name removed and insults her. Carolina turns the insult back. She also borrows coffee, and she notes that when she tries to repay it they will not take it. Interpretation: writing functions, in her own words to the neighbors, as both a record and a threat.
- **Ordinary social life:** 15 July 1955, Vera’s birthday. She finds shoes in the garbage, washes them, and patches them. She trades bottles for bread. She spends 65 cruzeiros on meat, fat, sugar, and cheese. At night she finds João in a crowd, hits him a few times, and has him home in five minutes. 19 July, at the tap, she argues about Jânio and Adhemar, then leaves when her can is full.
- **Social uncertainty:** 21 July. A man asks what she writes. She says: all the messes the favela residents practice. He asks if the children around her are hers. She looks: only two are, but she says yes. She says she has no husband and does not want one. A woman who has been watching says goodbye. Carolina writes that perhaps the woman did not like the answer. Vera asks the man for money. Within minutes the rumor is that Vera was given 100 cruzeiros.
- **Intimate friction:** 15 July, the slap and the man who does not come. 20 July, breakfast is different for each child. She writes that since she cannot give them a decent house, she tries to give them a proper meal. She advises a man with a knife not to fight, smells alcohol, and stops, writing that drunk people do not listen. When she is nervous she does not like to discuss. She prefers to write.
- **Peer friction:** 19 July. She writes that the women beat the children when she is out. She says she will cite everything they do. Silvia calls her a vagabond. Carolina asks what Silvia is doing in the favela if her life turned out the same. The same days include Arnaldo’s bread, the swing-builder, Seu Lino’s coffee, and Dona Mariana at the river.

### 19. Tove Jansson
- **Source:** *Letters from Tove*, edited by Boel Westin and Helen Svensson, English by Sarah Death (2019). Letters to family, Eva Konikoff, Atos Wirtanen, Vivica Bandler, and Tuulikki Pietilä. Real. Moomins are not used as evidence of how she behaved.
- **Posture:** She writes to a person as a way of having them in the room. She gives large parties in town, then uses the island to stop seeing people. When an affair becomes concealment and jealousy, she writes that they should stop. In the same season she can write a proposal that the other person is free to decline.
- **What she does:** To Eva she reports a studio party and then the relief of days on the island with her mother. To Vivica she writes that while she is writing she has Vivica there. The letters are explicit enough that Bandler reprimands her. After Vivica returns colder, Tove describes jealousy and the need to conceal, and in 1947 proposes they stop seeing each other. Weeks later she asks Atos whether they should marry, and adds that if he does not want to, they can talk about something else. To Tuulikki, from the mid-1950s, the letters are about weather, work, and wanting her there. In 1964 she records the move to a barer islet after friends and strangers kept interrupting the quieter island. Interpretation: the island move is a practical boundary, not a stop to correspondence.
- **Ordinary social life:** To Eva Konikoff, Bredskär, 5 June 1952. She has been on the island a week with her mother, after arriving on the verge of tears. She then describes a May Day Eve party at the studio for 20 people, given the evening a newspaper visitor arrived. To the family, Pompeii, 4 June 1939, she writes a long illustrated letter from a sickbed and does not drop the travel narrative because she is ill.
- **Social uncertainty:** Summer 1947, to Atos Wirtanen. She asks whether it would be a good idea for them to marry, and says that if he does not want to, there is plenty else to talk about. He accepts and suggests waiting. The wedding does not happen. The proposal is worded so that he can refuse without a scene.
- **Intimate friction:** Letters to Vivica Bandler, 1946–47. Early on she writes of peace and ecstasy, and of being without guilt. Bandler reprimands the explicitness. Vivica comes back colder. Tove writes that there are anguish, irritation, jealousy, and the constant need to conceal it. She suggests they stop, “and definitely this time.” She goes to the archipelago and starts a cabin. The later letters to Tuulikki do not use that voice. On 4 May 1962, alone with the cat, she describes rain and painting and wishes Tuulikki were there.
- **Peer friction:** The 1952 party is also a business evening: she finishes cartoon synopses the day the visitor arrives, entertains him, then writes that the evening may have misled him. The boundary with friends is the 10 June 1964 letter and sketch, the move from Bredskär to Klovharun. She does not describe a fight with a particular friend. She changes the island. Disagreement, where the letters show it, is handled by stopping the meetings or moving, and by telling a third person what the stopped relationship had been.

## Audit of this further set only

**Well represented.** Speech that changes with the room (Janie, Baoyu, Fermina, Ifemelu, Daniel). Household quarrel that continues for years (Biswas, Fermina, Sofia, Glückel). Being extra inside someone else’s house (Biswas, Botchan, Ji-an, Yukari opposite Toshiko, David in church). Repair or rupture that is a visible act: a letter, a closed door, a handkerchief, a meal, a move to another island, a promise made in a salon.

**Still thin.** A long friendship that is mostly meals, errands, and mild dispute, with no household to manage and no romance. Listening as the main act while also being a member of the group. Ji-an listens, but from outside the room. Workplace cooperation among equals that simply resumes the next day. Adolescence. A person who stays socially inside a tight code of implication and rarely ruptures it. Face-to-face repair after a serious break between equals: several of the real lives write the break instead of sitting through it.

**Same posture twice?** Botchan and Biswas both take offense inside a group that already has rules. Botchan hits the men and leaves within a year. Biswas loses the room, stays related by marriage, and grows old in a house with Shama. Lila and Ifemelu both say a hard specific thing and then change the arrangement. Lila does it inside one neighborhood for decades. Ifemelu stops letters and moves countries. Cathy, Ji-an, and Daniel speak less than the people around them, and the mechanics differ: Cathy runs the gathering by agreeing; Ji-an is not in the gathering; Daniel inserts a heavy disclosure at the wrong moment. Pepys, already in the other file, and Sofia both fight at home and write it down. Pepys buys a dress before the next dinner. Sofia keeps a diary she expects him to read and later waits outside a house she is not allowed to enter.

**What would broaden the pool further.** Someone observed for years in loose recurring group pleasure. Someone whose repeated conduct with friends is mostly short answers. A minute-to-minute household journal of withdrawal and then ordinary welcome that is not a marriage quarrel. Etty Hillesum’s 1941–43 diaries and Ida B. Wells’s 1885–87 Memphis diary were named by the real-lives researcher as unused records of this kind. They are not included here. They are remaining search surface, not a recommendation.

No trait is proposed for adoption. No combination with Pool A is proposed.
