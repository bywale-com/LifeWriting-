The Interview

He had rewritten the sentence twice on the train, and it was still cleaner than the night had been.

The sentence was his. He had put it in the note weeks after the second email, when he could look at the logs without the heat coming back into his face: I believed the provider's timeout meant the message had not been accepted. That belief was the bug. Between Chester and Sherbourne he held the phone low against his knee and tried to put back what the sentence had left out. The timeout had been real. The belief had been real. Between them he had sat for forty minutes at the kitchen table with the laptop warming his wrists, writing, in a scratch file he later deleted, that the provider was inconsistent and that anyone would have retried. He had wanted a culprit with a status page. The sentence he published was the one that remained after that want got embarrassing.

He did not change it on the train. Changing it there would have been another cleaning, done in the dark window so that a man he had not met would meet the better version.

At Yonge he changed to the southbound train and stood. The car was full of people who had already settled into the particular stillness of the second half of a commute. A woman held a coffee and a lanyard. A boy in a Western jacket slept with his mouth open. Wale kept the backpack on one shoulder. Etienne had said, that morning, in the kitchen that always smelled faintly of the upstairs neighbours' frying, "Wear the shirt with the buttons," and Wale had put it on, and had been annoyed at the putting-on, and the shirt was fine. Navy sweater over it. The shoes he had polished at eleven, after his father texted go early, as if earliness were a skill that could be instructed from a parking lot in Etobicoke between drops.

He had not told his father it was an interview. He had said a company downtown wanted to talk about the work. His father heard verdicts in the word interview. Wale had decided, with a quickness he did not inspect, that he would rather be slightly false tonight than narrate a maybe over the phone.

The building was on John Street, south of Richmond, brick, with a dental clinic's name in the window and a directory in which Calder appeared in the same type as a physiotherapist and a company that seemed to sell insurance to other companies. The elevator paused between the second and third floors long enough that he looked at the panel to see if he had done something wrong. Then it rose. The hallway smelled of carpet and, faintly, of someone's microwave. The door with Calder on it in small metal letters was propped open, as if the office had not decided whether it was receiving people or simply open.

A woman at the desk was on a call and also signing a courier's pad. She lifted a finger without looking up, the way a person does who has more than one job and means to finish both. He waited. When she was done she looked at the screen and then at him.

"I'm Wale Omotayo," he said. "For Nadine. Wah-leh. O-mo-ta-yo."

She typed, backspaced, typed again. "I'll put Wale on the sticker, and if the last name is wrong you tell me before it sticks to you." The printer failed in a small way. She wrote the surname by hand. Her handwriting was better than the printer. "Denise. Nadine's on a call for another few minutes. Sit wherever. If you want water, the cups are beside the machine, and the machine lies about being ready, so give it a second."

He thanked her and sat in the chair that faced the hallway rather than her desk, then wondered if that had been a choice about not watching her work, which it had, and whether it looked like he was watching the door for someone important, which it also did. He put his hands on his knees. The backpack went on the floor. He did not take the laptop out.

The man who came in a few minutes later arrived already speaking. Fifty, perhaps, a wool coat too heavy for the week, a phone at his ear.

"I'm in the office," he said. "I'm standing in it. And I'm telling you it isn't in the drafts, because I looked in the drafts. Your system lost the certificate. I am not interested in where the system thinks it put it."

Denise's mouth tightened by a degree. She waited until the call had ended or been ended.

"Paul, Jonah's with a brokerage until ten-thirty. If this is the certificate, it's the same folder as February. I can have someone sit with you."

"It's not February," Paul said. "It's this morning. Hamilton uploaded the renewals and the file is gone. And the certificate is also not in the drafts."

"Sit," Denise said. "I'll tell them both things."

Paul sat two chairs away and stood again to look at a print on the wall, as if sitting were a concession. Wale looked at the carpet. He had, already, a small warm theory: a man frightened of a missing document, a system that had put it somewhere unsurprising, a person who had shown him and would have to show him again. He had seen Ruth do it in the North York office, clicking, not sighing. Showing someone once doesn't always land in the place they look when they're scared. The sentence arrived whole, which should have warned him. He turned toward Denise.

She had already turned to the courier, who had come back because the suite number was wrong. The sentence stayed where it was. He was glad, and also disappointed, and the disappointment told him something he would rather not have been told.

Jonah came down the hall and stopped at the edge of the waiting area. He saw Paul. The calculation that crossed his face was so plain that Wale felt he had looked at something private. Jonah came over anyway, not all the way to sitting.

"You made it," he said.

"Early. My dad."

Jonah nodded as if that were an ordinary explanation. They had met in rooms where people's fathers were not mentioned. "Nadine first. Then Rafi. Don't do the whole theory of it with her. She has a form." He glanced at Paul, who had noticed him. "I'm not in the interview. I just wanted to say hey. Don't thank me yet."

"I was going to."

"I know," Jonah said, and went to Paul with his face already set into the expression of a man who can look at a folder.

Wale sat with the thanks he had not given. It was true he had been going to give it. It was also true that the thanks was for a story he only half possessed: that Jonah, who had been in the room when the work was refused, had later thought of him and said something generous. The phrase don't thank me yet had a remainder in it. He did not know the remainder yet. He knew the beginning.

Pennant Advisory kept an office in a plaza off Dundas, east of Hurontario, above a clinic and beside a tax preparer. The sign in the hallway still had the previous tenant's name ghosted under the vinyl. Wale had gone because Jonah's email said they were looking for a partner to build a client portal for a professional-services firm they advised. The email said partner twice. It also said the budget was flexible, which Wale had learned to hear as a number that would arrive late and be treated as a test of character.

Graham Pell received him in the small boardroom. He had once been an operations director at a bank and had not stopped dressing for it. He shook Wale's hand and said, lightly, "Wally," as if offering a convenience.

"Wale," Wale said.

"Of course," Graham said, and forgot it before the laptop was open.

Jonah sat at the side with a notebook. He was a year or two younger and already had the posture of a person whose work was to remember what Graham would later claim to have decided. A third chair stayed empty. The client, Graham said, had entrusted Pennant with the vision.

The vision was a portal in the firm's colours. Clients would log in and see their status. Staff would stop sending attachments. There would be a feeling of modernity. Graham showed three screenshots, all of them blue, and a spreadsheet of fields. He did not show the process that produced the status. When Wale asked where the status came from, Graham said the girls keep it in the drive. Jonah, without looking up, said the caseworkers keep it in a workbook and in their heads.

"The portal reads the workbook," Wale said.

"The portal replaces the workbook," Graham said. "That's the point. We were told you build systems. We're not looking for a lecturer. We're looking for a front door."

The number, when it came, was thirty-eight thousand, said as a kindness. For that they wanted the door, the colours, the login, the workbook migrated once, and a manual they could hand to a future vendor. Graham used the phrase hand-off with approval. It was, he said, how professionals closed.

Wale looked at the spreadsheet. One column was last email. Another was last email 2. He asked what happened when a client was emailed twice.

Graham smiled with his mouth. "They get a reminder and a reminder. It's not a bank. Nobody dies."

"Somebody calls you," Wale said. "If the thing they're waiting on is a deadline, the second email doesn't calm them. It tells them you don't know which of you is speaking."

He said more than that. He talked about a send that could succeed while the workbook failed to change, and about a row that had to exist before the email so a retry would find it, and about not putting a door on a file two people could edit. He heard himself doing what he had been asked not to do. Jonah's pen had stopped. The room had the feeling of a man discovering that a meeting he had booked was turning into a comment on the work he had been doing before it.

Wale tried to come back to the thing in front of them.

"I can't build that portal," he said. "Not for thirty-eight, and not for more. It would look like the screenshots for a month and then it would rot, and the rot would be in a place I don't operate. What I run, I keep. If the client is an immigration firm, I can put them on it and stay with the part that sends. If they aren't, I can still tell you the shape of the sending problem, and someone else can build the door."

Graham closed the laptop. The click was small. "We're looking for a partner who is excited about the client's brand. I don't think that's you. Jonah will close this out."

In the hall he said, "Thanks, Wally," already turning into a call. Jonah walked Wale down to the parking lot. The light was white and unkind. Jonah looked angry in a way he was trying to convert into manners.

"He was going to pay you," Jonah said. "The colour thing is annoying. The money was real."

"The money was for a thing that would break and have my name near it," Wale said. "Or nobody's name. That's worse."

"You made him feel stupid."

"I wanted the workbook off the table," Wale said. "The stupid part wasn't required. He was frightened of looking ignorant in front of a client who had asked for modern. The colours were a way through that. I saw it and I still gave him a lecture. Those can both be true."

Jonah laughed once, without any pleasure in it. "I'll write it up."

What he wrote said that the vendor had come in high and had not been a fit for a brand-sensitive engagement. He did not write the workbook. He did not write last email 2. He did not write that he himself had said in their heads. Graham forwarded the summary to the client with a line about continuing the search. Jonah saw the forward and felt the relief of a document he would not have to defend, and under the relief a thin precise shame, because the summary was the sort of sentence he had taken the job in order to stop writing.

Wale, at home, did not know about the forward. He knew he had left a false account standing in the room. He also knew he wanted his own account to exist somewhere Graham could not sand it. Two days later he sent Jonah a little more than two pages. No warmth in the greeting. He was not pursuing the portal. If anyone at Pennant still meant to email clients from a workbook, the danger was specific: a send that succeeded, a row that did not update, a retry with no memory. He described the key he would have used, not as a bid. He said he was sending it because the meeting had ended on a description that could hurt someone.

All of that was true. It was also true that he wanted the paragraph findable later, so that if a client was emailed twice, the person who had warned them would have warned them in writing. He left that motive out. He was not yet in the habit of putting it in.

Jonah read the note at his desk, felt lectured, and archived it. He did not reply. He left Pennant seven months later, after a cheaper vendor's reactivation sequence emailed a family three times about a biometrics appointment they had already attended. The principal blamed the coordinator who had owned the vendor. Jonah rewrote his résumé that night. He did not mention Wale in the interviews that followed. He still had the note.

He sent it to Rafi from his personal mail, on a Thursday, with a covering line he rewrote four times.

Rafi had spoken in the kitchen, mostly to his mug, while another engineer left with a yogurt. "I don't need another person who can invert a binary tree. I need someone who has had a job run twice." The March incident was months behind them and still in his teeth. Jonah, rinsing a container that had held rice, heard himself answer before he had decided to.

"There's a guy," he said. "I don't like him, exactly. He wouldn't build a portal for us when there was money. He sent a note about not emailing people twice. I still have it."

Rafi waited, which was his way. "Send it."

Jonah sent it that afternoon and spent the next hour hoping the note had curdled with time, that Wale would read as mystical or pompous or obviously unemployable, so the thing would die in an inbox and not in a room with Jonah's name on it. He wrote a second email, shorter. He's one person. He doesn't have the degree. The website looks like a template. I can introduce, or you can ignore it. He did not write: I told my boss it was the price.

Rafi read the note on the streetcar, standing, his bag between his feet. He had expected a pitch. The opening was a refusal, which annoyed him, because nobody had asked this person for anything and here he was, already declining. Then he reached the key. Case, requirement, window, channel. The worker can die after the provider has accepted the message and before any record of that acceptance exists. If the retry's only memory is the job, you will send again. He heard Sandra, under whom he had worked at the card processor out by the airport, saying that exactly-once was a phrase salespeople sold to people who had never rebooted a worker. He got off one stop early.

At home he opened the Omcoda site. We do not take briefs, build to specification, and hand off. He made a sound. He had seen that sentence, in other clothes, on the sites of men who were severe at kickoff and absent by month six. He clicked the incident because he wanted the pose to fail.

It did not fail. It was dated. It named a worker, a column, a constraint added afterward, and a belief about timeouts. It did not name the client. It did not say journey. Near the end it said what the caseworker still could not see. Rafi sat back. He was interested, and suspicious of the interest, because the March review he had let be edited still stung, and a stranger's honesty is an easy place to set down your own.

He asked, through Jonah, for the migration, if the man would send it.

Jonah had not written to Wale since archiving the note. The ask from Rafi came that night. He wrote it before the errand could cool into something he might talk himself out of.

Rafi Qureshi runs renewals. He said in the kitchen that he doesn't need another person who can invert a binary tree. He needs someone who has had a job run twice. They had one in March. Reminders went out twice to a brokerage's clients. I still had your pages. I sent them. He wants the migration that added the key, and the incident you actually keep, if that isn't the one on the site. There's a developer seat on the service. I told him what I was willing to put in mail. You're one person. You don't have the degree. The site looks like a template.

He did not write that he had said, over the rice container, that he didn't like Wale, exactly. He did not write that the summary Graham forwarded had said price, and that the summary had been his work.

Wale read it at the same table where the laptop warmed his wrists. What he felt first was the note being taken out of a drawer by a man who had never answered it. What he felt next was that he wanted the hour. He sent the migration, and a second account in which Adeola's name had been turned into the firm, and he did not ask what March had cost. Asking would have been the lecture again. Wanting the hour had already made him careful.

On Sunday Rafi read the migration at the kitchen table while his daughter watched a program in the next room with the volume low, on account of the cough. The backfill ran in its own transaction, apart from the constraint. The table was obviously small. The backfill was chunked anyway, as if the table were large. He could not tell whether that was judgment or a pattern copied from people whose tables mattered at a different size. He liked not being able to tell. At the bottom of a parser he had not been invited to read — it was in the folder, and he looked — there was a comment. French boilerplate variant. Model drafted this. I do not fully trust it. One bad classification, QSC, left in as a fixture. Fix.

He wrote to Nadine. I want an hour. No puzzle before that. If he is useless we will know within the hour.

She came and sat on the edge of his desk, which meant she intended to win.

"Chris also had an hour," she said. "Chris was lucid. You liked him."

"Chris had never held a pager," Rafi said.

"Chris told me he was finished being solo. He had the sentence ready. I am not running a process where you fall in love with a document and I am explaining, in July, that we hired a man who was leaving the whole time."

"Then be in it," Rafi said. "I'm asking you not to make him perform a test before a person has talked to him."

Helen, consulted in a channel, sided with neither of them in a way that was a side. Rafi could have the hour. Nadine's screen still happened. They were not opening a founder track. The seat was Software Developer, on renewals, and the band was the band.

Nadine put Wale's name at the top of a private list she kept of people she would not let herself be charmed by. Then she took him off it, because she had not met him, and the list itself was a charm against her embarrassment about Chris. She kept the questions Chris had caused her to write.

"Wale?" Nadine said from the doorway. She said it correctly. He wondered whether Denise had told her, or whether she had checked, and then he stood and the wondering was over, as it should have been.

The room was small. The window faced the brick of the next building. On the table: her laptop, a printed page turned down, a glass of water she had poured and not drunk. A leak at the base of the glass had started a dark coin on the table and had not yet reached the paper. He saw it and left it, because it was not his glass, and sat.

"Thanks for coming in," she said. "This one is mine. Rafi will want the system. I want to know whether the seat is something you want, or something that got you on the subway. People apply here for stranger reasons than the posting."

"Jonah said there was a renewals service that had already sent a thing twice," Wale said. "That's why I came."

"He told you that."

"He said Rafi was tired of trees and had a job that ran twice. The tree part was a joke. I believed the job."

She looked at him. He understood, a second late, that he had carried a kitchen sentence into her room, and that Jonah might not have meant it to travel. "If that was loose, I'll leave it there," he said. "I'm here because I run a system with the same kind of failure in it. I want to see the kind that has more than one person standing next to it."

She let the kitchen sentence go. He was grateful, and then unsure what the gratitude was for. She might have been sparing Jonah. She might have been keeping a mess off her agenda. He did not know her well enough, and he tried to remember that he did not.

"Tell me what you do," she said, "in a way I can repeat to Helen. Helen is not in today. She will not open your links."

He had rehearsed this and the rehearsal had made it longer. He started again.

"The company is Omcoda. One person. The product is Tower. Two immigration firms use it. North York: a lawyer, Adeola, and her caseworker, Ruth. They use the monitoring and the emails. Brampton: Harpreet Gill. She pays for the monitoring and does not allow the system to email her clients. The data is in Postgres. One application, in Montreal, ca-central-1, because the firms ask where it lives. A worker wakes up and looks at deadlines. Medicals, biometrics, documents, things that expire on a date whether or not anyone is in the office. When a window opens, the worker writes down that it means to tell someone, and then it tells them. I spend most of the actual effort on that first write. The firms pay a monthly amount. It isn't large. It is large enough that if I am wrong, a person who is already waiting on a file hears from the firm twice, and the second time frightens them."

She wrote quickly. "One person."

"Yes."

"And one of the two firms doesn't use the part you just called the point."

"Harpreet doesn't trust the email," he said. "She was right not to, in the spring. I think she's wrong now. She hasn't moved, and I stopped arguing after the second call. The arguing had turned into me wanting a cleaner fact."

The pen paused. "A cleaner fact."

"Both firms on the full thing would be easier to say out loud," he said. "She wants her clients not to get a machine's mail. I was treating her caution as a smudge on the description. It isn't. It's her caution."

Nadine's face did the small work of translation. "I'm going to ask you something plainer," she said. "It helps me if you let it stay plain. Tell me about a disagreement, and what happened afterward."

The Mississauga room was already in his mouth. He could have given her the version that fit a form: he had declined scope, the client had gone another way, everyone had been professional. Jonah had warned him, in the hall, about theory. The short version was also Graham's document. He found he did not want Graham's document in this room, not because Nadine had earned a confession, but because he was tired of that particular tidying and he could feel himself reach for it.

"A consultancy asked me to build a portal," he said. "Thirty-eight thousand. Workbook underneath, colours on top. I said no. I said the workbook would send duplicates and the portal would make the duplicates look finished. The principal heard an insult. Jonah worked for him then. He was in the room. I didn't get the work. Jonah had to write the summary. The summary said I'd come in high. Two days later I sent him a note about the email problem. Not a proposal. I told myself I sent it so they'd be safer if they hired someone else to build the door."

"And that was why."

"It was one why," he said. "The other was that I wanted the no written down in my words. The meeting ended in his. I minded that. On the day, the second reason felt like being clear. A lot of it was pride. I don't know which was larger. I'm not saying that to be even-handed. I mean I actually don't know. I liked the second reason more, which makes me trust it less."

The leak reached the paper. He moved the glass onto a tissue on the far side of the laptop while he was still talking, so the moving would not become its own polite event between sentences. He was not certain he had kept it from being that.

"If the form needs the pieces," he said, "the disagreement was with the principal. I said the thing. I lost the money. I'd refuse it again. I'd write the note again, and I'd either put both reasons in or I wouldn't send it. I haven't picked. That's the part I don't have a result for."

She looked at the darkened corner of the page, then at him, and for a moment the next question was not already waiting. "People usually choose one reason before they sit down," she said.

"I chose one in the note," he said. "I'm trying not to do that in here. It's making the answer worse, I know."

She wrote, and he had the sense of a word begun and replaced. He did not lean.

"School," she said, ordinary again, which closed something and was also a kindness. "The résumé says Toronto Metropolitan. It doesn't say you finished."

"Two years," he said. "Computer science. I stopped when Adeola's firm went live. I told myself one term. I haven't enrolled."

She waited. The wait was practiced and not cruel. It sat between them with the glass. He felt a speech arrange itself: the work had been a school, he still meant to go back, he could talk about isolation levels if that helped. He recognized the speech as the thing the wait was built to pull out of people. He drank from the paper cup. The machine had not been ready.

"I'm not enrolled," he said. "I don't have a date. Saying I intend to isn't a date."

She nodded and went on. He understood that the silence would be a mark of its own, and that he would not see the mark, and that this was allowed.

"What do you want to be paid," she said.

He had looked, the night before, at numbers belonging to companies that flew people somewhere and called the day a loop. In the elevator he had been carrying one hundred and thirty. In the room, against the brick, the number showed itself as borrowed.

"I was going to say one-thirty," he said. "Those were other jobs. I don't know your band. I didn't ask Jonah. I didn't want him standing between me and a price."

"Ninety-five to one-fifteen," she said. "Base. A bonus that is not a story. You can take the day. You don't have to be flexible for me in the room."

"I need the day," he said. "Not as a move. I have to put the firms' money next to it, and the hours I'd still owe them at night, and see if the number survives that. If I tell you it's fine now, I'll be making it up. I'd rather make it up tomorrow, in a note, where I can see myself doing it." He winced, not theatrically. "That's not better. I'll look at it properly."

"All right."

She asked about a difficult client. He told her about the column Harpreet had wanted, a field that existed so Brampton could keep being Brampton. He had said no too fast on a call, and had heard his own speed, and had called back. Three weeks later he had shipped a note field that was the column in other clothes, because she needed a place to put the sentence she was already writing in a margin.

"So you gave her the custom piece," Nadine said.

"I gave her a note," he said. "I didn't build her another program. I care about that difference. Helen might not. I'm not going to pretend it's a large integrity. It was a text box. The part I got wrong was the first call, where I heard a threat to the model and she was telling me where her staff write things down when they're on the phone."

Nadine's mouth moved, not quite a smile, and not for his benefit. "That one I can repeat," she said.

Then the question about the future, dressed up as a question about how he liked to work. What the next years were, if Omcoda remained his and the seat was also his.

He answered at the wrong height first. "There are failures I can't see by myself. I have one worker. I can think about a second one and I have never been in the room while the second one was actually alive. Calder, if Jonah's right, has already had the ugly form of my problem. I want to work beside the person who was there. I'd keep the firms. I would not tell them I'm on their pager all day if I'm here. I haven't counted the hours. That's inside the day I asked you for."

Her face did not change. The steadiness was the information. He saw flight risk land in her, as clearly as a flinch, and the seeing removed the hope that he had sounded safely committed.

"You're asking if I'd leave," he said.

She didn't walk it back. "I'm asking whether I'd be explaining it to Helen in a quarter. I've given that explanation. I didn't like getting fluent at it."

He almost said he understood, and stopped, because understanding her would have sounded like a bid for leniency. "I can't prove I wouldn't leave. If someone proves it in a room like this, it's a sentence they brought. What I can say is that leaving unfinished work costs me more than leaving a title. That's not a promise. It's the pattern. I left the program with the work unfinished on that side. I'm not offering it as a good trait. It's the thing you'd actually be using to bet."

She was quiet. The theory Jonah had named gathered in his throat, the general principle about commitment and craft. He kept his mouth shut. She watched him keep it shut.

"Rafi's late," she said. "Something about the morning run. You can sit with Denise. If the seat is wrong I'll tell you. I don't use silence as the no."

"Thank you," he said. At the door he remembered. "Your glass was leaking onto the page. I moved it. I don't know if that was the form."

"A copy," she said. "I saw you move it."

Paul was gone. Denise was eating yogurt with a spoon that had come from home, and clicking through a sheet. She nodded at the chair. He sat. The relief he had expected after the first room failed to show. What arrived instead was the pride sentence, replaying, accurate, and slightly enjoyed. The enjoyment didn't make it a lie. It made the sentence warm in the hand, which was its own problem. He opened the incident note on his phone, found the line about the unique index making a second send impossible, and left it. Editing the line on their network, ten minutes before the man who had read it, would have been a small fraud even if the new words were truer.

He thought of Nadine's face at the moment he said he would keep the firms. He did not think she had decided to stop him. He thought she had put him somewhere she would not spend her own capital, and that the place was reasonable, and that a reasonable place could still be a wrong one, and he couldn't tell which from the chair. He let the two possibilities sit without building a grievance out of either.

Rafi came from the stairwell with his phone in his hand and his badge backward. Wale had made him younger. A sentence said to a mug had sounded like someone his own age, complaining on the way out of a kitchen. The man in the doorway was nearer forty, with a softened lanyard, and the look of a person who had meant to be on time and had spent the intention on something else.

"Wale. I'm behind. Come through. I want to say why before we both pretend I was in a meeting."

On the short walk from the chairs Wale assembled a manner for a man who arrives late as a kind of rank. He disliked the manner and kept it ready. The room they entered was a corner, not a conference room. The whiteboard had been erased badly, so that window and suppress still floated in the blue. Rafi left the door an inch open, put the phone face down with a visible effort, and sat.

That's why Paul is here, though Paul thinks we lost a certificate. The certificate is an older argument and it is in the drafts folder and he doesn't believe in folders. The upload is fine. I stayed on the stairs until the lag dropped, because if I had come in and been pleasant and it hadn't dropped, I would have liked myself less for the rest of the day."

The manner had nothing to attach to. Wale let it go and felt briefly unarmed.

"Was the double upload March," he said, "or a relative of it."

"You didn't get March from the lobby."

"No. Jonah, when he asked me for the migration. He said reminders, twice, to a brokerage's clients. I shouldn't have carried the month in. The lobby I did squash. Paul said you'd lost a thing. Denise said the certificate was in the drafts. He said the upload was this morning. I folded those into one story."

Rafi was quiet for a second that belonged to Jonah, and then he put that second away. "I'll talk to him about the month. Not in the hall, and not today. March was mail," he said.
