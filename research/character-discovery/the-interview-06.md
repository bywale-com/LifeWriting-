The Interview

The tape on the directory had curled, and the suite number under it was not the suite number written on the tape. Harton was on three. The tape said 305, in marker, as if the building had been corrected by someone in a hurry and then left to the glue. He looked at it long enough to be annoyed that he was looking at it, and went to the elevator.

The elevator smelled faintly of clove mouthwash from the dental office on two. He took it to three anyway. In the small glass lobby a man about his own age sat behind a desk with a bowl of mints and a printed list, and the list had already been written on.

“Wale Omotayo,” he said, and spelled it before the pause could arrive. “For Andrea D’Souza.”

The man found the line, crossed something out, and printed a sticker that was wrong in the middle vowel. He looked at the sticker and then at the screen.

“Omotayo,” Wale said again, same pace, no extra warmth and no extra edge. “The second vowel is an a.”

“Sorry.” The man reprinted it. His name was on a little plate: Martin. “Water?”

“Please.”

He sat with the cup where he could see both the inner door and the window onto Richmond. The other chair faced a wall of photographs of people in lanyards, all of them apparently delighted to be renewing something. He had chosen without making a point of it. Martin went back to a shipment that would not be signed for. A courier insisted that someone on three was expecting a lab sample. Martin said the lab samples went to two, and that he could not sign. They were both right, and the sample sat on the counter between them in a bag with a red stripe. Wale watched for a minute. He liked a stuck process when it was in the open and small. He did not like it enough to speak. It was not his door, and he did not know what the red stripe meant.

Andrea was not there at the hour. He set the cup on his knee and did not take his phone out. The phone had Samira’s message on it from a quarter to twelve, a photograph of a checklist with one date circled twice, and he had decided on the streetcar that he would not open the date in a lobby. If he opened it he would start answering it, and if he started answering it he would be half in Adeola’s office when someone came to collect him.

He had almost written to Sylvie Hartmann that morning, standing at the counter while the kettle clicked off and on again because he had lifted it too soon. The posting said a bachelor’s, or equivalent practical experience. He could have put the fact in a sentence and sent it before anyone cleared a room. He had not found a way to say it that was only the fact. Every version arrived either as an apology or as a request to ignore the line. He had put the phone face down and drunk the tea too hot. Sitting under the photographs, he could still feel the place where the sentence should have gone.

Martin and the courier finished without him. The bag went back into the crate. The lobby was quiet in the way of offices that have spent their noise in the morning.

He thought of Eli’s email then, not as a document but as the reason the sticker existed. He had seen it only afterward, forwarded by Eli with the sheepish line, *I toned you down and I still think I oversold you.* That was not true in the way Eli meant it. Eli had not sold him. Eli had described an afternoon and attached a note, and had left out the part that would have made the afternoon easier to dismiss.

The afternoon had been in February, above a pharmacy on Yonge a little south of Finch, in the second room of Banjo Immigration, where the light was always a little too white and the radiator knocked. Adeola had written to him before noon in the voice she used when a vendor was about to become a story she told other people. Three families. The system said the windows were closed. The families were not late. He was to call her, or they were done.

He had gone up the stairs instead of calling, because a call would have been him defending a job he had not yet watched. Samira was on the phone in the front room, one hand over the mouthpiece, and she had given him a look that meant both *she is angry* and *I am glad you came*. Eli was in the back with his coat still on the chair and a sweater under it, which was how he worked when the door opened all day onto the stairwell. He had the three files printed, because Adeola trusted paper when she intended to win.

“Show me a new one,” Wale said. “Not one of these. Pretend I just walked in with a passport and a list.”

Eli created a file. He was quick with the screen and slightly ashamed of being quick, as if speed would be counted against him if the speed had caused the trouble. When he came to the date, he put in the day he was sitting there. His eyes flicked up.

“That field,” Wale said.

“Ready to file.”

“Are you ready to file?”

“I just opened it.”

“The screen doesn’t say that.” He turned the laptop a little, not taking it. The label sat under the box with the calm of a thing that had been named by someone who already knew what the date meant. “It asks you for a date you don’t have yet. You put the date you do have. I would put it too.”

Eli’s shoulders dropped a fraction. In the doorway Adeola stood with her arms folded, a pen in one hand, her braids pinned up for the day. She had heard enough.

“So it is your screen,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Not his typing.”

“It’s his typing of the date the screen invited.” Wale kept his eyes on the field, then looked at Eli, because it was worse to say this about him to her. “You still typed it. If we pretend you didn’t, the next person will type it for the same reason and we’ll call that one a mystery.”

Eli let out a breath that was not quite a laugh. Adeola did not soften on a schedule. She came in and tapped the printed files with the pen.

“And these three.”

“I’m not going to flip them from the hallway,” he said. “The job was wrong in the way it decided, and I don’t know yet if it was wrong about each family. Samira has to look. I’ll stop the job from touching them again until she does. Then I’ll change the label, and I’ll stop the date from being earlier than the last thing that was actually done.”

“Today,” Adeola said.

“The stopping, today. The looking, today, if Samira has the afternoon. The constraint when I’m sure I won’t lock her out of a real file.”

Adeola studied him with the irritation she reserved for answers she could not usefully refuse. “You came all the way up here to tell me you will not press the button I want.”

“I came up here so I wouldn’t do it on the phone to get you off the phone.”

She made the small sound she made when a person had been accurate and inconvenient. “Eli, give him the letters.”

He had stayed until the light outside went blue. Samira found that two of the families were still inside the window if you counted from the last completed item, and that one was late for a reason that had nothing to do with the label: a document had been promised and had not arrived, and the file had been carried along by hope. She was gentler with that one than the screen had been. He watched her write the reason in the margin and felt the particular relief of a rule that had failed in public and could be pointed at. On the streetcar home he drafted the incident note in his head and disliked the first draft, which made him quicker than he had been. The note that he posted, on the plain page Omcoda kept for such things and nowhere else, began with the sentence *I blamed the worker. The worker closed the window it was given.* Eli had sent Sylvie that page, and a picture of the transition list with the names of the families removed, and four lines of his own.

Wale had those lines from the forward. Eli had written them late, from the Harton office, after a woman named Gwen had told him in a retro not to bring her outside heroes. He had waited until Gwen was gone. He had not said *brilliant*. He had not said *hire him*. He had written: *This is the person who noticed the screen was teaching me to enter the wrong date. We are doing the cousin of that on reinstatement. I am not asking you to take my word. The note is short.*

He had not written that Adeola had been ready to end the contract. He had not written that Wale had no degree. He had not written that, an hour into the afternoon, Wale had been short with her — “Then stop reading me the logo and let me see the row” — and that she had stared at him until he apologized, not for the request but for the way it had come out. Eli had left that out because it made nobody look the way a recommendation is supposed to look. Wale, reading the forward on a later night, had understood the omissions and had not felt managed. Eli was allowed to refuse to turn a bad hour into a reference.

Sylvie had opened the note on the streetcar, on the 504, because the subject line had annoyed her into it. *Ready-to-file is not a feeling.* She had expected a post with a moral at the end. It was three pages, close-set: the job, the label, the bad rows, the backfill that would run only where a checklist completion existed, and the admission about the worker. She had been irritated to be interested. Implementation people recommended engineers for social reasons. She had clicked through Omcoda that night anyway, after the dishes, and had found the sentence on the front page about not taking a brief and handing the result back. It had struck her as a slogan and then, unwillingly, as a description of a fight she was tired of having with her own sales staff.

She had not asked for a call. She had asked Eli whether the man would send her the key he used so a window did not notify twice. The reply came back exact, a little longer than the question, with the pathway version inside the unique key. She had marked it to read on Saturday. On Saturday a second message was waiting. He had been wrong about the version. The version was a column on the row. If it lived in the key, a published correction would mint a second window and a second notice for the same family. He was sorry to have wasted the first read. He had written *sorry* once, and had not decorated it.

That second message was why she told Andrea to put him on the calendar. It was not a favor to Eli. She said so twice, because Andrea had heard the sentence people use when it is a favor. Paul Okada, who managed the team, was not going to be in the building. A dental college had a renewal weekend coming, and their book would not load for a slice of registrants, and Paul had said, without romance, that if Sylvie wanted the hour she could take the hour. He was not moving the college. Andrea had insisted on going first, the same afternoon, so that a staff engineer’s curiosity would not become an unofficial loop. Sylvie had said, “If your screen is a performance, I will not continue it.” Andrea had said that was not how screens worked. They had left it there.

The inner door opened at eleven minutes past the hour. Andrea came through with a laptop under one arm and a badge that had flipped backward. She was already apologizing, in the tone of a person who apologizes often and means it as far as the next meeting allows.

“We ran over with a college,” she said. “Thank you for waiting. Wale, yes? Did I say that right?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I practiced it and then I didn’t trust the practice.” She smiled. The smile arrived slightly before she had finished looking at him. “Come on through. Do you want anything? You have water. Perfect.”

The room was called 3B and was colder than the lobby. A table for six held the two of them at one end. She took the head without seeming to choose it. He put his coat on the next chair and sat at the corner, because the head of the far side faced the glass and the glass faced a hallway of people passing with bowls. He did not explain the choice. Andrea opened the laptop and angled it, not hiding it and not offering it.

“I’ll keep us to the time we still have,” she said. “I know Sylvie is after me, and I don’t want to cheat her. This is the structured part. Sylvie will do Sylvie’s part. Tell me, at a high level, what you’ve been building. Assume I can follow a product and I will not follow your schema.”

He had expected the request to be harder to start. It was not hard. It was only a question of altitude.

“Omcoda runs a system called Tower for small immigration practices,” he said. “Not a website for them. A system that watches whether a client is still inside a program’s window, and that brings a quiet file back to a person before the quiet becomes a miss. I operate it. We don’t build a custom version and leave. If a firm wants something Tower isn’t, I say no.”

“How many firms?”

“Three.” He heard the cleaner number he had almost used and stepped on it. “One of them I undercharge.”

Andrea’s eyebrows went up, a genuine movement, not a technique. “Why?”

“I took them down on a morning they were filing. The alert came to my email. My phone was in the other room. Forty minutes. Adeola — she runs that firm — couldn’t submit what she had planned to submit. The undercharge is the rest of the year. It doesn’t make the forty minutes smaller. It keeps me from pretending the outage was a story about how fast I brought it back.”

She typed. He could not see the words. He could see that there were not many of them. He felt the familiar dissatisfaction of a true thing being entered into a box that would have to be comparable to other boxes later. He let it pass. The forty minutes were not going to be improved by his supervising her notes.

“And you were recommended by Eli Navarro,” she said. “He used to work at that firm.”

“He was the person entering the dates. He works here now. He didn’t ask me before he wrote to Sylvie. I would have said yes. I’m not sure that matters.”

“It matters a little,” Andrea said. “People get recommended by friends who like them. I like Eli. My job is to find out whether we’re looking at the work or at Eli’s relief.” She glanced up. “That isn’t a trick. He told me he was relieved when you didn’t blame him. Relief is not a technical reference.”

“No,” Wale said. He was quiet for a second, not to be grave, because the distinction had pleased him and he did not want to answer before he knew what he thought of it. “He should have been relieved. The screen was teaching the wrong date. He also typed it. Both of those sat in the room. If he had only been relieved, I don’t think he would have sent Sylvie the note. He would have sent her a nicer story.”

Andrea nodded as if he had returned a form correctly. “Walk me through a time you influenced someone without authority.”

The phrase cooled him. He did not frown exactly. His face went a degree more still, which was what irritation did when the other person had not earned a sharper response. He turned the cup.

“I don’t know how to use that one cleanly,” he said. “I’ll tell you a disagreement. You can decide what it counts as.”

“That’s fine.”

“Adeola wanted Tower to text clients on its own when a window hit thirty days. I wouldn’t. The text would arrive on a person’s phone at whatever hour the job ran, in the firm’s voice, and the firm had not actually agreed the sentence. A wrong sentence at night is my work sitting on someone’s kitchen table. She said I was protecting myself.” He looked at the table, then back, because the next part was the part people skipped and he did not intend to skip it. “She was right that I was. I told her so. I also thought the client was being protected, and that those were not the same protection. She disliked the ‘yes, that too.’ We did not have a meeting where anyone was coached. She was standing next to the printer. I built a queue a caseworker sends, instead of a job that sends. She uses it. She still thinks I cost a family a day. I think the automatic text would have cost a different family a worse night. We don’t have a sentence that resolves it.”

Andrea’s pen, which had been moving in the margin of a printed sheet, stopped. “What was the business outcome?”

“She uses the queue.”

“And the relationship?”

“She still calls me when the file is wrong. She still thinks I am too slow about messages. I would rather have that than a relationship that survived because I agreed to a text I couldn’t stand behind.”

“Some hiring managers would hear inflexibility.”

“They might.” He said it without heat. The room was cold and his hands were not. “If the firm is the one who has to live with the family’s reply, there are days she should win and I should build the thing I dislike. She hasn’t convinced me this was one of those days. That’s a real disagreement. It isn’t a method.”

Andrea smiled again, smaller. “What would your customers say is your superpower?”

He blinked. The word sat in the air with its little cape. He did not smile at it. He also did not repeat it back to her in a parody voice, though the parody arrived in his head fully formed and he refused it, not as a virtue, because it would have made the next ten minutes about his taste.

“They wouldn’t,” he said. “Adeola would say I come when the file is wrong, and that I don’t make her sit through a tour before I look. Samira would say I ask her to show me the new one instead of defending the old one. I don’t think either of them has a word they use.”

She wrote a short word. He saw the size of it and felt a thin, specific annoyance: the afternoon in the white room reduced to a trait that could be scanned. He almost said, *If you need a word, don’t use a heroic one.* He didn’t. A wrong adjective in a recruiter’s margin would not change a row in anyone’s database. He could live with being slightly misfiled. He noticed that he would not have lived with it if Sylvie misfiled the key. The difference steadied him, and then, a moment later, bothered him, because he was not sure he had earned the right to decide which misunderstandings were cheap.

“Why Harton?” Andrea asked. “You have your own thing. Founders usually come in wanting to advise, or wanting us to buy them, or wanting a salary while the thing finds itself. Which version am I in?”

“None of those.” The answer was quick, and then he slowed himself on purpose. “Tower is real. I’m not shopping it. I want a renewals system that already has other people in it, where the cost of a wrong window is a person who can’t work, not only a family waiting on a consultant. Harton already has that book. Eli said you calculate reinstatement from the day someone types. If that’s the work, I want the interview. If the work is a layer of language on top of a book nobody is allowed to change, I don’t.”

“The posting is for a product engineer on Renewals,” she said. “Bachelor’s, or equivalent, and three years in a product company. I need to ask this directly. Do you have the degree?”

Here it was, and he had not put it first, and the not-first was suddenly as present as the cold in the room. He felt it in his neck.

“No. I’m partway through the courses. Not close enough to speak as if the line is a formality.” He held her eye. “I saw the posting. I should have written you before you booked the room. I drafted it this morning and didn’t send it, because I couldn’t get the sentence to stay a sentence. That’s worse than not having the degree. The degree is just a fact. The draft was me trying to arrive without the fact.”

Andrea sat back. The smile did not come. For a moment she looked like someone doing arithmetic on a form she had not wanted to reopen.

“Candidates usually bury that,” she said. “Or they say ‘equivalent’ and make me dig. I appreciate that you didn’t make me dig. I also have a screen that says the posting isn’t decorative. Paul is the one who can treat experience as equivalent. Paul is in a college incident today. I can’t wink at a line because Sylvie liked an email.”

“I’m not asking you to wink at it.”

“What are you asking?”

“For the conversation I came for. And for you not to be surprised later.” He heard the tidiness of that and added, “Surprised in the debrief, I mean. You’d have a right to be annoyed. You cleared an hour.”

She looked at him as if the annoyance he had handed her had arrived in the wrong shape, smaller than the annoyance she knew how to process. “All right. Compensation. What are you expecting?”

“The posting didn’t have a band. What’s the band?”

“We usually get a number first.”

He was quiet. The radiator in this building did not knock. The silence was only the length of time it took him to decide that guessing was the more dishonest of the two chores.

“If I say a number first, I’ll be guessing what someone with my shape of experience is allowed to cost in this building. Your guess is better than that guess. If the rule is that I have to go first anyway, I’ll go. I’d rather you didn’t pretend it’s a preference when it’s a rule.”

Andrea laughed once, not entirely comfortably. “It’s a rule someone wrote so we don’t anchor ourselves out of our own process. The band for this seat, in Toronto, is one twenty to one forty-five. Base. There’s a bonus that is real and not life-changing.”

He nodded. The bottom was near the year he could survive if one firm left. The top was more than anyone had paid him. He did not perform indifference, and he did not reach for the top with his face.

“That’s enough to keep talking,” he said. “There’s a condition on my side. I have three firms on a system I can’t hand to a cousin. I would need a named afternoon, or some evenings that are actually evenings, until they’re either quiet or I’ve moved them onto a person I trust. If this job eats the nights because the pager is unofficially constant, then the band is for a different job than the one I can do without lying to Adeola. I haven’t told her I’m here. I won’t use her flexibility as a thing I’ve already negotiated.”

“Moonlighting makes Legal tired.”

“Then Legal should say so before anyone falls in love with a loop.” He regretted *in love* the instant it was out. It was her phrase from a kind of meeting he had not attended, and it sounded like he was mocking a process he was sitting inside. “I’m sorry. That was your problem’s slang, not mine. I mean: I’d rather be disqualified in this room than discovered as a conflict after Sylvie spends the hour.”

Andrea typed more this time. The typing was ordinary and a little hard. “You’re easy to write down and difficult to score,” she said, almost to the screen. Then, to him, because she was not a careless person: “That isn’t a verdict. I have to hand Paul a comparable note. You don’t answer in comparable units. Some of that is good. Some of it will read as if you think the units are beneath you.”

“I don’t,” he said. He was immediate, because hesitation would have sounded like the thing she had just named. “I think the units fit a kind of work I haven’t done. I can learn the units. I can’t answer ‘superpower’ as if I have one without becoming someone Adeola would not recognize. You’d be hiring the person in this chair, and then she would meet the other one.”

Andrea closed the laptop halfway. “Last structured thing, then I’ll let you go to her. Five years. Where are you?”

He hated the question in the simple way that he hated being asked to describe weather he had not stood in. The hate showed, if at all, as a brief glance toward the window, where Richmond was ordinary and bright.

“In a system I don’t have to narrate in order for it to be true,” he said. “With at least one person who can tell me when a lock doesn’t cover the thing I think it covers. If that’s Harton, it’s because the renewals book is the work and not a tour of the work. I don’t know your building well enough to claim more than that.”

“So you might not want us.”

“I might not. You might not want me. Those can both stay possible until someone has seen the book with me.”

She stood, and the interview stood with her, a little unfinished on purpose. At the door she said, “Sylvie runs long when she cares and short when she doesn’t. Don’t fill her silences. She uses them.” Then, after a half-step, “And thank you for the degree. I’d rather have the awkward version.”

“So would I,” he said, and was not entirely sure it was true. He had wanted, that morning, the version in which the sentence sent itself.

In the hallway he asked Martin, who had reappeared with a stack of envelopes, where the washroom was. Cold water, the mirror, his own face looking slightly more awake than he felt. He did not give himself a talk. He dried his hands and came out and nearly walked into Eli.

Eli had a lanyard that still looked issued rather than worn, and a shirt with a crease from the package. He was smiling with a caution that belonged to corridors.

“You came,” Eli said.

“You sent a letter.”

“I toned you down.”

“You said.” Wale looked at him properly, not as a node in the afternoon. The caution was real, and under it a pride Eli was trying not to spend in public. “You look busy in the way you wanted. Is that true, or is it the lanyard?”

“It’s half true,” Eli said. The smile became a person’s. “They let me sit in the room when a college argues about a date. They don’t let me say the date is the wrong kind. I say it anyway and then I apologize for the tone and not the content. Gwen likes the apology more than the content.”

“Gwen liked you enough to hire you.”

“Gwen liked me enough to hire me into implementation.” Eli lowered his voice, not quite a whisper, a private setting. “I didn’t tell Sylvie that Adeola was going to walk. I didn’t tell her you snapped at Adeola about the logo. I told her about the screen. If they ask you for the noble version, don’t build it. I’ll look like an idiot, and also I’ll look like I didn’t trust her.”

“I wasn’t going to build it,” Wale said. “The snap was real. You can tell her if you want. It isn’t a secret I’m using.”

Eli shook his head. “It makes me the audience. I’d rather she know I entered a bad date than know I watched a quarrel.”

“You entered a date the screen asked for.”

“And I entered it.” Eli’s mouth went sideways, the old shame, smaller now. “You already did this part. Don’t do it in a hallway to be kind.”

Wale stopped. The kindness had been real and also a little easy, a thing he knew how to say. Eli had heard the ease. “All right. You entered it. The screen helped. I was the one who shipped the label. That’s the order.”

“Thank you,” Eli said, and meant the order, not the comfort. A door opened somewhere and his posture remembered the building. “She’s in 3C. She doesn’t do the speech. If you don’t know, say you don’t know. She remembers people who covered.”

“Andrea said not to fill the silences.”

“Andrea would have timed them.” Eli touched the lanyard as if it itched, caught himself, and dropped his hand. “I’m glad you didn’t wear a different voice in there. I could hear you through the glass for a second. You sounded like the pharmacy office. That’s the only reason this isn’t a mistake I made at you.”

He went toward 3C with that sentence in his ear. It warmed him more than Andrea’s band, and it made him wary of being warmed. Eli’s stake was not only friendship. Eli had put a judgment into a building where he was still new, and the judgment was now walking down a corridor with a corrected sticker on its coat. If the hour went badly, Eli would feel it in rooms Wale would not be invited back to. He held that without knowing what to do with it except not to perform a rescue.

Sylvie Hartmann was already in 3C, which was smaller and warmer and had a whiteboard no one had cleaned. A diagram of boxes and arrows occupied the left half. The right half had been wiped badly, a grey cloud. She was standing at the board with a marker she had not uncapped, reading the boxes as if they had been left by a person she intended to argue with. She was older than him by a decade and a little more, with her hair in a clip that was losing, and a watch she looked at when she turned.

“Wale,” she said, correctly, no performance of having practiced. “I’m Sylvie. Sit if you like. Or don’t, if the board is the point. I have forty minutes before I have to tell a dentist why Sunday is not a personality.”

“The college Paul is in.”

“The college Paul is in.” She capped nothing; the marker was still capped. “Andrea has the process. I have a question I couldn’t settle from your note. You can refuse parts of it. Don’t recite Tower at me. I can read.”

He took the chair that left the board visible and put the notebook he had not needed with Andrea on the table. She noticed the notebook and did not comment. She uncapped the marker at last and wrote, in a plain hand, *window*.

“What is it?” she said. “Not the column. The thing you mean.”

“A stretch of dates in which a fact is still allowed to become an application,” he said. “It has a start that is earned by something that happened, and an end that is earned by a rule, not by our nerves. If we haven’t earned the start, we don’t have a window. We have a wish with a timestamp.”

“And the job.”

“The job wakes up and asks whether the wish has started being a window, and whether the window is about to stop, and whether a person has already been told. If a person has been told for this window, it does not become a second person being told because we published a comma fix in the rule.”

“Your first email put the version in the key.”

“Yes.”

“Your second took it out.”

“Yes.”

She waited. He understood, a beat late, that the wait was the question.

“I wanted the key to be finished,” he said. “I had the idea of uniqueness in my hand and I put every noun I could see into it. Version felt like rigor. It was a way to mint a new event every time we learned something. That’s not rigor. That’s amnesia with extra rows. I saw it on the Saturday morning because I tried to explain the key to Samira in my head and I got to the part where she would ask why a family had two warnings for one deadline. I didn’t have an answer that wasn’t embarrassing. So I wrote to you before I could talk myself back into the first version.”

“You do that a lot,” she said. “The second letter.”

“I do it when the first one has already gone.”

“You could wait.”

“I could.” He felt a small flare of defensiveness, physical, in the chest, and he did not dress it as a principle. “I also send too fast when I want the other person to see that I know the shape. You asked a precise question. I wanted to be the kind of person who had it. That part wasn’t about the key.”

Sylvie nodded once. The nod was not forgiveness. It was inventory. “Draw the decision and the notice as different things. Eli’s colleges collapse them, and then they can’t understand why a bounce retries the moral status of a dentist.”

He hesitated at her notebook, which lay closed by the marker.

“Use it,” she said. “I don’t sanctify paper.”

He drew two boxes. His handwriting went quick when the thing was clear, less careful than the handwriting he used for Adeola’s margins. “The decision is: this window, this version we believed at the time, this outcome. One. The notice is an attempt to put that outcome somewhere — the caseworker, the client, a mailbox that might be dead. Attempts can repeat. The decision shouldn’t multiply because a mailbox bounced.” He tapped the second box. “If you unique on the address, you glue the outcome to one inbox. You can’t tell the caseworker something different from what you told the client, and you can’t retry a bounce without looking like a second judgment.”

“I said matter and email to Andrea when I briefed her,” Sylvie said. “She may still think that’s what you do.”

“It isn’t.”

“Good.” She did not apologize for the misstatement. She corrected the diagram in her head, visibly, a small shift of the mouth. “Show me the skip. When the job wakes and decides not to work.”

He wrote the key as he had corrected it: tenant, matter, the start of the window. He wrote the version beside the row, outside the parentheses. He was about to say that a change in the facts reopened the decision, and he stopped, because the sentence in his mouth was smoother than the code.

“If the facts hash changes, we recompute,” he said. “The hash is the facts, not the rule. The version is written on the decision so a person can see what we believed. It is not, today, part of what the job compares.”

Sylvie looked at the parentheses for a long moment. He did not fill it. He watched the paper, which gave her the silence without staring her into hurrying. Andrea had been right about the silences, and he was glad, in a narrow way, that he had been told. He would have been tempted to add a clause.

“All right,” Sylvie said. “Hold that. Our book is uglier and larger. A registrant is active, conditional, expired, in reinstatement, suspended, or dead. Dead is not a joke, though we had a man who paid his fee and died in the same week, and the registrar wanted him pending because the money had cleared. I laughed at my desk and then I called the registrar back and did not laugh. The fee had cleared. The state machine had no death that was not an override sitting on top of a renewal.”

He laughed, short, before he had decided to. It left. “The family.”

“Yes.”

“So death is a fact, and the pending state was a loyalty to the payment.”

“Loyalty is a kind word.” She said it dry, not cruel. “The registrar did not want to refund a dead man in a quarter when the council was already angry about refunds. I understood the motive. Understanding it didn’t give us a state. We overrode. The override is a nullable column on the registrant. It has been a nullable column for two years, because a director wanted a weekend to go green and I shipped the column instead of the argument.”

She said it levelly. The levelness was not indifference. He could hear the two years in it. He did not rush to agree and he did not arrange his face into sympathy, which would have been a way of standing above her error. He winced anyway, small, at the column, as at a sound.

“When the column is set,” he said, “what does the next job do?”

“It reads the column and stops thinking.”

“And when someone clears it by accident.”

“The next job thinks again, with whatever rule is current, and the registrar calls us and says the system punished a compassionate decision. They keep a spreadsheet so the compassion can be retyped. I am aware that the spreadsheet is the system. Don’t say that in the debrief as if you found it under a cushion. We know.”

The prickle was instant. He felt his answer come up hot, the one that began with *I wasn’t auditioning.* He held it one second, long enough to hear that she was protecting a bruise and not scoring him.

“I wasn’t going to say it in the debrief,” he said. “It’s not a discovery. It’s the situation.”

She studied him. The study was open, not a mask of study. “What did you do, when a person had to contradict the calculation?”

“An event,” he said. “A reason, a person, an expiry. Not a silent edit of the eligibility. If you edit the row, the next run feels spiteful. It ‘fixes’ the kindness and the caseworker stops trusting the screen, and then you get the spreadsheet anyway, only later and angrier. The expiry matters because a contradiction without an end becomes a second, worse rule that nobody published.”

“And if they need it to last.”

“Then they renew the contradiction on purpose. That’s unpleasant. It’s visible. I would rather they be annoyed at a renewal than surprised by a resurrection.”

Sylvie turned the marker in her fingers. For the first time she looked interested in a way that changed her posture. She sat on the edge of the table, which brought her down to the paper, and the staff-engineer distance of the first minutes went out of the room without a ceremony.

“Paul thinks expiry is a tax on registrars who are already tired,” she said. “He may be right about the tiredness. He is wrong about the tax. The tax is being paid now, in a spreadsheet, by a clerk who was not hired to be a second database.” She stopped. “I shouldn’t hand you Paul. He’s not here to answer.”

“You didn’t hand me a villain,” Wale said. “You handed me a disagreement you haven’t finished. I don’t need him to be foolish for the column to be a bad place to hide a fact.”

“It is a bad place.” She said it plainly. “I shipped it. Remember that when you feel the urge to narrate this hour as you enlightening a building.”

“I don’t feel that urge,” he said, and then, because the sentence was too clean, “I feel the urge to say the column is bad, which is cheap when I don’t have to migrate it. You’ve had two years of the colleges living on the exceptions. A correct model that surfaces every quiet exception in a council year is not free just because it’s correct.”

Something in her face changed. Not gratitude. Recognition, wary and a little relieved, as if he had put down a tool she had expected him to swing.

“Eli didn’t brief you on that.”

“No. You did, just now.”

“Most people, when I tell that story, tell me the spreadsheet is the system.”

“You told me not to.”

“People don’t always hear that as information.”

He shrugged, one shoulder, not a performance of modesty. “It was information.”

She let the silence sit again. In it he became aware of the whiteboard behind her, the old boxes, one of which said *date entered* and one of which said *date met*, with an arrow that had been redrawn so many times it had cut a groove in the ink. He looked at the groove.

“That’s the cousin,” he said. “Eli’s. The day someone typed, and the day the condition became true.”

“The day someone typed is seductive,” Sylvie said. “It’s in the row. It doesn’t require a clerk to decide what ‘met’ means when half the evidence is a scanned course credit and a letter from a supervisor who uses the word *completed* for three different events. Engineering would like ‘met’ to be a feeling we can reject. Implementation would like ‘entered’ to be sacred because it reconciles to the import. Both are hiding.”

“From the definition.”

“From owning one.” She pointed the marker at him, not as a weapon, as a baton. “You owned one for ready-to-file, after you didn’t. What did it cost the firm, besides Eli’s pride?”

“An afternoon. Two files reopened, one file that was actually late and had been disguised by the same bug. Adeola’s trust, which came back narrower. She trusts me to come. She does not trust the screen to be obvious. That’s fair. I also lost a week of the other work, and I was unpleasant to her for one sentence in the middle. She collected it.”

“What did you say?”

He had told Eli he would not build the noble version. Here was the bill. “I told her to stop reading me the logo and let me see the row. She was reading me the consequences, not the logo. I heard the part that sounded like a speech because I was behind and embarrassed. I apologized for the shape of it. I did not apologize for wanting the row. She accepted exactly that division and has repeated it back to me once since, when I was about to be flowery about a downtime. It worked. It was not pleasant.”

Sylvie looked, for a second, like a person who had been given a tool she respected and did not intend to praise. “Good. Now be wrong about something you haven’t already apologized for.”

“That’s a strange request.”

“It’s a practical one. I can smell a prepared failure at twenty paces. Take the publish path. A rule is corrected while your job is in the middle of a matter. What do you guarantee?”

He went to the board, because the paper had become too small, and drew the matter lock as a bracket. He talked as he drew, faster than he had talked to Andrea, the social watchfulness dropping because the problem had weight. “The job takes the matter row. It reads the current version. It evaluates. It writes the decision if this window doesn’t have one, or if the facts hash moved. The publish inserts a version and swings the pointer. It doesn’t take the matter lock. So—”

He saw it as he said *so*. The bracket on the board protected the matter from a second job. It did not protect the matter from a pointer moving on another table. He had known this in the abstract, the way he knew rain existed, and he had not put it against his own parentheses until the marker was in his hand. The knowledge arrived with a drop in his stomach that was embarrassingly physical.

He finished the sentence anyway, which was the mistake. “In practice the publish happens when someone is at a desk, not in the middle of the run. The window is short.”

Sylvie did not smile. She did not pounce. She looked at the bracket. “Night is not a consistency strategy. You don’t strike me as someone who accepts it from a client.”

“I don’t.” The defensive sentence was still in the air, his own, and he hated the sound of it more than he hated being caught. Heat came up into his face. He put the marker down so he would not keep drawing decorations around a hole. “I heard it. I was about to spend the next minute making the race small because I had just drawn the lock with confidence. The lock is real. It is not the lock for this. If the job has read version four, and the publish commits version five, and the job writes a decision that cites four, the unique key will not save me. It’s the same window. I stored the version as a label. I compare the facts. A rule change doesn’t look like a fact change. The decision sticks under a rule that is no longer current.”

“Yes.”

“I argued one step past the place I knew.” He rubbed a thumb against the side of his finger, once, and stopped. “You didn’t even have to say the sequence. I said ‘in practice’ because ‘it doesn’t handle this’ was sitting there and I didn’t want it.”

“I was going to say the sequence,” Sylvie said. “You saved me a drawing. Don’t turn the saving into a performance either. You were wrong, and then you were quick, and the quickness included a dishonest sentence. The recovery was good. It does not delete the sentence.”

“No.”

He stood with the unpleasantness of it, not converting it into a lesson he could hand back. The whiteboard marker smell was sweet and close. From the hall came a laugh that had nothing to do with them. He looked at the bracket he had drawn, which was still a correct bracket for a smaller claim, and he felt the urge to add the true fix immediately, to be the man who left the room with the race already designed. The urge was strong. It was cousin to the first email.

“I don’t want to invent the repair in the same minute,” he said. “The honest shape is: the rule version has to participate in whether the decision is still valid, without becoming a new window every time a comma changes. A dirty mark on the open windows of that pathway, or a compare against the version that is current at commit, under the same lock as the read. I haven’t earned the choice between those by being embarrassed. If I pick one now I’ll pick the one that sounds cleaner.”

Sylvie was quiet long enough that he wondered if he had over-explained. Then she said, “That is the first time today you’ve sounded like someone I could leave with a migration.”

The praise, if it was praise, was so specific that he did not know where to put his face. He looked at the board. “Because I didn’t pick.”

“Because you noticed you wanted to pick in order to leave clean.” She slid off the table. “People say they like to be corrected. Most of them like the moment after, when the correction has become their personality. You got angry in the middle. Your ears did the thing.” She said it without mockery, a clinical kindness. “Then you stopped arguing. I believe the stop. I don’t need the brand.”

He laughed once, helpless, at the ears, and the laugh embarrassed him further, which at least was simple. “I didn’t know they did a thing.”

“They do. It’s useful. Don’t manage them.”

“I don’t think I could.”

“You’d be surprised what people try to manage after they’re told.” She sat, and the conversation changed temperature again, not back to the first distance, toward something more ordinary. “Andrea will ask me if you can work a system you don’t own. I’m going to tell you what I think, and you can correct the facts if I have them wrong. You like owning the definition. You will suffer in a building where the definition is a committee. You will also suffer if you own it alone, because there is nobody to stop ‘in practice.’ Harton is a committee that occasionally tells the truth. I am not offering you a monastery.”

“I didn’t come for a monastery.”

“You came because Eli said we calculate from the day someone typed.”

“Yes.”

“We do. On reinstatement, for two of the colleges. The third stores a date met and then lets an import overwrite it with date entered if the import file has a value, which it always does, because the export that feeds the import was written by a man who has left and who believed empty fields were rude.” She rubbed her forehead. “I am so tired of his politeness.”

“Is the import the system of record?”

“No. And it behaves as if it is, for that column, which is worse. The record is whatever arrived last.” She looked at him. “You want to say something.”

“I want to ask who is allowed to publish a rule,” he said. “Not as a speech. If a clerk can swing the pointer on a Friday because a council member called, then any fix I describe is a story. If publish is reviewed and rare, the race is still real and the dirty mark is a different size. I don’t know your Fridays.”

“Reviewed and not rare,” she said. “Council members call on Fridays on purpose. Paul can publish. I can publish. A product manager named Owen can publish, and Owen publishes when he has been made afraid. I am telling you that because if you join and you are polite to Owen’s fear, you will reintroduce date entered under a better name.”

“What does he do when he’s afraid?”

“He adds a flag.”

Wale felt the irritation this time as a tightness at the jaw, not at her, at the flag, and at his own easy picture of himself as a person who would of course resist a flag. “I have added a flag,” he said. “When Adeola was afraid of the automatic text, the queue was not a flag. When I was afraid of the illegal transition, I added a check, and before that I had let a model hand me a set of states that included a jump from eligible to lapsed. That one went to production. Samira caught it because the client called, not because we alerted. There was no alert. I talk about the constraint I added afterward more than I talk about the missing alert. I’m trying not to do that here. The order is the missing page, then the constraint.”

Sylvie did not move. “What did the model give you, exactly?”

“A plausible lifecycle,” he said. “The vocabulary of workflows. It looks like thought if you read it fast. I was tired and I wanted the enum to exist so I could write the job. I understood the words. I did not understand that one of the edges couldn’t happen at Banjo Immigration, because a person doesn’t fall out of a window they were never shown. They are inside, or not yet, or late. Lapsed-from-eligible was a story from some other business the model had averaged. I shipped the average. I can read every transition that’s in there now. I could not have said that in March, and the reason I can say it is that I sat with Samira and listed them, not that I became generally wiser about tools.”

“Which parts do you still let it write?”

“Migrations, first draft. Tests around a rule I’ve already stated. The boring arms of a case statement.” He was matter-of-fact, and under the matter-of-fact a thread of shame he did not pad. “If I can’t explain the draft without looking at it, it doesn’t go in. That rule is newer than the incident. I don’t always like how slow it makes the evening.”

“Good. I’m not interested in purity. I’m interested in whether you can tell the difference between a sentence you mean and a sentence that arrived.” She tapped his notebook, still closed. “Fourteen days. Your reactivation note, the one under the incident, the footnote. You suppress a second nudge for fourteen days. Why fourteen?”

He had forgotten the footnote was public. The heat returned, different from the ears, lower and duller, the heat of a number he had hoped would not be picked up.

“Adeola asked for two weeks,” he said. “She said a family that has just been pulled back into motion should not get a second ping while they’re gathering a document. Fourteen was the integer I had. I wrote it down as if it were derived. It isn’t derived. I don’t know if ten would lose a family or if thirty would let one go quiet again. I have not looked. The comment in the code cites her and a date, which makes it look like a decision instead of a habit.”

“Look at it,” Sylvie said. “Not for me. If you come here, you will find a hundred fourteens. The work is knowing which ones are load-bearing superstition. If you defend them because a customer said them in a tired voice, you’ll reproduce Owen. If you rip them out because they embarrass you in an interview, you’ll ship a purity incident.”

“That’s fair.”

“It wasn’t a compliment.”

“I know.” He held her look. “You’re technically ahead of me on this book. The race you didn’t have to draw, the import that overwrites, the council Fridays. I can feel the difference. I don’t experience it as an insult. I experience it as the reason the hour is worth the sticker.” He heard himself and almost smiled at the sticker, which was too small a word, and let it stand because it was true. “If I had been ahead of you, I would be worried I was in the wrong room.”

Sylvie glanced at her watch. She did not apologize for the glance. “I have to go be a person about dentistry. Two things before I do, and you can hate both.”

“All right.”

“First. I asked for this hour because of the second email, not because Eli is likable. Eli is likable. That would not have moved me. The second email wasted none of my Saturday except the part where I had to admit an implementation specialist had seen a real defect in our cousin. I have not told him that. You can tell him I was serious. Don’t tell him I was impressed. He’ll spend it, and Gwen will smell it, and his life will get more theatrical.”

“I won’t tell him impressed.”

“Second. I am going to recommend that Paul talk to you even though the degree line is unmet. Andrea texted me during your screen.” Sylvie’s mouth flattened, a private irritation at telephones. “She said you volunteered it, late, and that you didn’t decorate it, and that you asked to be disqualified now rather than carried. She also said you may think our questions are beneath you. I didn’t hear that in here. I heard you refuse a kind of answer. Those are different, and Paul will blur them if he’s tired, and he will be tired. If he blurs them, don’t get lofty. Say the concrete thing again. If you get lofty I will not spend a second meeting pulling you back.”

“What does lofty sound like, so I know the one you mean?”

“It sounds like a small essay about units of comparison.” She said it without meanness. “Andrea wrote that you gave her one. She wasn’t wrong to hear it. She was wrong, maybe, about why. You can decide whether the essay was necessary. I’m not your editor.”

He thought of the sentence about units, and of the way it had come out fully formed, and of the pleasure, slight and unrespectable, of having said a true thing cleanly to a person with a rubric. The pleasure was the part he had not named in the room. He named it now, not as an essay, as a correction of the record she was about to carry.

“The essay wasn’t necessary,” he said. “The refusal was. I added the essay because I didn’t want to be scored as difficult without having looked intelligent about it. That’s the part that thinks the units are a game. I don’t want that part hired by accident.”

Sylvie stood very still. Then she laughed, quietly, the laugh of a person who has been handed an inconvenience she prefers to a charm. “All right. I’ll put it in my own words. If I put it in yours, you’ll sound like a novel.”

“Please don’t.”

“I wasn’t going to.” She took her notebook back, glanced at his two boxes, and tore the page out along the perforation, cleanly, and gave it to him. “You’ll want the parentheses more than I will. I have them.” At the door she paused, and the pause was not a silence she was using. It was a person stopping. “The ears, for what it’s worth, happened when you were defending the race, not when I told you about the column. You can stand other people’s bad shipments. You have a harder time being caught wanting to look finished. That’s ordinary. It’s not fatal. It will be fatal if you build a style around confessing it faster than anyone can use it.”

“That last part,” he said, “I will probably do once, in the debrief, if I’m not careful.”

“Then be careful once.” She opened the door. The hall noise came in. “Martin will get your coat if you left it. You left it in Andrea’s room. Go and eat something. You look like you spent the glucose.”

She was gone toward the dentists before he could answer. He stood in 3C with the torn page and the whiteboard bracket and the feeling, not yet sorted, of having been seen at a resolution he had not authorized and did not, strangely, want to argue with. The glucose remark should have annoyed him. It had been accurate. Accuracy from her landed without the small wound it would have made if Andrea had said he looked tired. He noted the difference and did not build a theory. He erased the bracket before he left, because the next person should not inherit his hole, and then he wished he had left it, because erasing it was another way of leaving clean. He was, he thought, going to be tiresome to himself on the street if he kept this up. He stopped. He put the marker in the tray. He went to get his coat.

Martin had it already, folded once over an arm, and the sticker peeled at the corner where the wool had rubbed. “She does that,” Martin said. “The running. You can leave the badge in the bowl. Or keep it, people keep them, we don’t chase them.”

“I’ll leave it.” He dropped the sticker among the mints, which was slightly wrong, and Martin picked it out without comment and put it in a cup that existed for stickers. “Thank you for the reprint.”

“Spelling’s the job,” Martin said, and answered his phone.

On Richmond the light had gone a little golden and the wind had stayed. He walked toward the streetcar with the torn page in the notebook so it would not crease into nonsense. He did not feel triumphant. He felt used, in the decent sense, the way a muscle feels used, and he felt the dishonest sentence still sitting in 3C even though he had erased the drawing. *In practice.* He said it once, under his breath, so it would stay ugly.

Adeola called as he reached the stop. He had meant to call her. She disliked being meant.

“Well?” she said.

“Nobody offers a job in the room.”

“I asked if they were serious.”

“One of them was. Sylvie. She runs the book I’d be working on, if anything happened, which it may not. There’s a degree line. I don’t have it. I said so.”

“And the other?”

“She was doing her job. Her job is to make people comparable.” He watched a cyclist take the curb too tightly and recover. “She was not unserious. She was pointed at a different object.”

Adeola was quiet for a second. He could hear the office, a printer, Samira’s voice far back. “Are you going to become unavailable?”

“I don’t know yet. I haven’t pretended to them that you agreed.”

“Don’t practice the sentence on me,” she said. “When you know, say the thing you will do, not the difficulty of knowing. Samira sent you a date. It is circled twice because the client’s letter and the portal do not match, and I would like a person rather than a philosophy.”

“I saw the message before I went in. I didn’t open it. I didn’t want to answer her from a hallway with the other problem still in my mouth.”

“That is either respect or delay.”

“Yes.”

She made the sound, the one that was not agreement and not dismissal. “Look at it tonight. Not at midnight. At midnight you write sentences you have to retract, and I am tired of being your second letter.”

He smiled at the shelter glass, a real smile she could not see and would not have needed. “Tonight. Early. If the dates disagree because of a label, I’ll say so. If they disagree because the portal is a different fact, I’ll say that and I won’t collapse them to soothe the file.”

“Good.” She hung up.

The streetcar was crowded and warm from other people’s coats. He stood and wrote on the torn page, under his own boxes, in letters small enough that the man beside him could not casually read them: *Publish doesn’t take the matter lock. The decision sticks. The version on the row is a label, not a check. “In practice” was a wish.* He looked at the lines. They were plainer than anything he had said while wanting to be finished. That was why they were worth the stop.

At home the light in the kitchen was the light he knew. The kettle, the chair that rocked slightly on the uneven floor, the laptop asleep where he had left it that morning face down beside the unsent draft. He did not open the draft. He opened Samira’s photograph first, because Adeola had been right about the order of loyalties, and because the circled dates were a thing with edges.

The letter said the course had been completed on the third. The portal, exported into the checklist by Samira’s careful hand, said the ninth. Six days, and the window was not generous. He sat with it. The temptation was to call the portal the truth because it was newer, or the letter the truth because it was on paper. He did neither. He wrote to Samira that both dates were facts about different events until someone said which event the rule used — the day the instructor signed, or the day the portal accepted the upload — and that he would not pick one in order to let the job run. He asked her which date the consultant had promised the client. Then he stopped, and added that if she did not know, the file should be marked not yet evaluable, not nudged. He read the message for the hidden second version of himself, the one that wanted credit for caution. It was only the instruction. He sent it.

The failed job in the queue was not the race. It was a bounce. A caseworker address had a display name with a comma, and the splitter he had written in a hurry months ago had treated the comma as the end of the mailbox. Three notices sat poisoned behind it. He fixed the split, wrote the test the model would have been happy to draft and that he wrote himself because the comma was his, and shipped it from the kitchen table while the kettle heated. The deploy was small and nervous in the ordinary way. When the notices went, he felt the drop in his shoulders that no interview had produced.

Only then did he open the publish function. He read it the way he had stood at Sylvie’s board, without an audience to be quick for. The pointer swung. The matters did not lock. The comment above the function was cheerful and short and mentioned none of this. He did not rewrite it that night. A rewrite in the same evening would have been the clean exit, and he could feel the wish for it in his hands, and he left the hands on the tea instead.

He put the torn page on the counter where the morning would find it without a search. Beside it he wrote, on a fresh card, the thing Sylvie had not asked him to keep and that he did not intend to confess to anyone unless he failed to: *Don’t be careful in the debrief by the method of sounding careful.*

The card looked like a motto, and he disliked mottos. He turned it over. On the back he wrote the concrete substitute, which was uglier and therefore more likely to survive contact with Paul. *If he blurs it, say the queue, the forty minutes, the version column. Don’t explain comparison.*

He left both sides up, which defeated the turn, and stood in the kitchen irritated at his own stationery. From the other room the laptop fan wound down. Samira had not yet replied. Adeola would, if he was late. Harton would take days and might take a template. He thought of the template Andrea had described only by implication, the quiet close of a window, and of how much he hated quiet closes in other people’s systems, and of how he had not asked her, in the end, whether failed screens were told or simply went still. He had meant to. The glucose, or the pride, or the clock. He did not know which, and he did not settle it by force.

He drank the tea while it was still too hot, as he had in the morning, and waited for Samira’s answer, which was a kind of waiting he understood.
