The Interview

He came up the wrong stair. The PATH had been colder than the street, and he had followed a courier because the man walked as if the next turn were obvious, and then the courier shouldered through a door marked for staff and Wale was left where the tile changed colour. He took the wider corridor, came out on King, and stood with the revolving door still turning behind him while the map on his phone told him he had passed the address. Peter Street was one block over. He had given himself twenty minutes to be early and had spent nine of them underground. What remained was enough if he did not try to earn the nine back. At the corner the light was red and his legs argued with him. He waited anyway.

The building had a lunch place on the ground floor and the smell of the fryer in the stairwell. Marlow was on the third floor, behind a door with a small steel plate and no other announcement. He had expected a logo on the glass. There was only the plate, and a line of tape on the carpet where someone had recently pulled a cable. He wiped his palms on his trousers before he opened the door, then wished he had not, because the motion felt like a preparation someone could see.

The woman at the desk was on the phone. She lifted a finger without looking unkind about it, and he stood where he was. The finger was for the person on the phone as much as for him. She said, "If he sends it after noon it won't be in Friday's file," and then, softer, "I know. I'll tell her you called." When she put the handset down she looked at him as if the call had not quite left her face.

"I'm here for Danielle Pereira," he said. "Wale Omotayo."

She drew the keyboard toward her. "One more time."

He said it again, and then spelled the surname, because the first time through a new desk he had learned that saying it slowly was not the same thing as spelling it. She typed, frowned at the screen, and typed again.

"They've got you with Danielle and Helen," she said. "You're a little early. You can sit. Water?"

"Yes. Thank you."

Her name was on a small card taped to the monitor: Ruth Kwong. The tape was yellowing. She got up and went to a kitchen he could hear and not see, and he was left with the room.

There were apples in a bowl, two of them soft at the shoulder. On the wall opposite the desk, a row of printed logos had been mounted on a pale board. He recognized most of them only as the kind of mark a consultancy uses when it wants to look older than it is. The fourth from the left he knew. Pell & Rahman, the P and the R sharing a stem, the ampersand in a gold that had not printed well. He had sat across from Nadia Rahman in a coffee place on Dundas eight months ago while she turned that mark face-up on a folder and asked him what it would cost to have it on a portal that belonged to her firm alone.

She had been precise and a little insulting, though he had not felt the insult as a weapon. "You're one person," she had said, "so this stays simple. We don't need you to invent a product. We need our clients to stop emailing my staff for updates." He had liked her for the clarity. He had said he would not build a separate system. She had asked whether he was too small to do the work. He had answered that he was too small to own two systems, and he had heard, as he said it, that the sentence had a shape she could take home and dislike. She had disliked it. She had paid for both coffees anyway, which was worse than if she had let him pay, and she had said, "When you have a company, you'll understand why that answer only works for you." He had not corrected the word company. On the sidewalk afterward he had wanted to go back in and tell her that Omcoda was the company, and that the answer was the company. He had not gone back. People who returned to improve a sentence were asking to be seen improving it.

Now the mark was on Marlow's wall. He looked at it long enough that Ruth, coming back with the water, glanced at the board to see what he was looking at.

"Friends of yours?" she said.

"No," he said. "I've met them."

She set the water down on a coaster that stuck to the glass. "They're in a lot. Fridays." She did not ask more. He was grateful in a plain way, and sat.

The chair was low. He sat forward in it at first, as if the interview had already begun and posture could be entered into evidence, and then he heard himself doing it and sat back. The notebook he had brought stayed closed on his knee. He had written three questions in it on the subway and already disliked two of them. A man came out from the inner door in a hoodie and white earphones, nodded at Ruth, and went past Wale without looking at him, a laptop under his arm and a banana in the same hand. The banana was very yellow. Wale watched the door settle shut. He did not invent a greeting he had failed to make.

His phone lit. Samir: Good luck. Two words and no punctuation. Wale held the phone until the screen dimmed. He could feel, in the brevity, both the decency and the request buried under the decency. He did not answer. Under that thread, unanswered since morning, was Lena Hart. He had read the first line on the streetcar platform and then locked the phone, because he had not wanted her voice in his head while he looked for the building. The line was still there when he blinked. We're going to need to pause for a bit. He turned the phone face down on the notebook.

He had not told his mother he was coming here. She would have asked whether the place had benefits, and she would have told him to wear the tie he did not own, and she would have been right about the benefits. Not telling her had seemed, at the sink in the morning, like a way of keeping the day his size. Sitting under the logos it felt like a small meanness he had already spent.

Danielle came out at four minutes past the hour. She was younger than her emails had sounded. The emails had been careful. In the doorway she was already speaking, as if they had begun in the corridor.

"Wale, hi, I'm Danielle. Thank you so much for coming in. Helen's going to be a few minutes, she had something blow up, not blow up, just a thing, she'll be here. We can start. Ruth, he's with me."

"I signed him in," Ruth said.

"Perfect."

Danielle's handshake was dry and decided. She walked him down a short hall of rooms named after lakes. Nipissing was full. A man inside was drawing a box on a whiteboard and then another box overlapping it, and saying, "I'm not saying it's a platform." Danielle opened Simcoe. The table was too large for two people. On the glass, the morning from Peter Street looked brighter than it had felt.

"Get in okay?" she said. "The PATH is a nightmare if you don't do it every day."

"I came up on King," he said. "I walked back."

"Oh no." She laughed once, the polite kind. "Well, you made it, that's the part that counts."

He smiled because she had laughed, and because to explain that he had not been lost so much as obedient to a stranger would have asked her to care about a corridor. She was already sitting, already turning a page in a notebook that had his name at the top in handwriting neater than he expected. Under the name she had written Referral — Samir E. / Helen requested. He was not meant to read upside down. He had read it anyway. She saw him see it and turned the page over with no fuss, as if the page had simply been ready.

"So," she said. "I'll do the first part, and Helen will join for the rest. This isn't a quiz. We just want to understand what you've been building and whether the shape of it fits what we're hiring for. Obligations. Product engineer. Helen can say that in her own words when she's here. Sound fair?"

"Yes."

"Great. Then maybe start wherever you start. Tell me a bit about yourself."

He had known the question would come and had still not chosen the first sentence. The one he had rehearsed on the subway — I operate a monitoring system for immigration consultancies — sounded, in this room, like the first line of a brochure. He let it go.

"I've been running a system called Tower for almost two years," he said. "It belongs to Omcoda. We watch eligibility and filing windows for consultancies, and we run the follow-ups when a client goes quiet. I built it. I also answer when it does the wrong thing. Before that I was in school. I'm not finished."

She wrote unfinished and did not hide the word. "Computer science?"

"At TMU. Two years. I paused when the first firm went live. I tell myself I'll go back. I don't have a date."

"Okay," she said, in the tone of someone putting an object in a drawer without yet deciding whether it would be needed. "And Omcoda is your startup."

"It's the company that operates Tower. There are two firms on it. I don't think of it as a startup in the way that word usually means. We don't raise money. We don't take a brief and build something we hand over. If a consultancy wants a thing only they will use, I turn that down."

"Sure," she said. "A startup. Early stage."

He looked at the table. The second time she said it he would have to answer, and he did not want the first correction to be a lecture. She was doing her job. The job had a box, and he had arrived with an object that did not drop cleanly into it. If he made her feel clumsy for the box, the rest of the hour would be about her embarrassment rather than the work.

"How did you get the first customer?" she said.

He had a clean version. A consultancy needed deadline tracking; he had proposed a retained system; they had agreed. The clean version was not false. It was the version with the debt removed.

"My mother goes to a church in Brampton," he said. "A woman there, Mrs. Odunayo, has a small practice. She was running filing dates out of a spreadsheet and a notebook she didn't entirely trust. My mother told her I could look. I looked. She wanted me to keep fixing the sheet. I didn't want to be the person who fixed the sheet forever, and the sheet was already lying. So I built the first version of the monitor and I asked her to use that instead. She was angry with me for about a month. She's still a client."

Danielle's pen hovered. "Angry because you changed the process."

"Angry because the system wouldn't let her mark a file ready when the fee hadn't been recorded. The sheet had let her. She said the sheet understood that sometimes you know a thing before the file does. She was right that she knew things. She was wrong that the file should pretend."

"And you held the line."

The phrase was so ready that he almost agreed to it out of courtesy. "I held that rule. I gave in on other things. She still has a list she keeps beside the screen for the cases she doesn't trust the statuses to describe. I know about the list. I haven't found a way to make the list more true than the screen without letting the screen become the list."

Danielle nodded as if this were a competency and wrote client conflict → held standard. He saw the arrow and felt the slight sickness of being translated, and also the justice of it: he had told the story, and she was not wrong about what she had heard. She was only early.

"Helen Cho asked for this conversation personally," Danielle said. "You came through Samir El-Sayed. How do you know Samir?"

"We met at a meetup. We talked a few times after. We don't work together."

"What did he tell you about the role?"

"He didn't. He sent me a message last week that said Helen had read something I wrote and might want to talk. Before that I didn't know Marlow was hiring. I knew he worked here. I didn't know on which part."

That was true as far as the messages went. It left out the window above the bar on College, and the way Samir had gone quiet when the hole in his retry became visible, and the sentence about users. Wale kept those. He told himself he kept them because they were Samir's to tell. Sitting with his phone face down on the notebook, he knew he also kept them because a man who arrived and undressed his recommender was a man being careful in a way that looked like honesty and functioned as an application.

Danielle asked him what he wanted from the next five years. He said he wanted to be trusted with a system that other people depended on, in a place where more than one person could change it. He heard how much of Omcoda's loneliness was in the sentence. She asked whether he was talking to other companies. He said he was not. She smiled at that, pleased and a little suspicious, and moved to the questions she had printed.

The meetup had been on a Tuesday in July of the year before, in a room over a bar where the air did not move. The talk had been about events as the answer to every question the speaker had not been asked. Wale had stayed because leaving in the middle would have meant climbing over a woman who had fallen asleep with her eyes open. Samir had stayed because he had come with a colleague who had left at the first pause. They ended up at the same open window, grateful for the alley air, which smelled of garbage and was still better.

Samir had been at Marlow three months. He talked about it the way people talk about a room they have just been given a key to and have not yet been scolded in. He was on the reminders, he said. The old job had been failing silently. He had made it retry. Wale had asked what happened if it ran twice. Samir had said the email provider deduplicated. Wale had asked on what key. Samir had said he was fairly sure it was the recipient and something in the body, inside a window of time. Wale had said that if the date changed, or if a caseworker's name was added at the bottom, the provider would not call it the same email. Samir had looked at the alley.

"We're not a consultancy," Samir had said. "We have users. A lot of them. You can't sit in the worker and hold its hand."

"I have two firms," Wale had said. It was the first time he had given the number to someone who had not already signed a cheque. The number sat on the windowsill between them and got smaller in the open air. He had not added a plan or a market to it. Samir had nodded as if the nod were a kindness, and then he had said he should find his colleague, though his colleague was gone. They had exchanged the sort of follow that is not a promise. After that they had eaten once, near Samir's office, and Samir had spent the meal describing a manager without saying the manager's name. Wale had listened. He had not offered the retry again. When the meal was over Samir had seemed both relieved and faintly disappointed, as if he had wanted the argument and also wanted to survive it.

Wale had not known, then, that the retry Samir described would send a second notice to a man who had already filed. He knew only what Samir had written last week, on the steps outside the apartment, where the message had been waiting after a deploy: she asked if I knew anyone who had actually handled the double send. I sent her your note. I said you run it for real.

He had not said two. He had not said that the note's author had described the failure to him in an alley before either of them had earned it. Reading the message on the steps outside his apartment, Wale had seen the omission and had felt, first, the small warmth of being spoken for, and then the calculation inside the warmth. Samir was in trouble or near it. A note from outside turned a private mistake into a class of mistake. Wale did not think Samir had lied. He thought Samir had chosen a true sentence that would survive being read by Helen. People did that. He did that. The note itself was that.

Danielle was asking him about compensation.

"We band this role from one twenty-five to one forty-five, base, depending on how we level you," she said. "Equity is a separate conversation if we get there. I don't need a number today. I need to know we're not wasting each other's time."

He did not have a number that was honest in the way she meant. What he paid himself was not a salary. It was what remained after the server, the error tracker, the accountant he had seen twice, and the months Mrs. Odunayo paid on the twentieth because the twentieth was when her own clients paid her. Lena Hart's invoice was six weeks old. The email under his phone was not going to make it younger. The bottom of Danielle's band was more than Omcoda had paid him in the last year. The fact arrived in his face before he could decide what to do with it. He felt the heat and knew it could be read as pride, or as disappointment, or as a man doing arithmetic he should have done at home.

"The band isn't a waste of time," he said. "I don't have a cleaner answer today."

She wrote a small mark he could not read. Her mouth made the shape recruiters' mouths made when a candidate refused the gift of a scripted expectation. She was about to ask again, in different words, when the door opened.

Helen Cho came in with a bicycle helmet in one hand and a laptop under the arm. She was older than Samir by more than a decade, and she did not arrange her face into an apology before she spoke. She set the helmet on the credenza, where it ticked once against the wood, and sat down in the chair that had been empty.

"The reminders queue backed up," she said. "I wanted to see it drain before I left it. I'm Helen. You're Wale. I told Danielle a few minutes. It has been more like twenty."

"What was the lag?" he said.

Danielle's pen stopped. Helen looked at him, not offended and not pleased, only a little rearranged.

"Twenty-two minutes at the worst of it," she said. "We're back under five. It wasn't a failure. It was a pile-up behind a firm that uploaded a few thousand contact updates at nine. The worker is fine. The worker is also the same process as the web, which is why I don't like leaving the room while it's catching up."

"You restart them together," he said.

"We restart one thing. Yes." She opened the laptop and did not look at it. "Danielle, how far did you get?"

"Background, level, a first pass on comp," Danielle said. "I was going to do the behavioural set before you came in."

"Do you still want to?"

"I want some of it." Danielle said this with a pleasantness that had a nail in it. She turned a page. "Wale, tell me about a time you had to influence someone when you didn't have authority over them."

He could have used Mrs. Odunayo. He could have used Nadia and called the refusal an influence, which would have been a lie about what refusal was. He thought of Ifeoma, who had not been influenced. She had been informed, and then she had had to call her own client and say the firm had sent a stupid email, and his part had been to make the stupidity stop spreading before noon.

"A caseworker at one of the firms called me because a client had received a second notice to file, after she had already filed," he said. "The caseworker didn't need a story about retries. She needed the rest of that morning's notices not to go out until I knew which of them were lies. I turned the schedule off. I checked the ones still waiting. Two had already gone. I called her back and told her about those two before she found them herself. She was quieter about the two than she had been about the one. After that she used the check I added. I didn't persuade her of a design. I just got back to her with the bad news while it was still mine."

Danielle waited for the rest, for the lesson. He didn't have a lesson that would fit in the blank she was holding open. Helen was watching the helmet on the credenza as if it might tick again.

"What did she say, the second time?" Helen said.

"She said, 'All right. Send me the names.' Then she hung up. She didn't thank me. The thank-you came two days later and it was one line, and it was about the names being right, not about the system."

"What was her name?" Danielle said, pen ready, as if a name made a story auditable.

He had already decided, an hour ago, that he would not bring Ifeoma's name into a room where it could become an anecdote. Then Helen had asked what the woman said, and the woman without a name had felt like a prop, and he had nearly said it. He was glad Danielle had asked in a way he could refuse.

"I'd rather not," he said. "She isn't part of your process. The names she asked me for were her clients."

"Of course," Danielle said. She was embarrassed, and she covered it by writing something longer than the moment required. Wale looked at the grain of the table. He had learned, from being the person who had misspoken, that a face needs somewhere else to be for a second. When he looked up she had recovered her page.

Helen had not moved to help her. She had, he thought, noticed the looking away, and had put it somewhere.

"I read the note you sent Samir," Helen said. "The one with the notified_at column. I also read the migration file and the test file you sent after. Danielle hasn't. If I misstate something, fix it. I don't want the version I've been repeating in my head for three weeks."

So Samir had forwarded the files. Wale had assumed as much and had still, sending them, included a line he now disliked: The second test is the one that failed before the fix. Helpful, and also a small bow.

"All right," he said.

"You used a unique index on case and pathway," Helen said. "The job inserts a row, the insert is the right to send, the send happens, you mark notified_at. If the worker dies after the insert and before the send, the retry finds the row and finishes. If it dies after the send and before the mark, the unique index stops the second insert. That's the shape."

He was quiet long enough that Danielle glanced at Helen to see if the question had been unclear.

"The index isn't case and pathway," he said. "It's case, window, and channel. A pathway has more than one window. A window can be an email and also a task in the caseworker's list. If I had keyed it the way you just said, the second channel would never send, and the next window wouldn't either. The first version of the table was too wide. I narrowed it after I saw what it suppressed."

Helen nodded before he had finished the last sentence. The nod was ready. He heard the readiness and felt his jaw tighten around a question he did not ask. She had not misremembered. She had put the wide key on the table to see whether he would agree with it because it had come from her. Asking her if that was what she had done would have been accurate, and it would have turned the correction into a small victory, and he did not need the victory. He went on.

"The insert and the state change can share a transaction when the state change is ours," he said. "The email can't. The provider is outside. So the hole you described is real. The index covers a second insert. It does not cover a world where we handed the provider a message and then lost the fact of having handed it over. I talk about it sometimes as if the index closed the story. It closed the part I could reach with a constraint."

"Did you have a duplicate after the index?" Helen said.

"Not that I've seen. I haven't proved I can't. We don't send enough mail for silence to be evidence."

She sat with that. He had the feeling, unfamiliar in interviews because he had not been in any, that she was relieved by a limit. Danielle wrote proves? and circled it, a private argument.

"In the note," Helen said, "the index is already there when the worker dies. You wrote it as if you had built the net and then fallen into it."

Here it was. He had known on the subway that if she had read closely she would arrive at the order of events. He had hoped she would arrive at it so he would be forced, and he had also hoped she would not, so he could remain the person who had written the calm paragraph. Both hopes had fit in him without touching.

"The duplicate happened first," he said. "Two emails, same client, same window. I turned the schedule off, did the morning I've just described, and then I sat in the kitchen and wrote an index. That index was the wide one. Case and pathway. The key you gave me earlier. It would have stopped the duplicate I had just sent. A week later a task never appeared, because the email row was already sitting on the case and the pathway, and the task was a second channel. I narrowed it then. Case, window, channel. The note came after the narrow one had been in production for a few days, and it was written as if that narrow key had been waiting for the failure. It hadn't. The note skips the week. It flatters the order."

Helen looked at the laptop she was not using. The key she had set down as a small unfairness had been his actual first migration. She had meant to see if he would flinch. She had recited him instead. She did not say so. To say so now would have been a confession that the question was a test, and she was not ready to hand him that, and she was also, obscurely, embarrassed that her test had so little invention in it.

The room was quiet. Someone laughed in Nipissing and the laugh died at the glass. Wale put his hands on the notebook and took them off. He wanted a sentence that would make the confession look like thoroughness. He did not take one.

"I thought the calm was a kind of vanity," Helen said. "When I read it. People write that calmly when they weren't the one on the phone. Now I know the calm was also a rearrangement. I'm glad you said it. I would have liked you less if I had found it in the file and you had talked around it."

"I wrote it to be useful," he said. "I also wrote it to be forwarded. I knew that fully this morning. I think I knew it when I sent it to Samir and pretended I was only documenting a job."

Helen almost smiled. It did not quite happen. "It was forwarded."

"Yes."

Danielle looked from one to the other as if a protocol had been violated in a dialect she understood the tone of and not the content. "Just so I'm tracking," she said, "the write-up isn't accurate, and the production system is."

"The write-up is accurate about the mechanism and dishonest about the sequence," Wale said. "The system does what the second version of the note should have said."

"Okay." She underlined something. The underline was heavier than the earlier marks.

Helen asked him to walk through a transition that was illegal. He started well. A file could not move from withdrawn to approved. A file could not become ready while a fee line was open. He said the database rejected these, and he was halfway through the comfort of that claim when he heard it. Helen had not interrupted. His own mouth had.

"I'm describing it as stricter than it is," he said.

She waited.

"There is a check on the status column. It only knows which words are allowed. It does not know which order is allowed. Withdrawn and approved are both legal words. The illegal move between them is rejected in the application, in the path the caseworkers use, and in the tests. There is an admin path I use when I'm fixing a bad row. That path can write the legal word in an illegal order if I'm careless. I closed the hole the model left in the caseworker path. I talk as if I closed it in the database. I didn't. I don't actually know a clean way to put the whole transition table into a constraint without making every change a migration I'm afraid of. So the invariant lives in tests and in a function I have to remember to call. Forgetting is still possible. I forget more carefully than I used to."

"What did the model get wrong?" Helen said.

He was briefly unsure she had heard him, because he had not yet mentioned a model. Then he understood she had assumed one, and that the assumption was fair.

"I used a model to draft the first transition table," he said. "I can write one. It is slow in a way I don't trust, because I start to like a symmetry. The draft was symmetrical. It allowed a withdrawn file to come back as approved if you called a particular update. It looked like a courtesy. A person changing their mind. Mrs. Odunayo's office caught it, not as a theory. A file moved. The fee wasn't there. The status was ready anyway, through a screen I had thought was safe. The draft had been confident. I had been tired enough to treat confidence as a review."

"What do you still give it?" Helen said.

"Boilerplate. A first pass on a migration, which I then read line by line, because I can be talked into liking an ALTER TABLE if I didn't have to type it. Explanations of error messages from the provider. I don't let it near the decision about what must never be true. When I have, the work has come back sounding like a policy and behaving like a hole."

Danielle had leaned in despite herself. "There's a question I'm supposed to ask later," she said. "I'll ask it now, because we're in it. How should we think about the work if a tool produced a lot of it? I'm not accusing you. I ask everyone something like this now. The answers are usually bad."

"Then I may also answer it badly," he said. "The tool produced a table I would have been ashamed to ship and a quantity of typing I was glad not to do. It did not operate the system on the morning the second emails went out. It did not decide to call her back about the two. If you hire me and you want the distinction, look at what I can change without the tool open. The transition function I can still write on a whiteboard. The provider's exact retry header I would look up. Those are different kinds of not-knowing. I try not to dress the second one as the first."

She held his eye for a moment. The distinction landed on the script in her own notebook, the prompts she asked cleanly and could not have derived if the page were taken away. He had not been describing her. She heard herself in it anyway, and the hearing made her sharper. He saw the sharpness arrive and did not step back from the answer to soothe it.

"You paused a degree," she said. "If you came here, would you be gone in eight months when you decided to be a student again, or when the company needed you full time?"

"The company needs me full time now," he said. "That's part of why an employed job is a real question and not a hobby. I don't know what I would do with Tower if I worked here. I've been avoiding the sentence. If I tell you I'll shut it down, I would be saying it because it helps this hour. I won't do that. Mrs. Odunayo's windows don't pause because I got a badge. The other firm wrote to me this morning. They may pause. I haven't answered."

He had not planned to say the morning's email. It had come up on the back of the refusal to perform a shutdown. Danielle's face changed in a way she would have called neutral. He could see her storing flight risk beside unfinished. Helen's face did something else. She looked, for the first time, slightly angry with him, and he did not yet know for what.

"Don't tell me their names," Helen said. "Tell me whether you can be in a codebase you don't own, on a morning when your own queue is also on fire."

"I don't know," he said. "I know I can be in mine when it is. I know I get narrow when it is. Narrow is useful for an hour and bad for a colleague. I haven't had the colleague."

Helen nodded once. The anger stayed.

She asked about isolation. He told her the truth he had, which was that he had chosen read committed and a unique index over a serializable transaction he did not trust himself to operate, and that he could not explain predicate locks to her without pretending. She explained a case her last company had hit, in payments, where two transactions had each failed to see the other's insert and had both believed they were first.

"If the index rejects the second insert," he said, "and the code around it retries the whole operation, do you retry the charge, or only the row that records the charge? I can imagine a week where the index is doing its job and the customer is still billed twice, because the retry is faithful to the wrong unit."

"That was the week," Helen said. "We retried the charge. A man in Halifax sent us both receipts and a sentence I still remember about incompetence. We changed the retry to the record after that, not before. I didn't see it. The person on the rotation did, and she had to convince me the index was not the hero of the story."

He nodded. The question, which had felt like thinking, had been her aftermath. He did not tell her he would have caught it. He had not caught the sibling of it in his own kitchen.

"We tried an outbox," she said. "For the reminders. The dispatcher and a button labelled resend both called the provider. We deduplicated on the outbox id, and the button minted a new id, because a person who presses resend means a new thing. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they pressed it because the page was slow and the first send was in flight. We mailed twice and the id was innocent."

"Did you put a hash of the rendered body on the row?" he said.

"We talked about it. The body includes the date in words. 'Friday' is not stable. Samir had a version that stripped the date. It also stripped a sentence the caseworker had been allowed to add. That's a hard thing to ask them."

"Yes," Helen said.

"Samir speaks highly of you," she said.

"He was kind to pass the note on," Wale said. It was true, and it was also the least he could say without making the hallway the subject. Helen heard the boundary. He saw her hear it. They talked about the cron. He had assumed, from the way Samir used to talk, that Marlow had a worker process and a queue and the ordinary dignity of those words. "Because the last time we split them, a deploy restarted the web and not the worker, and nobody owned the file that was supposed to keep the worker alive. We lost a morning. People mailed us from the firms asking if we had closed. After that the cron felt like humility. It is also a way of not deciding who gets paged."

He touched the edge of the notebook and did not open it. The sentence he was about to say would not help him. It was already in his mouth, and it was true, and hiding a true sentence in an interview was its own performance.

"The cron is a workaround for an ownership problem," he said.

Danielle looked up. Helen's jaw moved once. The anger he had seen earlier found its object, or found a second one.

"Yes," she said.

The quiet that followed was hers to end. He did not decorate the sentence with a softer one. He also did not add the example of his own double send in order to stand beside her quickly, while the standing beside would still purchase forgiveness. He waited. The waiting was hard. His shirt was damp at the back where it met the chair.

"I was hoping to hire someone who would find that embarrassing," Helen said.

"It is embarrassing," he said. "It's also ordinary. The same shape is how I sent two emails. I owned the file, and I still mailed the wrong morning."

"A client goes quiet in the system when we haven't had an inbound message," he said. "A lot of the real contact is a phone call. The caseworker is supposed to log it. They don't, when the day is bad. Then the reactivation job writes to a person who was on the phone yesterday, and we look inattentive, which is the thing the firm cannot afford to look. I added a button. The button is honest. The button requires a human to do a small chore in the minute after they hang up, which is the minute they use to do the next thing they were late for. I have reports. I have a note. I do not have a design that makes the truth cheaper than forgetting. One of the firms has mostly stopped trusting the quiet-client list. They export and they feel, which is the old sheet in a new coat."

"Did you change the inference?"

"We added a sentence to the screen explaining the inference. The sentence is a confession we ask the user to forgive. So no."

He nodded. He could feel the wish to propose a thing, a cheaper chore, a different event. The wish was his vanity of being useful on contact. He held it. She had not asked for a proposal. She had been telling him the truth about her house.

"What do you do when you notice you've talked someone into forgiving a confession?" he said.

"Usually I ship the sentence anyway," she said, "and I stay irritable about it, and I wait until a customer repeats it back to me as if it were their requirement. Then I have permission. It's a bad method. It depends on someone outside being ruder than I am willing to be in the room where we decide."

I added a note field that could be pinned to the top of the file. The note can say anything. It does not change the status. She uses it as a status. I pretend the difference is the design. Some days it is. Some days the pinned note says promising and I have simply moved the lie to a place my tests don't see."

"Did the cousin's case come out all right?" Danielle said.

The question was human. It was also a way back into stories with endings. He did not know the cousin. He had not asked. That omission had bothered him at the time for one evening and then the evening had been replaced by a failed job, and he had not returned to it.

"I don't know," he said. "I didn't ask. I asked whether the note had stopped the scene in the office. She said the scene stopped because the cousin went home. So I may have built a field for a problem that had already ended, and kept it because I had felt like a person who helps."

No one wrote that down. Danielle's hand stayed on the closed notebook. Helen's irritation with him, the part about the email and the uncommitted shutdown, seemed to change temperature. She was not softer. She was more interested, which was not the same gift.

"I should tell you something before you ask me what you'd be walking into," she said. "I asked for this conversation. Recruiting didn't find you. The migration convinced me. You restored a backup, and you wrote down the two rows with a null channel. People do not invent nulls when they are performing. "What is the sample?" he said.

"A small scheduling bug in a toy service. You fix it. You write how you knew. You record yourself for twenty minutes."

He imagined the recording. He imagined himself explaining a toy while Tower's jobs ran. The objection that rose in him was partly pride, and he did not want pride as his representative.

"I can do it," he said. "If I do it, I'll answer it as the toy it is. I won't drag my system in to make the toy look smaller. If the recording is how you compare, compare the recording. The thing I can't do is pretend the toy is why I'm in the room. You already know why I'm in the room."

"Fair," Helen said.

"It's fair if everyone gets the same extra hour with you," Danielle said. "They don't."

"No," Helen said. "They don't. I'm not going to insult him by calling that fairness after the fact."

She turned back to Wale. The turn was physical. The chair creaked. "There is a logo in the lobby you spent time with. Ruth mentioned you knew it, when I came in. She wasn't gossiping. She was telling me the coffee was finished. It came out in the same breath."

He had not heard Ruth say it. He had been ahead of Danielle in the hall by then, believing the logo had stayed in the lobby with the apples. It had not even lasted the walk to Simcoe. He felt foolish, and then he felt the ordinary fact that offices were small.

"Pell & Rahman," he said.

"They're a customer. Nadia is in here on Fridays, as Ruth likes to warn people. Did you work with them?"

"I refused them. She asked for a portal that would be theirs alone. I said I wouldn't build a second system. She told me I would understand the refusal differently when I had a company. I let the word stand. I shouldn't have. Omcoda was already the company. The refusal was the company."

"Would you be able to work on a product that says yes more often than you do?" Helen said.

This was the question he had written in the notebook and then disliked, arriving from the other side. He owed it a real answer because she had asked it without the notebook's stiffness.

"I don't know," he said. "If I worked here I might build the exception I wouldn't build for her. I've been sitting with that since the lobby. I haven't finished deciding whether it would be dishonest or whether it would be the first time my preference met a payroll that isn't only mine. Those aren't the same. I can feel myself wanting them to be the same, because the same is cleaner."

"Which firm?" Danielle said, and then, quickly, "You don't have to say."

"I don't want to name a conversation she thought was private," he said. "The logo isn't a secret. The coffee is."

Helen accepted it. He could see the annoyance go through her anyway, a small one, at being made to feel nosy for a curiosity he had provoked and then fenced. She did not push. The not pushing cost her the pleasure of confirming a guess she had already made. Nadia's name had done half of it. He saw that too late. He had refused the surname and then used the first name earlier without noticing. No: he had not said Nadia. Helen had. Ruth had not. So Helen knew the story from a side he had not been on, or she knew Nadia well enough that a refused portal was a thing Nadia would have mentioned on a Friday, lightly, as a joke about a one-person company. The thought that Nadia had described him to this room, months ago, in his absence, moved through him like a change in pressure. He had no way to ask.

"She told me," Helen said, as if he had asked. Maybe his face had. "Not your name. She said a young man with a system had lectured her about owning two of them and had left her to us. She was funny about it. She was also still irritated. When Samir sent me your note I did not connect them. The connection occurred to me when Ruth said you had stopped at the logos. I shouldn't pretend I didn't enjoy the connection. I did. It made you less abstract. It also means Nadia will have an opinion if she sees you in a hallway. You should know that before you tell me you can sit in a place that says yes."

"What was the opinion?" he said.

"That you were proud. That you might be right. She held both, which is more than most of our customers do when they've been refused."

He took it. Proud was not wrong. Right was not a crown he was going to pick up off her Friday anecdote. "If I met her here I would not relitigate the portal," he said. "She already heard the sentence once. Hearing it from an employee of the company she hired would be a worse version of it."

"Good," Helen said. "Because I would not let you."

The sentence was a boundary and a relief. He had not known he wanted the boundary until it was in the room. Some part of him had been ready to be the conscience of a place he did not yet work. She had seen that part and declined it in advance. He felt the decline as a correction, the way a wrong transition felt when the test failed. The heat in his face was simpler than the compensation heat. He nodded.

She asked what he wanted to ask.

He had the two surviving questions, and the one that had occurred to him while she talked about the morning they had lost.

"When a reminder is wrong," he said, "who gets woken, and is that the person who can change the job?"

"The on-call is a rotation of four," Helen said. "Two of them can change the job. One of them can open a pull request and wait for me. One of them pages me no matter what the chart says, because he has been here eleven years and he does not believe the chart. I am trying to make the chart true before I hire someone into the fiction of it."

"When a caseworker overrides a calculated deadline, where does the override live?"

"In a column on the same row. Nullable. If it's filled, the screen prefers it and the job prefers it. We keep the calculated value beside it. We do not keep a history of the override beyond the audit log, and the audit log is weaker than I tell people it is. You can see who changed it last. You cannot see the value they replaced if they changed it twice in a day. I have a note to fix that. The note is four months old."

"What happens when a firm wants a workflow that breaks the model?"

"We have a configurations table I dislike," she said. "We have a services team that writes the configuration and then calls me when the configuration implies a state I don't have. Sometimes I add the state. Sometimes I make them put the exception in a manual step and they hate me for a quarter and the manual step saves us a year. The rule is not written down. The rule is my mood and my memory of the last incident. That is the part of this job I am least proud of. A person joining Obligations will inherit my mood unless they force it into a page we can argue about. I want that. I also fight the people who try, because the page makes me visible."

He recognized the pinned note in what she had confessed. He did not say he recognized it. Saying so would have been a way of twinning himself to her before he had earned the twin.

"When those conflict," he said, "the invariant and the firm that has to succeed this quarter, what do you want from the person in the pull request?"

"I can name it," he said. "I'm less sure I can name it and stay employed. I haven't had a boss. I've had clients. With clients I can refuse and lose the money. The refusal is clean because the loss is mine. Here the loss would land on people I don't know yet. I might get polite at exactly the moment you want the name."

"Then we would find out," Helen said. "I would rather find out in a request than in a hallway."

The team was in the office three days. He said that was fine. He lived on Dovercourt and the subway was not a theory he would discover later. She asked whether he had questions about benefits. He said he did, and then he found that his questions were his mother's questions, dental and the drug plan and whether the month he asked for would cost him the start of coverage. He felt the meanness of the morning again, the not-telling, and set it aside. It would keep.

"If we don't continue, it will not be because you have two firms or because the degree is paused. It might be because I don't believe you can leave a fire that is yours. They stood. Danielle shook his hand and said it had been good to meet him, which was true in a sense she would have to examine later and which she meant, in the moment, as a closing of the form. Helen's handshake was brief. She looked tired in the way of a person who has done a thing she wanted and now has the rest of the day she had postponed. At the door she said, "The queue is under five. I checked while you were talking about the pinned note. I wasn't not listening."

"I didn't think you weren't," he said. He had, for a second. The second had been about himself.

Ruth printed nothing and asked for nothing except the badge, which he had folded into his pocket. He smoothed it without comedy and put it on the desk. "Thank you for the water."

He took the stairs so he would not stand in the small elevator with his face still arranged for the room. The fryer smell was stronger at noon. On Peter Street the light had gone flat and white. He moved out of their line and stood by the parking garage entrance, where the air was cooler and smelled of concrete.

He took the phone out. Samir's two words were still unanswered. Lena's message was under them. He opened Lena's.

We're going to need to pause for a bit. Cash is tight until November. Can you keep the lights on. I know the invoice is late. I don't want to pretend it isn't. If you can't, tell me this week so I can export before Friday.

She had not used a question mark. She rarely did when she was afraid of the answer. He had watched her, in the single conference room she rented by the hour, speak in statements to clients who needed statements. The fear was in the shortness.

He began a reply. The first sentence came out in the cadence he had just been using. The monitoring doesn't pause cleanly, because the windows don't pause, and a quiet system still has dates inside it. He read it and deleted it. She had not asked him for the nature of the system. She had asked whether he would be there, and she had given him a week because she was decent. He had been in a room where a woman had asked, in another vocabulary, whether he could be in a codebase he did not own while his own queue burned. He did not yet know the answer. Putting Helen's distinction into Lena's inbox would have been a way of sounding like a person who had just been precise, at the expense of a person who needed a yes or a no.

He put the cursor at the top of the reply and left it there.

Then the other memory arrived, late, the way a missed row arrives after the report has been sent.

There had been a twelfth. Not in the eleven waiting and not in the two already gone. A digest, the next morning, which he had forgotten because the digest was a different schedule and he had turned off only the one he was afraid of. Ifeoma had found it. She had called him at eight. She had not been quiet that time. She had said, "You told me the list was finished." He had told her the truth, which was that he had finished the list he had remembered. In Simcoe, describing the morning, he had said two had already gone, and he had told her about both before noon, and the sentence had been true, and it had stood in for a completeness that was false. He had believed it while he was saying it. The belief was the worst part. He had not sat in Simcoe and managed an omission. His face heated in the cooler air of the garage entrance. A man with a sandwich asked him if he was going in. He said no and moved a step aside.

Helen had told him not to help her decide in a follow-up email. The twelfth was not that help. It was the same class of correction as the order of the index. Lena had replied with a single word, okay, and then, after an hour, a second message listing the filings that scared her in October. He read the list. Two of the dates were wrong against Tower, off by a day, the old off-by-one that came from a window stored as a date in one office and as a datetime in the other. When he had finished the note he sat, finally, on the one chair that did not face the window. The two October dates were still wrong. He wrote the test first, against the dates in the ministry letters, and watched it fail. The letters were the authority. His display had been a convenience that had drifted. Then he got up and moved the egg pan off the burner, because he could already smell the start of it sticking, and the morning would be worse if he left it.
