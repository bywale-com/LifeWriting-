The Interview

He had printed the note because he did not want to stand in a lobby looking down for permission to come upstairs. The paper had been folded twice. On the outside was the address, Peter Street, third floor, and the two names in the order Sabrina had sent them: Maya Chen first, Priya Raman if the morning slipped. On the inside, where he would have had to open it in front of someone to see it again, was the sentence he had not known what to do with. Priya will have read the note. She won't have read you. Don't help her skip that.

The streetcar had been crowded as far as Queen and then, as if the city had remembered an appointment, it thinned. He stood anyway. Sitting would have put the folded paper in his lap and he would have opened it. A man beside him ate a pastry out of a paper bag with great attention, protecting his coat from the flakes, and Wale watched the care of it longer than he meant to, because it was easier than arriving. At the stop he checked the street number against the paper once, though he had already checked it on the corner. The building was brick, narrower than the glass one beside it, with a scaffold over the entrance and a courier arguing softly with a buzzer. He waited for the courier to finish. There was no principle in the waiting. He only did not want to step into someone else's errand and then have to speak.

The elevator smelled of wet wool and coffee. Beside the third-floor door a plate said Northspan. He had looked them up after Sabrina said the work was filing dates for accounting firms, and then he had closed the tab, because reading their pages felt like arriving already employed. The door opened onto a room that was trying not to look like an office and had lost. There were plants, and a sofa the colour of putty, and a bowl of oranges with one orange beginning to go dull at the stem. Behind a desk, a woman with her hair in a clip was on a call, holding up a finger that was not unfriendly and not an apology, only a fact about the order of things. He stood where she could see him and did not approach the desk until she had finished. When she did, she looked at the screen and then at him.

"Omotayo?"

"Yes. Wale."

She tried the first name and missed it by a syllable, landing near a name he had answered to in high school when correcting it would have taken more time than the conversation. He said it again, once, the way he said it at home, and she repeated it correctly and looked relieved to have done so. She typed, frowned at what the calendar had done to his last name, and wrote the sticker by hand. WALE OMOTAYO. The letters were clear. She peeled it and passed it over, and he put it on the shirt pocket because there was nowhere else that did not feel like a costume. The shirt was the better of the two clean ones. He had decided at seven-forty and then doubted it on the streetcar and could do nothing about the doubt now except refuse to touch the collar.

"They're running a little behind," she said. "Maya will come for you. If you want water, the glasses are by the machine. The oranges are decorative. I keep telling them."

"Thank you."

"Denise."

He said her name back, so he would have it, and went to the sofa. He did not take an orange. He set his bag by his foot and his phone face down on the cushion beside him, a habit that had started in a library and had never been examined. For a minute he looked at the room as if it might be on an exam later: the track worn into the carpet from the door to the kitchen, the glass room where two people were speaking with their voices lowered and their hands active, a paper on the wall that said OWN THE OUTCOME in letters large enough to be seen from the elevator. He felt, very briefly, the small pleasure of finding the phrase cheap. The pleasure was familiar and he did not respect it. He looked at the carpet track instead, which had been made by actual feet and could not be used against anyone.

He had a list. He hated that he had a list. Two firms, not an agency. The degree unfinished. He had come because someone who had used the system thought a person here had read an incident note. If he pretended to want the job more than he did, the hour would be about the wrong thing. He kept the list anyway. People who had nothing prepared often called the nothing honesty.

From the glass room a man's voice rose just enough to clear the glass. "The client doesn't care about your queue." The voice was injured and a little proud of the injury. Wale thought of Martin Hale, who had been angry in a smaller way, without an audience, and felt a loyalty to that smaller anger that he would not have been able to defend. He did not know these people. He was already arranging them.

A person about his age came out of a side hallway with a visitor sticker half peeled off, saw him on the sofa, and said, "Good luck," in the tone of a confederacy that was supposed to exist between anyone waiting. Wale thanked him. The thanks was a half-second late and did not offer anything back, no grimace, no where-are-you-in-the-loop. The other man nodded as if he had expected less and went to the elevator already pulling at his sleeve. Wale saw that he had refused a small fellowship he had not meant to refuse. He stayed on the sofa. Chasing it would have been a second performance, worse than the first.

He turned the phone over and then back. Sabrina had said Thursday in one message and Wednesday in the next, and he had believed the calendar invite because calendar invites were how this kind of place made things true. He was early by twenty minutes even against the invite. Early was not a virtue he had chosen. It was what happened when he did not trust the Queen car.

He had been in the kitchen at his place, which was not a kitchen so much as a wall with a coil burner, when she called on the Sunday. A pot was on. He turned it down and put her on speaker because his hands were wet, then took her off speaker because the wall was thin and the man next door worked nights.

"There's a staff engineer here," Sabrina had said. "Priya. She said if she has to interview another person whose worst bug lived in a textbook she's going to cancel the req. I told her I knew someone whose bug called me."

"You told her that."

"I told her not to hire you because I said so. I told her to read the note. The clean one, if you'll send it. I shouldn't have—" She stopped. He heard a fridge door, the suck of the seal. "I said your name before I asked you. I'm asking now. You can say no and I'll tell her I got ahead of myself, which is true."

The water in the pot had begun to fret against the metal. He moved it off the coil. He had felt, standing there with steam on his wrist, two things that did not agree: gratitude of a plain kind, because she had thought of the work and not of a version of him that interviewed well, and a resistance to being handed across a kitchen to a stranger who was tired. He also felt the old exposure, the sense that a room he was still cleaning had been opened from the hallway.

"Send her the incident," he said. "I'll take the names out tonight. Don't send her the thread."

"I won't."

He had believed her, which was not the same as having asked what she had already done.

On the sofa he had got that far in the remembering when Sabrina herself came out of the kitchen with a glass of water and stopped so abruptly that a little of it jumped onto her wrist.

"You're here," she said.

"You said Wednesday in the invite."

"I thought Thursday when I saw you. That's my fault, not the invite's." She shook the water off her wrist onto the carpet, a small domestic gesture that made her look more like the person who had sat beside him in an office above a dentistry than like someone who belonged to the glass room. She was dressed more carefully than she had been at Hale. The care made him sad in a way he had no right to, so he did not look at the clothes long. "I wasn't going to come manage this."

"You aren't."

"I'm getting water and going back to a call." She glanced at the hallway, then at his shirt, then at his face, in that order. There was something she was not saying. He could see the shape of the withholding, the way her mouth stayed neutral past the moment when she would ordinarily have told him a useful unpleasant thing. At Hale she had been bad at concealment and had known it and had used the knowledge as a kind of manners: better you hear it from my face than from Martin's later. Here the concealment was new. He did not reach in after it.

"Priya read the note," Sabrina said. "The clean one. That's all I'm going to brief."

"All right."

"You don't have to be grateful in the room. I already know." She said it quickly, so that neither of them would have to find out whether he was. Then she went down the hall with the glass, and the wet mark on the carpet darkened and stayed.

He understood that she had protected him from a piece of information and had also protected herself from being a person who coached a candidate in a hallway. Both motives were real. He sat with his hands on his knees and did not rehearse the list again. Denise called someone else forward. The oranges remained uneaten. When Maya Chen came for him she was already speaking his name correctly, having heard it from Denise, and she apologized for nine minutes as if nine minutes were a dog that had got out and she had brought it back.

The room she took him to was small and internal, no window, a table with a notch in the edge where someone's laptop had bitten it. He put his phone face down beside the notch, as he had on the sofa, and then disliked himself for the gesture and left it. There were two chairs on her side and one on his. The empty chair had a sweater on it. She moved the sweater to the sill of a blind window and did not explain it, which meant the sweater belonged to a meeting that had been real in the morning and was not real now. She had a notebook and a closed laptop. The laptop wore stickers that had been chosen to suggest a life and had ended by suggesting a store.

"We'll do this part together," she said, "and then Priya. Priya's in something. She'll come when she can. I run the first conversation for the Obligations role: the practical things, how you work with people who aren't engineers, what you're actually looking for. I don't grade the code. If I pretend to, you should assume I overheard a word."

"That's clear," he said.

"Good. Some people still try to impress me with a stack." She smiled, and the smile was professional and not empty. "Walk me through the background. Not the whole childhood. The part that got you into the room."

He began in the wrong place, which was the place he lived. "The system watches dates and rules on immigration files and tells the firm when a file is about to go quiet or go bad. The firm does the talking. I don't take a brief and build a custom app and hand it over. There are two firms. Omcoda is the name on the invoice and the name on the server."

Maya's pen hovered. "So you help immigrants navigate the process."

"No. The firms do that. I keep a piece of software running so the people at the firms know which file needs a human being today."

"Got it," she said, in the voice of a person putting a cup back on a saucer that had almost tipped. "B2B. And you're the founder."

"I'm the person who built it and operates it. Founder makes it sound like there's a round and a team. There isn't. There's a bookkeeper once a quarter."

She wrote something. From his side of the table he could not read it, and he refused the indignity of trying. "Help me understand the scale."

He had known the question was coming and had decided, on the streetcar, that he would rather have the number in the room than the fog he had heard other people use. "Hale Immigration pays fourteen hundred a month. Larkin Reid pays nine. Hale is a month behind. Last year, collected, it was a little under thirty thousand. I'm not supporting a staff on it. I'm supporting the system and part of a rent."

The pen slowed. She did not do him the insult of saying love that, and he was grateful in a way he would not express, because the gratitude would have been for a disaster avoided that he had invented. She said, "Thank you. People often say traction and then I have to spend twenty minutes finding out whether traction means a cousin." She looked up. "Does the lateness worry you?"

"It tells me Hale's cash is lumpy and that Martin doesn't like owing a person he has also been angry at. I don't think it tells me the software is a hobby. It might tell you that. You'd have reasons."

"I haven't decided what it tells me," she said, which he thought was true, and also the sort of true thing a careful interviewer said while deciding. "You're in a degree program."

"I'm in one course. The rest is paused. I paused a semester when Hale came on, because the onboarding ate the evenings the lectures sat in. I don't have a graduation date. If the role needs the date, I should say so now."

"The posting says bachelor's or equivalent practical experience. Priya wrote the equivalent. Recruiting argued with her and lost, on the condition that this conversation still happened." Maya said it without bitterness he could detect, which did not mean the bitterness was absent. It might have meant she had spent it earlier, on someone who could do something about it. "What course?"

"Databases."

"Useful, or irritating?"

He was surprised into an actual answer. "Both. The lectures are about guarantees I skipped and then met in production under worse names. The assignments are slow. I sit in them anyway."

She nodded as if she had placed a pin and would come back. "Tell me about a time you had a conflict with someone you didn't manage, and how it ended."

The list tried to stand up in him, wearing the shape of a story with a result. He set it aside, or thought he did. "Martin Hale wanted a status on a file changed so that a meeting with a client would be easier. The file had lapsed. He wanted it to read in progress. If I had stored that, the monitor would have contradicted it the next morning, or I would have had to teach the monitor to lie. I said no. I offered him a separate sheet, an export, for what he intended to say in the meeting, sitting next to the state and not inside it. He called the export condescending. He was partly right. The name I had put on it was polite in a way that showed I thought he needed the truth labeled for him. I renamed it. He used it twice and then stopped telling me things for about a month. In that month Sabrina had to guess which files he had promised people what. The firm didn't leave. I don't know if that counts as the result you want. The work was worse before it was better."

Maya had stopped writing in the middle. "When you refused, who were you protecting?"

"The record. And the person who would have been told a calmer story than the file."

"I didn't ask what. I asked who."

He looked at the notch in the table. The difference was real, and he had slid past it because the what was the part he had practiced in his head on nights when he could not sleep, the justification, the invariant, the word lie. The who was closer to the skin.

"The client," he said. "And myself, because I didn't want to be the one who had agreed. Sabrina would have been the one on the phone. I should have said her before I said myself."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I talk about the system first when I'm uncomfortable. It isn't a principle. It's a sequence I fall into."

Maya sat back. The sweater on the sill slipped an inch and neither of them looked at it for long. "What I'm hearing," she said, and then she paused, and he watched her decide not to finish the sentence with a summary he would have to agree to. "Most people, when they notice they've left someone out, put the person back in and keep the story heroic. You put her back and then told me the reason was a habit. I don't know yet whether that's going to be hard to work with. I do know I'll remember it."

He did not know what to do with the remembering. Praise, if it was praise, seemed to require a receipt. "I wasn't trying to be hard to work with."

"I believe you," she said. "That isn't always comforting." She turned a page. "Sabrina spoke highly of you."

The sentence was the polished door. What had happened in that kitchen, two weeks before the Sunday call, Sabrina had not told him. Priya had been at the microwave with a bowl that was too hot to hold. She had said the thing about textbooks. Sabrina, who had been waiting for the machine to finish its indifferent churn, had said: I know someone whose bug called me at night. Priya had asked if this was a friend. Sabrina had said: He runs the thing we used at my last firm. I was the one using it. Don't hire him because I said so. Then she had gone to her desk and found that she still had, in a personal folder she should not have made when she left Hale, the thread. She had forwarded it because a description of a note is not a note, and because she wanted to be believed the first time, and because she was tired of being the person in implementations who mentioned a system and watched engineers smile with their mouths only. Ten minutes later the fear had arrived, late the way fear does when the action has already gone out. She had written Priya: please delete that, I'll get you a clean version, I wasn't supposed to have the names. Priya had already read it at the microwave, standing, the bowl burning a ring into her thumb.

Wale knew none of the microwave. He knew only what Sabrina had confessed in the Sunday call, which had stopped at I said your name before I asked you, and what she had promised about the thread. So when Maya said spoke highly, he had nothing honest he could put in its place that was not also a violation of Sabrina's own mess.

"She used it every day," he said. "She knows which parts broke. That's the recommendation I'd trust. The rest is kindness, and kindness isn't evidence."

"So a client referral."

"She worked at the client. She was the person the system was for. If you write client referral, write that too, or the phrase will make it sound like Martin sent me."

Maya's mouth did something small. "Does Martin know you're here?"

"No. I don't think he'd forbid it. I also don't think he'd understand it as separate from me disappearing on the system. I haven't decided about the job. I decided to have the conversation. If I tell him before I know what the conversation is, I'll be managing his fear with bad information."

She wrote. He thought she wrote client, and then a word he hoped was user, and he let it go. Correcting her notebook line by line would have been a way of taking the interview out of her hands. He could see, watching her face, that she was fitting him into a structure because the structure was how twelve conversations became comparable by Friday, and that the fitting was not contempt for him. It was a tool she was responsible for keeping in order. He had felt, a moment earlier, a thin superiority to the tool. Her question about who had caught him using a better tool than his. He put the superiority down. It did not applaud him for putting it down.

"Why do you want to leave Omcoda?" she said.

"I'm not sure I do."

The room changed temperature without anyone touching anything. Maya looked at the empty chair as if the sweater's owner might have been useful just then. "People don't usually say that in this room."

"If I say I want to leave, you'll spend the time testing a plan I haven't made. I came because Sabrina said someone here had read an incident write-up and might want to talk about it. I want that talk. A job might sit on the other side of it. I don't want to walk in already wearing the job."

"And if Priya likes you and Helen, her manager, likes Priya's notes and we make you an offer next week?"

"Then I'll have to answer a question I haven't answered. I'd rather answer it then, with the work in my head, than answer it now because the script expects it."

Maya closed the notebook on her finger. "I'm going to say something plainly. A person who might refuse is not a waste of my time. A person who uses the interview to feel understood, and then discovers he wanted to be understood and not employed, costs Priya a morning she doesn't have. She is already in a hole today. I need to know you know that."

"I know it," he said. "I can't make the cost zero without lying about where I am. If that's disqualifying you should say so."

"It isn't, yet." She opened the notebook again. The yet was doing real work. "Compensation. What are you looking for?"

He heard himself say eighty-five thousand and felt, in the same instant, that he had priced a different object: more than the invoices, enough to keep the room off Roncesvalles and the paused courses and the server, enough to stop doing the arithmetic on Martin's late month. The number had come from his life. It had not come from the market this room belonged to. Maya's face did not flicker into triumph. That was worse. She had heard low numbers before and had a procedure for them.

"The band on this role starts above that," she said. "I'm not going to tell you the top. I am going to tell you that if you leave the eighty-five in the notes, it will follow you into a conversation you aren't in. Do you want to revise it, or do you want me to write that you priced before you understood the band?"

He was quiet long enough that she did not fill it. The silence was hers, and she was competent at it. He could feel the cheap rescue available: a higher number, said with a little laugh at his own innocence. The laugh would have made him the sort of person the sofa confederate had wanted. He did not have the laugh.

"Write that I priced the wrong thing," he said. "I don't want to correct it by guessing what won't embarrass me. If we get to an offer I'll ask what the work is worth in your band and I'll answer then. I know that leaves you with a messy note."

"Messy I can use," she said. "Charm I have to translate." She said it as information, not as a slap. He took it as information and still felt the blood in his ears, because he had been seen wanting not to be embarrassed, and the wanting was visible even though he had refused the rescue.

She asked him whether he was authorized to work in Canada. He said yes. She asked when he could start. He said he would need a month to put the two firms in a state he could leave without the monitor going blind, and that he had not asked them whether a month was real. She asked how he worked with people who were not technical when they were frightened. He told her about a Tuesday in November and then found he was telling it wrongly, from the logs outward.

He stopped. "The order is the phone, not the logs."

Sabrina had called while he was in the library on the second floor, in a carrel whose walls were covered with other people's erased pencil. His phone had been face down because the man across the table had asked him, with a look, to be a person who did not have a phone. He had seen the screen light the laminate and had thought: after this page. The page was not important. He had wanted to finish a thought about a foreign key. When he turned the phone over there were two missed calls and a message that said simply call, in the lower case she used when she had no time to be a personality. Nine minutes. He had gone to the stairwell, where the concrete made every voice a confession.

She was past polite. Three clients had received the same reactivation twice. One of them had written back in a way she was going to have to answer before the day got any older. Martin was still in a meeting and would be worse after it. Wale had not said he was in a library. He had asked which files. His voice had come out flat, because if he let the alarm into it she would have to spend energy on his alarm, and he had decided, there on the stairs, that this would be a kindness. She had not experienced a kindness. She had experienced a person treating a frightened afternoon as a ticket. He had understood that only later, in her reply, and by then the understanding was useless in the way understanding often is.

"I called her back nine minutes late," he said to Maya. "I was finishing a page. The page could have waited. Three emails had gone twice. I spoke to her as if calm would help, and what she needed was a person who sounded like he grasped that she had to face the clients before I grasped the bug. I stayed on the phone while I looked at the logs. I fixed the immediate queue so it wouldn't send a fourth. The actual repair I did overnight. I wrote it up and sent it at one in the morning because I didn't trust myself to remember the fault accurately after sleep. She wrote back that she had read it and that I was not to write her at one, she was on the phones at eight. That sentence is part of the incident. If I tell the incident as a technical story and leave the sentence out, I've edited it into something I can admire."

Maya was quiet. Then she said, "What did you do with the sentence?"

"I stopped sending her night writing. I send in the morning now, even if the morning version is worse. I also—" He paused, because the next part was the part that made him look like a person constructing a personality for interviews. It happened to be true. "I still write the night version for myself. I just don't make her the address."

"All right," Maya said. She looked tired in a way that had not been on her face at the door. "I'm going to hand you to Priya when she surfaces. Before I do, I want one practical thing. If she is blunt, she is not angry at you. She is angry at a job that ate her sleep, and she doesn't always clean the anger off the table before she sets a glass down. Don't clean it for her. Answer the question she asked."

"Has she already talked to you about me?"

"She told me to look, and not to screen you out for the degree, and to tell her if I was going to say no before I said it. She didn't tell me what she thought of the work. Priya doesn't borrow my vocabulary and I try not to borrow hers." Maya stood. "Water?"

"Please."

While she was gone he looked at the empty chair and understood that the sweater was not Priya's. Priya, when she arrived, would not be a person who left a sweater as a marker of intent. He drank the water Maya brought. She said she would see whether the incident was ending, left the notebook closed on the table, and did not come back. The few minutes she had named became eighteen. He did not go out to find Denise and he did not take out the incident note to reread his own prose. He sat. At some point he noticed he was constructing answers again, and he stopped, and the stopping required an effort visible to no one, which did not make it unreal. Through the wall he heard someone laugh and then cut the laugh off, as if laughter were a door that had swung too wide.

Priya Raman came in with a laptop under her arm and a chip in the rim of a mug, and she shut the door with her heel because her hands were full. She was older than he had pictured from the email, or rather she was exactly some age he had failed to picture, because he had pictured competence and not a face. Her eyes were already in the room before her apology was.

"Eighteen minutes," she said. "A worker was chewing the same row. I'm sorry. Tomás stopped it. If I say we'll start clean, I'll be lying for the first ten minutes, so I won't."

"Who's Tomás?"

She set the mug down. The question had arrived before the handshake she had not offered. She looked at him as if recalculating the length of the table. "The person whose seat you'd be nearer than mine, if any of this went anywhere. He owns ingestion. He's leaving in six weeks. This morning he is still the one who can kill a bad claim without making a second bad claim. Sit, if you aren't sitting. You are sitting. Good."

She sat. She did not open the laptop. "Maya asked what she asks. I'm going to ask about the note, and about things around the note, and I'm going to be wrong about your system at least once because I read it on a Sunday and I read it as if it were ours. Correct me when I do that. If you wait to be polite, we'll spend the hour in my mistake."

"All right," he said.

"You stored the idempotency key in Redis."

"No. It's a unique index in Postgres. I didn't want a second store that could say yes when the database said no."

"Right," she said. "That's ours. I pasted our shape onto yours. Go."

He told it from the failure, because she had read the failure and did not need the brochure. A monitoring run decided a file needed a reactivation message. The worker inserted nothing first. It sent, and only after the provider accepted the message did it write the key: firm, case, pathway, cycle date, kind. If the process died between the acceptance and the write, the next run saw no key and sent again. It had died that way on a Tuesday in November, not from drama, from a deploy he had started too late in the evening and a process that had not come back before the scheduler fired. Three messages doubled. He had changed it so the intent row was inserted first, in a transaction, state pending, and the send happened after the commit. A retry found the row and did not send a second time if the state was sent. If the send failed, the row stayed pending and the retry was the point.

"And if you die after the provider accepts and before you mark sent," Priya said.

"The row is still pending. A second worker, or the same one, can send again. I didn't close that window. The provider doesn't give me a dedupe key I trust. There's a sweeper that looks for pending rows older than fifteen minutes and checks the provider log before it sends. If the log is behind, the sweeper can still double. I haven't had that happen. That's not the same as it being shut."

She nodded. The nod was a filing cabinet, not a blessing. "How many workers run?"

"One."

"So you haven't met the other bug."

"Two of them taking the same row."

"Say it the way it would actually happen."

"I read the pending row, then I set started_at. The unique index stops a second intent. It doesn't stop a second send of the one intent, if another process gets through the read before the write. With one worker that overlap hasn't happened. I haven't claimed it in a single update. I haven't needed to, which is how I talked myself into waiting."

"SKIP LOCKED?"

"I know the words. I haven't used them. We said them in class last week." He heard the we and let it stand, though the class was mostly people who did not have a queue in production, and the we was a kindness he was giving himself. "I repeated the words back. I didn't go home and look at the claim."

Priya wrapped both hands around the mug as if it were hotter than it was. "What isolation do you run?"

"Read committed. The default. I didn't choose it so much as leave it."

"Your current-state view," she said. "You rebuild it in the same transaction as the new determination. The monitor reads the view. Does the monitor's read sit inside that transaction?"

"No. The monitor reads, decides, then the intent write is its own transaction."

She waited. The waiting was not a trap in the theatrical sense. It was a space in which he could hear his own design. He heard it. A determination could commit while a monitor run had already read the old current row. Under read committed the run would not see the new row. It could send, or not send, on a state that had already been replaced. He had thought the view was safe because the write of the view was atomic with the event. The safety ended at the transaction boundary. He had walked past the boundary for a year.

The heat in his face was immediate and without dignity. He did not touch his collar. "A run can act on a stale current row if it read before the commit. I didn't see it. I saw the transaction that writes the view and I stopped looking there, because that was the part I had fixed. The fix was real and it wasn't the whole edge."

"When did you learn the name for what I just described?"

"Eight days ago. In the lecture. They called it a phenomenon. I wrote it down."

"And you didn't put the name on your own job."

"No."

She drank. The chip on the rim clicked against her tooth and she adjusted without commenting on it. "Look at it tonight. Not for me. If you look at it for me you'll write it in the voice of this room and then you won't recognize the hole the next time, because the next time won't have my sentence sitting on top of it."

"I'll look at it because it's mine," he said. "I was going to say I'll look at it tonight as a way of closing the subject. The subject isn't closed."

"Good." She opened the laptop then, not to a test, but to a file he recognized, his own schema rendered in her notes, with comments in a hand that was shockingly small. "I'm going to tell you what I thought on Sunday so you can throw out the parts that are nonsense. I thought the event table for determinations was the right source. I thought the nightly rebuild you mentioned in the margin, the one you abandoned, would have drifted, and the same-transaction rebuild was the adult version. I thought the tenancy was a firm_id column and a prayer. I thought the three-step migration, the one where you refused a default, was the best thing in the packet. I also thought you might have had help you didn't name."

"Which part."

"The date arithmetic. It reads like someone who knows the library and not the calendar. The rest reads like one person who has been alone with the consequences."

He could have defended the date function. The defense would have been true and incomplete, and he was tired, suddenly, of true incomplete defenses, having just given two. "An assistant drafted the first version of the expiry calculation. I read it. I shipped it. I had checked the shape and not the boundary. In March a date moved by a day over the daylight change and Sabrina saw it before I did, because a file on her screen said a document expired tomorrow when the document expired today. I rewrote the function by hand after that. There is one branch in the rewrite, the null expiry, that I copied back from the draft because it was late and the branch looked obvious. I could tell you what it does now. I could not have told you the night I shipped it. The determinations, the intents, the migration, those I wrote. If something in them sounds generated, it's because I was tired, not because a tool chose the state."

"How do you know you aren't doing that with the parts you call yours?"

"I don't know it the way you want. I rewrite anything that touches a status in my own words before it stays. Some nights the rewrite is shorter than the fear. Those are the nights I should throw it out. I haven't always."

Priya looked at the small handwriting as if it might have changed. "The null branch. What does it do?"

"If there's no expiry it treats the pathway as not time-bounded and suppresses the reminder. It does not treat null as expired. The wrong choice would have paged the firm for every incomplete file. I understood that part even on the night. What I didn't understand was the interaction with a pathway that is time-bounded and merely missing its date because nobody had typed it. Those suppressed too. Sabrina called that a silence. She was right. Missing is not the same as not applicable. I split them afterward. The split is mine."

"All right," Priya said. The word had more weight than good would have. "Tell me about the migration I liked, and then tell me why you're about to ruin the liking."

He almost smiled and did not, because a smile would have been an agreement that they were playing. "Eligible was doing two jobs in one column. It meant the rule had been evaluated, and it meant the answer was yes. Old rows that had never been evaluated would have become yes if I had backfilled a default. I added a third state, unknown, in a column that could be null for one release, backfilled unknown where there was no determination event, and only then set the constraint. The names are bad. evaluated and outcome would have been honest. I used status_state because I was close to the code and far from the reader. I haven't renamed them. Renaming is another migration and I kept choosing features the firms could see."

"I know why the default would have been worse," she said. "I was complimenting you. The three-step is the kind of thing I have trouble getting people to do when nobody is making them."

He had already drawn breath to explain the default, as if she had missed it. The breath was visible. He let it out. "Thank you."

The thanks was late and stiff and he looked at the mug rather than at her, and the lateness sat in the room as a third object. She did not rescue him from it. He understood, a moment too late, that she had offered a piece of regard and that his reflex had been to turn regard back into a lesson he was giving. The reflex was not humility. It was a refusal to be seen in the particular way she had chosen. He did not add a second sentence. Adding one would have been the same reflex in a nicer coat.

"You still haven't renamed the columns," she said.

"No."

"If you came here, you'd be in a codebase where the bad name is sometimes mine. Tomás wanted a ticket and a rename before every filing season. I wanted the season to happen. We fought. He wasn't wrong about the record. I wasn't wrong about the calendar. He's leaving partly because he got tired of winning the argument and losing the week. If that sounds like a warning, it is one."

Wale turned the glass of water a quarter turn. The condensation had made a ring he had been careful not to put on the schema printout, which was not on the table; the schema was only on her screen. He was inventing objects to avoid the question inside what she had said. "I don't know which of those I would fail. This month I would fail yours. I'd rename the column and feel serious while Hale's dates went out wrong. In November I failed his. I shipped the deploy that died, and I shipped it because I wanted the fix in before morning, and there was no ticket because there was no one to write the ticket to."

"You could have written it to yourself."

"I write notes to myself. They don't refuse me. That's the part I can't get from a notebook."

Priya was quiet in the way of a person matching a shape to a hole she had already. "You asked someone to read the code."

"Once. A classmate. He said it was cool and sent it back the same night. The comments were on the README, on the description, not on the claim logic. I decided asking was a kind of debt. The decision felt like dignity at the time. It was also anger. I don't get a reviewer out of dignity. I get the Tuesday night."

"If you work here, I will read your migrations. I will be slower than your dignity wants, and ruder than a classmate, and sometimes wrong. Tomás would tell you I nitpick the names because I can't stand a story I didn't author. He is twenty percent right. The other eighty is that a bad name in Obligations becomes a support call in February, and February is when firms find out they don't understand their own filings."

"I can live with slow," Wale said. "I don't know yet if I can live with a name I think is wrong because the season is here. I might argue. I wouldn't argue by deploying over you. If that's the bar, it's a low bar, and it's the one I can actually promise."

She looked at him then without the filing cabinet in it. "Maya said you aren't sure you want to leave."

"Maya was accurate."

"Then why sit through the part where I show you a hole you already feel sick about?"

"Because the hole is real either way. And because I wanted to know whether the person who read the note could talk about it without turning it into a personality. You can. I don't know if I can do the job. I know I don't want to leave the conversation in the state where I only defended."

She seemed to accept that, not as a compliment to herself, which he had been careful not to construct, but as a reason that did not insult her time. She shifted. "Reactivation. I read it as a drip. A nudge to keep a client paying."

"That's the firm's word when the firm is being honest about money. The message is: this file has a date, the date is close, a person has to do a lawful next thing or the file goes bad. Retention is what Martin calls it when he wants me to send more of them. I send them when the rule says to. I don't send them because a week has been quiet."

"Our sales lead will call the equivalent retention if you ever sit in a meeting here. Greg says the word with his whole chest. The firms hear it and relax, because retention sounds like care. Can you live with a word you didn't choose?"

He did not answer at once. The poster in the lobby had been a word he didn't choose, and he had enjoyed despising it, and the enjoyment was still in him, small and available. This was a different use of the same muscle. "In the meeting, yes. If the word starts deciding what gets stored, I would fight the ticket. I might be unpleasant about it. I can try to hear what he means and still keep the filing true. That's the version I think I could do. The version where his word is the requirement, I wouldn't do well. I wouldn't miss by accident." He heard the neatness of the last sentence and let it stand, because pulling it back would take longer than the thing was worth. "I should tell you I walked in with the wrong stakes. I was ready to treat your filings as if a wrong status here were the same as a wrong status on a permit. A missed GST filing can wreck a firm. It isn't the same wreck. I don't want to correct myself so hard that I make your users into paperwork."

Priya's eyebrows moved, a small concession to surprise. "Who told you to say the second half?"

"Nobody. You looked like you were about to let me be humble at their expense. I do that. I make a correction into a performance of fairness and the original people disappear. Sabrina has told me. She didn't use those words."

"What words did she use?"

He remembered them with an accuracy that was a little merciless. They had been in the office above the dentistry, the first month, an afternoon with the heat too high and the smell of disinfectant coming up through the floor. Sabrina had a spreadsheet open. The red rows were not, as he had assumed, the rows with the nearest dates. He had asked. She had said the red was the client's mood. She had nowhere else to put it, and if she did not put it somewhere she would walk into the call already wrong. He had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when a model failed, and she had mistaken the quiet for judgment.

"I'm not a column," she had said. "Don't look at me like I'm a column you haven't added yet."

He had not been looking at her like that, he had thought, and then had admitted that he had been, that the mood was already becoming a field in his head, nullable, enumerated, a thing he could store. He had said, "You're right. I was about to make your afternoon into a type." She had laughed once, unwillingly, and had said, "Make the type later. Right now tell me what you need me to show you so tomorrow morning isn't a scavenger hunt." They had worked for two hours. He had not made the mood into a column. He had made a list called call before noon that she could order herself, and he had left the ordering outside the database on purpose, and he had told her why. She had trusted him more after that than the incident deserved, and less than he had wanted.

"She said she wasn't a column," he told Priya. "I had started designing one."

Priya sat with it. "That's the part she didn't show me."

"What did she show you?"

"The refusal. The double send. The write-up. She said you wouldn't build Martin a private lie. She didn't say anything about moods or mornings. She wanted the recommendation to be the hard part. She was protecting it from sounding like affection."

"It isn't affection," Wale said, and then heard the brutality of the speed. "She doesn't have to feel warmly about me for the recommendation to be real. She was the one on the phones. That's a kind of knowledge. It doesn't require her to want me in the building."

"She may not want you in the building," Priya said. "Have you considered that?"

"Yes."

"And you came anyway."

"I came to talk to you. Whether she wants me hired is a separate want. I shouldn't make her responsible for it by staying home."

Priya rubbed the burn on her thumb, the one from the microwave, though he did not know its origin and saw only a small healed shine. "There's a thing I have to tell you before we go further. I saw the thread. Not the clean note. The forward, the one she asked me to delete. I read it standing up, and then I deleted it, and I remembered it anyway. I should have stopped at the first paragraph and waited. I didn't, because I have been burned by sanitized stories and I wanted to know if the write-up was a reconstruction. It wasn't. It matched. I still shouldn't have had the name."

The name arrived a second later, when he asked, though he already knew. "Which name?"

"Daniel. The one who wrote back in capitals. Sabrina's reply had it. Your clean note did not."

He heard the stairwell, the lowercase call, the nine minutes, the concrete. He heard his own voice on the Sunday: Don't send her the thread. The anger was not complicated at first. It was hot and narrow and had Sabrina's face on it, the face in the hallway withholding something, the glass of water. Under it, almost immediately, was the other fact: he had not asked, on Sunday, what she had already sent. He had accepted a promise about the future and called it a description of the past. And under that, Priya, who had used a client's fear as a verification method and was now putting the method on the table because she did not want it between them unspoken. He saw all three. Seeing them did not cool the first.

"The note I agreed to didn't have her answer in it," he said. "You know more than I agreed to show. I'm angry. I'm not going to pretend the anger is only about procedure. A person wrote back frightened and his name walked into a company he has never heard of because two of us wanted to be believed."

"Two."

"Sabrina, and then you, for reading it. I don't put myself out of it. I wrote the night mail with the names in the thread. I knew where that mail lived."

Priya's jaw moved. "You can say it plainer if you want to take a piece out of me. I'd rather that than the balanced version."

"I don't want a piece," he said. "I want the name out of any note you give Maya or Helen. I want you not to repeat the situation to Greg or to anyone who sells. And I want to keep going, with you knowing that I know. If I stop the interview I'll be punishing the conversation for a fact, and I'll also be making a gesture Sabrina has to live inside tomorrow. I don't want the gesture. I do want the name gone."

"If you want to stop, we stop," Priya said. She said it as a test. He heard the test. She meant it and she also wanted to see whether his fairness was a position he could spend.

"I don't want to stop. I want what I said."

She held his look. The distrust did not leave her face all the way. A candidate who has been handed a moral advantage and declines to use it is not, by declining, clean. He may be saving the advantage for later. She seemed to be running that possibility to its end. "It isn't in the notes," she said. "I didn't write the name. I didn't tell Maya. Helen will hear about the idempotency window and the single worker and the migration. She will not hear Daniel."

Her eyes went to the notebook Maya had left and away. The notebook was Maya's, not hers. He saw the glance and the uncertainty it opened, a narrow one: she might be telling the truth in the only way truth can be told when the other person cannot audit it. He chose to believe her. The choice was not proof. He made it because the alternative was to sit in a room and conduct an investigation he had no authority to conduct, and because her face, when she said Helen will not hear, had the ugliness of a person repeating a promise they have already broken once in a smaller way and do not intend to break again. Ugliness of that kind was more trustworthy to him than a smooth denial would have been. He might be a fool for the preference. He noted it and stayed.

"Then we keep going," he said.

The air did not clear. It changed jobs. He was aware of his own hands. She was aware, he thought, of the notebook she had not written the name in, and of the possibility that he was storing the advantage. Neither of them said so again.

Priya turned the laptop around. On the screen was not his schema but a trace from the morning: a row id, a transaction that had been open for minutes, a stack sitting inside a function named fulfillObligation, and a second session waiting on the lock. "This is what Tomás killed. Don't perform an interview answer. Tell me what you see. If you don't know our code, say what you would need."

He read. The letters were small and his pulse was still in his eyes from the name, and he waited until the pulse receded rather than narrating through it. "Something is holding the row and not finishing. The waiter isn't the interesting one. The holder is. I don't know your claim query. If I guess, I'll guess my bug and miss yours. The stack is in fulfill. If fulfill talks to a machine on someone's desk, I'd want to know whether that call sits inside the transaction that has the lock. If it does, the row stays hostage until the machine answers. If it doesn't, this trace is a different incident and I shouldn't pretend I can see it."

"It sits inside," she said. "Tomás pulled the call out last winter. I put it back in last month because we were seeing partial writes and I got superstitious. My superstition reopened the lock. He told me it would. I did it anyway, on a Thursday, because Greg had a firm that would walk if onboarding slipped into this week. This morning the partial write didn't happen. The lock did. A session sat in fulfill until the desktop agent stopped answering, and every claim behind it waited. Tomás cancelled the backend. He was right in the channel, in front of three people, and he was not kind, and he was correct."

"Are you going to take it back out?"

"Yes. Tonight. Not as a penance. As a repair. The penance would be leaving it so I have to feel serious." She heard herself and made a face that was almost distaste, directed inward. "You can say it. The founder impulse to confess in production."

"I sent a confession at one in the morning," he said. "You already know the reply. I don't think you're doing that. I think you're telling me the part of the morning that explains the eighteen minutes, and also testing whether I'll use Tomás as a club. I won't. He isn't here. He sounds right about the lock. He also sounds like he wanted the correctness to arrive with a little humiliation on it. Both can be true. I don't know him."

"You don't," she said. "And you don't get to be generous about him at my expense if you haven't sat in the channel. Take the point back."

He felt the justice of it, and the sting, which was the part of his pride that had enjoyed sounding fair. "All right. I take it back. He was correct about the lock. The rest was me arranging a person I haven't met."

"Better." She took the laptop back. The better was not warm. It was a gate opening a small distance. "Your tenancy. Firm id on the rows, application checks, no separate database. What stops me, if I am a bug, from reading Larkin Reid while I am Hale?"

"A query that forgets the predicate. I have a linter rule and a test that fails if a repository method takes a case id and not a firm id. The test is not a proof. A raw query in a script will walk past it. I ran a script like that once, in a console, with the firm id in my head and not in the SQL. It was a read. It was still wrong. I deleted the script and put the console behind a wrapper that requires the firm. The wrapper is annoyance. Annoyance is the control."

"Would you bet Martin's files on the wrapper?"

"I bet them on it every day. Saying it that way makes me want to split the databases. I haven't, because two small firms and a split I can get wrong in the backup story might be a worse bet. I don't know where your line is. You have more firms than I do. If your line is already separate databases, then my wrapper is a student's answer and I should hear that."

"Our line is a shared database and a policy we fail at twice a year," she said. "I am not going to pretend the failures are pedagogical. Last spring an export included one foreign firm because a new hire joined on firm name and the names collided. No one left. Someone could have. I told you your tenancy was a prayer. Ours is a prayer with a longer liturgy."

He recognized the offer in that, the refusal to stand above him on a point where she was exposed, and he did not leap to equalize. Leaping would have turned her spring into his consolation. "Did the new hire stay?"

"She stayed. She is good. She tried to resign in my office and I would not take it. The join was hers. The review that let a raw export into the worker was mine. If I had taken the resignation I would have kept the review and lost the person who will not make the join again." Priya looked at the blind window. "You would have taken it, maybe. You have a taste for consequences landing where the hand was."

"I might have," he said. "And then I would have been wrong. The hand was in the review too. I like consequences when they confirm a diagram. The diagram is usually smaller than the fault."

She made a short sound that was not quite a laugh. It seemed to surprise her. "Helen will ask me if you can be in a room where you are not the author. What do I say?"

"Say you don't know yet. Say I took a correction on the monitor and a correction on Tomás and that I was slower to take a compliment than a hole. If you say I'll be easy, you'll be selling her something I didn't demonstrate."

"I wasn't going to say easy." She glanced at the time. The glance was the first ordinary office gesture she had made, and it returned them, partially, to a building with other meetings in it. "Ask me what you actually want to ask. Not the stack. I will not enjoy reciting the stack."

He had questions he had prepared and discarded: how many firms, what the on-call rotation was, whether the degree would be held against him at the offer. Under those was the one that had been in him since Sabrina said the word req. "When a firm wants an exception, who is allowed to say no, and what happens to them after they say it?"

Priya turned the mug. "Greg is allowed to want. I am allowed to say no. Helen is allowed to overrule me if the no costs a renewal she has decided we cannot lose. After I say no, Greg is cold for a week and then he brings the same request in a smaller coat. After Helen overrules me, I write the exception as a named rule with an owner and an expiry, or I don't write it. The one time I wrote it without the expiry, the column is still there. It is called courtesy_extension. There is nothing courteous about it. It suppresses a filing warning for a firm whose partner golfs with someone. I argued. I lost. I shipped it. Every quarter I open the list of suppressed warnings and there it is, and I remember that I know how to lose and keep the system alive, and also that alive is not the same as clean."

"Do you want it gone?"

"Yes. I will not sneak it out. Sneaking would prove I can't lose. The day Helen agrees, it goes. If you are the sort of person who would delete it on a Friday and call it hygiene, say so."

"I wouldn't," he said. "I would hate it out loud. I might hate it too often. That's the failure I can already see from here."

"Hating it too often is survivable. Deleting it is not." She nodded, more to herself than to him. "Second question. You have another one. Your face has a queue."

The observation was accurate enough that he disliked it and then let the dislike pass. "On the Monday Tomás is gone, what is true that is not true now?"

"The desktop ingestion will have one person who has read the whole path, and that person will be me, and I will be bad at it by Wednesday. The poison rows will sit longer. Greg will promise a date I would not promise. Sabrina's implementations will stall in the places where she needs a mapping changed, and she will be polite for three days and then she will come to my desk with a look I have learned." Priya said Sabrina's name without softness. "We are hiring because I said I will not be the only one who can reason about this. People heard that as a request for relief. It was. It was also a request for a witness. I am tired of being believed only when the incident agrees with me."

There it was, the thing he had almost named in the hallway in his own head and had not earned the right to name: that she was protecting herself from another hire who would increase the review burden, and that she was also asking not to be alone with the correctness. She had said it herself. He did not improve it. He did not say you want a witness in a tone that would have made him the author of her motive. He said, "A witness who can overrule you, or a witness who can only agree?"

"If they can only agree, I have hired a mirror. Mirrors are how courtesy_extension survives." She looked at his hands, which were quiet, and at the phone he had placed face down on the table without thinking when he sat, the library habit, absurd in a room where no one had asked him to be silent. "You put that face down as if the table had a rule. There isn't one."

"I know. It's left over from a carrel."

"Leave it if you want. I don't mind a person with a leftover." She closed the laptop. "I have to put the lock back tonight. I am not going to pretend this conversation ends with a signal. Helen will read what I write. I am going to write that you know where your system is thin, that the second worker was in your mouth as soon as I pointed at it, that the stale read took you a silence, that you were precise about a client's manager without making him a fool, and that you caught me with a name I should not have had and did not spend it. I am also going to write that you have never had a reviewer, that you are not sure you want the work, and that I do not know whether you will be bored here in four months when the exceptions are duller than a permit. If that last line keeps you out, it keeps you out honestly."

"Bored is possible," he said. "I get bored when the fear goes and the correctness remains. The boredom is a defect. It makes me reach for a refinement nobody asked for. You should write that too, if you write the rest."

"I'll write it." She stood. The mug stayed, ring and all. "Come see the floor on your way out if you want. Don't if it feels like a tour designed to create attachment. I don't have the energy for attachment today."

"I'll see it," he said. "I won't pretend it's a reason."

The floor was a rectangle of desks and a window looking onto a brick wall ten feet away, so the light was borrowed. Someone had written deadlines on a whiteboard in a colour that was almost gone. Sabrina was on a call at the far end, headset on, her free hand opening and closing once as she searched for a word the person on the line would accept. She saw him and did not wave. The not-wave was an agreement, made across the room, that she would not convert him into her candidate in front of the people she had to work with at one o'clock. He gave her the same. Priya walked him as far as the kitchen and then stopped, because further would have been hosting.

"The oranges are decorative," Priya said, seeing him see them.

"Denise mentioned."

"Denise is right about more than the oranges. She told me this morning that if I was going to be late I should say the number of minutes and not a mood. I said a mood anyway, to you, at the door. Eighteen was the number. The mood was extra. You didn't soothe it. That was correct." She held the stairwell door, though he was going down by the elevator. The gesture was slightly incoherent and she seemed to notice and to be too tired to repair it. "If you look at the view tonight and you find I was wrong about the stale read, write me. I would rather be wrong in the email than right in your manners."

"If you're wrong I'll say so," he said. "I don't think you are."

"Neither do I. That's not the same as a proof."

Denise, at the desk, had him sign a line that said he had returned the visitor sticker. He had not taken it off. He peeled it and gave it to her and she stuck it to a sheet already pebbled with other names, a small archaeology of mornings. She spelled nothing wrong on the sheet because he had spelled it for her once and she had kept it. He thanked her for the water that had been Maya's. Denise accepted the thanks without sorting the error, which was its own courtesy.

On Peter Street the scaffold made a cold shade. The courier was gone. A restaurant vent blew a smell of oil and lime onto the sidewalk, and he stood in it longer than a person with a destination stands anywhere. His phone, turned over at last, had a message from Sabrina: She asked me if you always talk like that.

He typed Like what and deleted it. He typed Did you forward her the thread or only the note and deleted that too, because the sidewalk was the wrong room for it, the same wrongness as one in the morning, a blow delivered to a person who was still inside on a headset. He wrote: I'm out. Can I call you tonight? She answered before he reached the corner. I'm in implementations until six. Was it bad? He stopped under the scaffold where the shade was cleanest. He wrote: It was a real conversation. I need to ask you something that isn't bad and isn't nothing. He sent it. He put the phone in his pocket before the reply could make him rewrite the sentence into something easier for her to carry.

The streetcar west was half empty. He took a seat this time. The folded paper was still in his pocket, the instruction on the inside, unread since the morning. He did not open it. Priya had read the note and had also, despite Sabrina's warning, read a piece of him he had not submitted: the nine minutes, the capitals, the flat voice. She had not skipped. She had gone through a door he had meant to keep shut, and then she had stood in the doorway and said so. He tried to decide whether that was the same as being read. It was not. It was adjacent. The difference mattered and he could not yet hold it steadily.

What he could hold, and what disturbed him more than Daniel's name for a moment, was the lock she had put back in fulfillObligation because a firm might walk. He could see the function as she had described it, the network call sitting again inside the transaction, the row held while a machine on a desk stopped answering, Tomás in a channel being correct and unkind. He could see the repair he would have reached for first, which was not hers: not the removal, but a test that failed if a repository method performed IO inside a unit of work that held a row lock. He would have written the test before the argument. The test would not have solved Greg. It would have made the superstition visible the next time someone reached for it. He thought about the test with a pleasure that was immediate and technical and almost tender, the way the man with the pastry had been tender toward his coat. The pleasure did not ask him whether he wanted a salary or whether Martin was behind or whether the degree was paused. It simply began.

He recognized it, on the seat by the window, as the first moment of the day in which he wanted their problem. Not the proof that a person had read him. The problem. The wanting made him uneasy in a way the interview had not, because the firms were still on the server at home, and Hale's late month was still late, and a monitor job he now knew could read a stale row would run at four whether or not he had enjoyed a thought on a streetcar. To begin designing Northspan's test on a Queen car toward Roncesvalles was a kind of absence. He did not scold himself for it out loud in his head. Scolding would have been another way of feeling serious. He put his hand on the pocket with the phone and did not take the phone out. Sabrina would finish implementations at six. He would call. He would ask about the thread, and he would also have to tell her that he had repeated, to Priya, the sentence about the column. He had not asked whether that sentence was his to carry into the room. If he demanded an accounting from Sabrina and left his own out, the call would be the diagram again, small and flattering.

At a red light the car paused beside a dentist's office with a faded sign, not the one under Hale, a different dentistry, and the smell of the vent from the restaurant was gone. He was tired. The schema comment in Priya's small hand, the word courtesy_extension, the single worker he had been content to call a design: these stayed. He would look at the view tonight because it was his. He would not write Priya until he had looked. The streetcar bell rang once. He stayed on past the stop where he sometimes got off to walk, because walking would have given him time to compose the call, and he did not want the call composed.
