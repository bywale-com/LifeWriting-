# LifeWriting Character Discovery — “The Interview”

You are writing one distinct version of a substantial chapter titled:

**The Interview**

This prompt produces a new chapter. It is not an instruction to revise `the-interview-01.md` or any other existing draft. Do not copy those drafts’ companies, recommenders, incidents, or dialogue. Write a new story.

The purpose is Character Discovery through fiction.

We are not yet defining the character through abstract traits. We are placing him into a sufficiently detailed real-world situation and observing what kind of person naturally emerges when every action, reaction, relationship, and consequence must make sense.

The primary problem this version must solve:

«The protagonist must not feel flat. Make him feel alive.»

Do not achieve that by making him a comedian, a social performer, a conventionally charismatic figure, or the center of attention. Do not change the core of him merely to make the prose more entertaining. Give greater resolution to temperament, affect, rhythm, reactions, and interpersonal texture.

By the end, a reader should be able to answer more than “What does this person believe?” They should have an emerging answer to “What does it feel like to spend an hour with him?”

---

## Literary method

Write this as serious psychological and social realism.

Borrow heavily from the narrative methods of Dostoevsky and Tolstoy, particularly:

- psychological specificity;
- social observation;
- apparently minor details that reveal character;
- contradictions inside people;
- realistic conversational rhythms;
- characters misunderstanding one another;
- thoughts that are not perfectly articulated even to the person thinking them;
- gestures, pauses, glances, changes in tone, embarrassment, restraint, and social calculation;
- causal continuity between past events and present behavior.

Reopen the relevant past when the present depends upon it.

In the middle of a present event, return briefly to something that happened hours, weeks, or years earlier. Do not merely say “they had met before.” Give exactly enough of the prior encounter, conversation, impression, misunderstanding, or decision for the present moment to become psychologically inevitable.

Use that technique whenever the scene raises a causal question: how the friendship formed, why this person would recommend him, what the staff member had already seen, why the staff member became interested, why an interviewer already has an impression, why he answers this way rather than another. Do not hand-wave. Interrupt the present, show the prior incident, and return.

Nothing important should feel as though the author simply needed it to happen.

Prefer scene to explanation. Whenever possible: scene first, interpretation second, or no interpretation at all. Trust dialogue, gesture, contradiction, timing, and other people’s reactions. The reader should occasionally understand something about him before he or the narrator explains it.

Do not have him consciously audit himself every few seconds. Genuine self-awareness remains. Some of it should already operate beneath conscious thought. Sometimes he simply reacts. Sometimes only the narrator sees the contradiction. Sometimes another person notices something about him before he does. Sometimes he discovers what he felt several minutes later. We want a man whose character is beginning to function, not a man constantly constructing himself in the narration.

---

## What it is like to be around him

At every major interaction, ask: what is it actually like to be around this person?

The reader should gradually be able to imagine his voice, conversational pace, facial reactions, timing, how quickly he answers, when he pauses, what makes him visibly interested, what makes him impatient, what amuses him, what surprises him, what embarrasses him, how irritation appears, how enthusiasm appears, how warmth appears, whether he becomes animated when he understands something deeply, and whether his directness lands dryly, gently, intensely, curiously, matter-of-factly, or changes with the moment.

Do not decide in advance that he is warm, cold, dry, intense, playful, restrained, or animated. Discover the combination through the scene.

His lines should not all come from the same emotional temperature. The factual content of a correction can stay the same while the person changes with delivery: almost at once, after a frown, after a laugh of surprise, after a stop, after visible irritation, after care because the sentence could wound. Do not attach an adverb to every line. Let action, rhythm, silence, gaze, posture, and context carry tone.

Restraint is not flatness. A restrained person can still be specific. If he suppresses a reaction, show enough of it that we know what was suppressed. If he says little, let us know whether the quiet comes from thought, annoyance, uncertainty, amusement, disinterest, restraint, embarrassment, judgment, or genuine listening. Quiet is not a personality. Give the quiet a cause.

Different people should bring out different expressions of the same person. He should not speak to everyone with the same cadence. With a technically serious interviewer, one aspect. With a recruiter, another. With someone technically stronger, another. With somebody he immediately likes, another. With somebody whose manner irritates him, another. With somebody who misunderstands him, another. The person stays stable. The expression changes.

## Canonical constraints

Do not contradict these.

He is not primarily:

- the clown;
- the funniest person in the room;
- the habitual center of attention;
- a social performer;
- someone whose social effectiveness depends on conventional magnetism or on people automatically flocking toward him.

Humor and wit can exist. They are not his central social mechanism.

An orientation already under investigation, to be shown rather than lectured:

He may possess an unusually low degree of contempt toward other people. He may recognize vanity, deceit, insecurity, manipulation, weakness, embarrassment, posturing, or moral failure without experiencing that recognition as proof that he is above the person.

A possible underlying conviction:

«Understanding someone's motives does not place me above them. I am made of the same human material.»

This may let him say something socially uncomfortable, or name what is plainly happening, without the ordinary desire to humiliate, dominate, score a point, or establish superiority.

Do not turn this into saintliness. Do not make him naïve. Do not assume this principle is the whole character. The chapter exists partly to discover what else must be true about him.

Earlier drafting made the following recognizable. Keep them recognizable. Do not soften them. The task is to discover what kind of living person carries them:

- unusually direct candor;
- non-contemptuous truthfulness;
- willingness to correct and be corrected without turning either into a status contest;
- strong technical perception;
- the occasional sense that he understands what another person is doing or thinking before they explicitly say it;
- intellectual honesty;
- capacity to notice when he has overstated, distorted, or managed an impression;
- refusal to exploit another person's weakness merely to make himself look better;
- technical expertise becoming evident through conversation rather than through boasting.

### Candor has more than one sound

Not every direct statement should sound identical. Sometimes he may be gently matter-of-fact, almost casually blunt, reluctant but unwilling to lie, amused that everyone is avoiding the obvious, slightly irritated, genuinely confused that something is being treated as unsayable, careful because he knows the statement could wound, or immediate because hesitation would itself feel dishonest.

He can be sharp. He can be angry. He can dislike something. He can say that another person is behaving badly. He does not need to make the other person smaller in order to say it. Understanding their motives does not place him above them. The directness should not carry the pleasure of exposure.

### Expertise should change how he behaves

When the talk moves into something he knows deeply, let that knowledge alter pace, exactness, animation, impatience with vague language, the reach for a pen, an unexpectedly specific question, an interruption he then notices, pleasure at a difficult objection, or a drop in social self-consciousness because the problem is actually interesting.

Do not announce that he is technically strong. Let another person realize “he actually sees this” from a question, a correction, a distinction, or a prediction.

### Ordinary preferences

He is not made entirely of moral principles. Let small preferences appear where the scene earns them: what kind of conversation bores him, what kind suddenly interests him, whether formal rooms suit him, whether corporate euphemisms irritate or amuse him, whether he wants coffee, whether he notices a building, whether waiting costs him something, whether he sits still, whether he reads while waiting, whether he looks out a window, whether he enjoys technical disagreement, whether he grows restless when someone takes too long to reach the point.

Do not insert random quirks to make him unique.

### Mistakes should have a temperament

When he is wrong, do not make every response perfectly composed. He might laugh at himself, wince, get briefly defensive and then catch it, become annoyed, go quiet, ask for a moment, argue one step too far, feel embarrassment physically, or become intensely curious about the correction. The response should fit the person the scene is discovering. It does not need to be admirable.

## Role context

The professional direction is already established.

He is developing toward software and product engineering with a deep backend and systems spine, while building and operating products through Omcoda. He lives in the Greater Toronto Area. Do not contradict that.

His evidence can include live products, real users, production systems, backend and domain modeling, APIs, databases, migrations, asynchronous jobs, operational failures, incident handling, customer discovery, product decisions, deployment, technical artifacts, and independent commercial work through Omcoda.

Do not treat him like a generic new graduate whose primary evidence is coursework.

The interview opportunity should make sense given this background.

## Canonical scenario

He receives an interview. It does not begin with a cold application. He was recommended. There are effectively two stages of recommendation.

At some earlier point, a friend or acquaintance brings him to the attention of a staff member at the company.

Determine:

- Who is the friend or acquaintance?
- How did that relationship form?
- Why would this person think of him for this particular opportunity?
- What exactly did they say to the staff member?
- What did they show them?
- What did they choose not to say?
- Was the recommendation casual, deliberate, enthusiastic, reluctant, opportunistic, or something else?

The staff member then sees enough to become interested.

Determine:

- Who is this staff member?
- What is their role?
- What company is this?
- What exact job could plausibly be available?
- What did they see in his work?
- Which parts were technically impressive independent of personality?
- Which parts created questions?
- Did they inspect Omcoda, a repository, a production system, an incident write-up, a product, an architectural decision, evidence of users or revenue, or something else?

Why did the staff member act? They might only have forwarded a name. They might have told recruiting to take a look. They might have asked for the interview themselves. A polite concession to the recommender might have changed after they inspected the work. They might have become curious enough to want the meeting. The truth might be more complicated. Decide, and make the causal chain credible.

## The interview

Write the actual interview.

He may encounter both of the following.

### A grounded technical person

This person speaks normally. Little corporate language. No condescension. They can be candid, informal, curious, and conversational. They leave enough space that his actual personality can become visible. This might be the staff engineer who received the recommendation, an engineering manager, a product engineer, a founder, or another technically serious employee.

The conversation should not merely be a technical quiz. Let them become curious about the person. Let things occur that neither anticipated. If this person is technically stronger, let them be stronger.

### A conventional recruiter or talent person

This person uses a more recognizable interview structure: expected questions, recruiter language, standardized prompts, resume framing, behavioral questions, compensation, logistics. The interview must still reveal something. He cannot rely on comedy, conventional charm, or magnetic performance.

Both interviewers leave with a strong impression. The technical interviewer should experience it more directly. The recruiter may not fully understand what they encountered, but should have concrete reasons for remembering him.

Do not tell us he had a unique presence. Show what happened that makes that sentence unnecessary.

## Pressure surfaces

Do not answer these as an essay, and do not mechanically include every one. Use whichever generate the most revealing chapter.

How does he enter the building? What happens at reception? What is he doing while waiting? What does he notice? Does he talk to anyone? How does he sit? What happens when someone arrives late? How does he respond to superficial conversation? Does he perform professionalism, or simply behave professionally? What happens when he does not know something? What happens when the interviewer misunderstands the work? What if the interviewer is technically wrong? What if they notice a genuine weakness? What if he realizes midway through an answer that he was wrong? What happens when the independent work is treated skeptically? What happens when the interviewer is clearly impressed? How does he receive praise? How does he discuss Omcoda without grandiosity? How does he describe something built with assistance from a model, and how does he distinguish what he understands from what the tooling produced? What happens in silence? What happens if the interviewer becomes unusually candid? What does he ask when given the floor? What is he genuinely curious about? How does he speak about former clients, colleagues, classmates, competitors, or people whose work he dislikes? How does he discuss failure? Can he say something very direct without making the room adversarial? Does an interviewer realize that he has noticed something they hoped would remain implicit? If so, what happens?

Where a belief matters, put it somewhere it costs something, then show what he does. Possible areas: enduring embarrassment, conflict, hierarchy, criticism, other people’s motives, disagreement, candor, insecurity, ambition, self-respect, curiosity, uncertainty, intellectual honesty. Do not state the belief and move on.

## Epistemic integrity

If the reader would ask “why would that happen?”, give enough grounding that the question disappears.

Especially: the recommendation, the friend’s motives, how the friend knows him, what the staff member was shown, why the staff member cared, why this role is plausible, why recruiting agreed, why the staff member did or did not attend, what the interviewers knew beforehand, what they did not know, what they expected before he entered, and what changed during the interview.

These facts do not all need equal page space. When a fact becomes relevant, reopen the event that established it, give the necessary social and psychological detail, and return to the present.

Nothing should exist only to make him impressive. Other people have their own motives. They are not props.

He may misread something, answer badly, over-explain, become uncomfortable, discover ignorance, embarrass himself, annoy somebody, be judged unfairly, judge something incorrectly, recover well, fail to recover, impress one person and concern another. Real edges make the chapter more useful. Do not write competence as spectacle. Do not make every other person less intelligent so that he can look impressive. If an interviewer sees through something, let them.

## Freedom, length, and form

Outside the constraints above, determine the company, role, staff, recommender, relationships, sequence, questions, technical discussion, dialogue, memories, prior meetings, mistakes, tensions, and ending. You may introduce events from days, months, or years earlier if they explain the present. Every added fact should fit the established life rather than inventing an unrelated biography.

Write a substantial half-chapter of a serious novel: the recommendation history, the lead-up, the interview, substantial dialogue, psychological observation, retrospective scenes, changes in how the interviewers perceive him, and aftermath. Keep it long enough for people to happen. Do not pad. Do not add events merely to lengthen it.

Continuous literary prose only.

Do not include character-analysis headings, bullet points inside the chapter, explanations of your choices, trait summaries, a lesson at the end, or a separate Character Architecture analysis.

The artifact is the chapter.

**The Interview**
