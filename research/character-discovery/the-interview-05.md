The Interview

The woman at the desk had a printed list, a pencil, and a nameplate that read Megan. She said the time back to him before she said his name, as if the time were the part she trusted. “Two-fifteen. Wally Omotayo, for Danielle at Arbour.” She looked up on the second word, not unkindly, waiting to be corrected or thanked. He said thank you. The correction presented itself and he let it pass, and in the few steps from the desk to the chairs he felt the small, precise shame of having done that, which was out of proportion to the thing and which he recognized from other counters, other forms, other people who had needed a pronunciation they could reuse. He put his bag on the chair beside him rather than on the floor, where the rain from his shoes was already making a dark coin on the tile, and he sat with his coat still on because the lobby was colder than the street had been.

Arbour’s reception was on the fourth floor of a building on Duncan, though the chairs and the ficus looked rented from the building. He had read the posting twice on the streetcar. They sold obligation-tracking to accounting firms: filing dates, the state of an engagement, the chase that followed a client who had gone quiet. The kinship with Tower was obvious enough to be a trap, and he had decided, somewhere around Bathurst, not to say the word kinship in the room. A screen on a stand faced the elevator, cycling through the product no one in the chairs was using. He had been early by twenty minutes on purpose and by a further ten because the streetcar had not done what the app had threatened. On the ride he had held his phone in his hand without opening the queue. There was a question from Ruth Peng he had not answered, a document type her firm wanted added to a pathway, and he had drafted the reply at the kitchen table and deleted it, not because the answer was unclear but because he did not want to arrive in this building with the reply half-saved and his attention snagged on whether he had sounded short. Ruth would be at her desk until five. The letters were not going out this afternoon. He had checked the send ledger before he locked the door.

The screen changed. A pale interface, a list of clients, a status in a pill the colour of a legal pad: Ready for review. Under it, in smaller type, a date and a name. The next slide was the same screen with a different name and the same pill. He watched it go by twice. The phrase could mean the preparer had finished, or that the client had uploaded what was asked for, or that some clock had run out and a person was now expected to look. Nothing on the slide said which. He felt the small forward pitch of having seen a thing, the wish to be useful with it, and then the immediate correction, which was that he was sitting in a lobby under a demonstration loop and nobody had asked him. He kept the observation anyway, the way he kept Ruth’s question, in a place that was not his mouth.

A man came out of the elevator with a visitor sticker already curling off his sweater and the look of someone who has remembered, too late, a sentence he should have said in the room. He nodded at the receptionist without stopping. Wale did not try to construct a story about how the man’s interview had gone. People wore that face when they had forgotten to validate their parking.

He was still in the coat when Danielle Cho came down, seven minutes past the time on the list. She apologized with the efficiency of a person who apologizes often and means the apology to end the subject. “Wale? Did I say that close enough?” She had looked it up, or someone had told her. Wah-lay, with the stress pulled forward.

“Close,” he said. “Wale. Like wallet, without the t. The rest can be Omotayo if you need it, or not.”

“Wale,” she repeated, and got it, and seemed glad to have got it, which made the earlier failure at the desk more his own. “I’m Danielle. Sorry to be late. Helen is coming from a call that ran. We’ll start and she’ll join.” She said Helen as if he would know the weight of the name. He did, though not from her.

In the elevator she asked whether he had found the building all right, and he said he had, and she asked whether he was still in the rain, and he said the rain had stopped on Queen. She nodded. There was a beat in which a different person might have offered something — a complaint about the King car, a small joke about the ficus — and he felt the beat, and let it close. She did not look offended. She looked as if a box had been left empty and she had already moved to the next box. He was grateful and slightly unsettled, which was the ordinary mixture in him when small talk died of its own accord and nobody was punished.

The room was glass on one side, looking into a lane where a delivery van had its flashers on. A whiteboard with nothing on it but the ghost of a diagram someone had erased badly, boxes and an arrow. Danielle set a laptop down and a notebook, paper, which he had not expected. She offered water. He took it. His hands went around the cup because they needed a place, and he noticed himself doing it, and left them there.

“I’ll give you the shape,” she said. “Twenty-five minutes with me, then Helen Ivers for the rest of the hour. She’s the staff engineer on Obligations. The role is product engineer on that team. Martin — Martin Albrecht, who’d be your director — isn’t in today. He asked us to keep the first conversation to an hour.” She smiled in a way that was not entirely a smile. “He also asked me not to let this turn into a founder pitch. I don’t think that’s what you came to do. I’m saying it so it’s not sitting in the room unsaid.”

“All right,” Wale said.

“Great.” She looked at the notebook, not the laptop. “Walk me through what you actually do. I’ve got the site, and I’ve got a paragraph from Helen, and I want the version you say out loud.”

He had prepared a version on the streetcar and abandoned it between Queen and King because it had started to sound like the site. The site said managed solutions, and it said they did not take briefs, and it had been written on a Sunday when he was angry at a man who had offered him forty thousand dollars to build a portal. The sentences were true and they were also a posture. He did not want the posture in this room.

“I run a company called Omcoda,” he said. “There’s one product. It’s called Tower. Immigration practices use it to watch files they already have — whether a permit window, or a program the person was going to qualify under, is still the window they thought it was. When it isn’t, the firm gets a queue. Some of those turn into letters to the client. Some don’t. There’s another side of it that brings back files the firm has gone quiet on, where the person paid and then everyone stopped writing. I operate it. I also write it. There isn’t a second engineer.”

Danielle wrote. “And the firms are your customers, not the families.”

“Yes.”

“How many firms?”

“Three. One in North York, one in Brampton, a smaller one in Mississauga. A bit over a thousand matters in the system. Fewer than that are being monitored every morning. The North York practice is the one that’s been on it longest.”

“Revenue?” She asked it plainly, which he preferred to the approach he had twice heard from people who said they hated to ask.

“It isn’t a salary,” he said. “The last three months, averaged, a little over four thousand a month. One of the three is late on the last invoice. I don’t know yet if that’s a delay or a leaving.”

She wrote the late invoice down. He saw her write it. He had not been asked for it. He had added it because leaving it off had a particular feeling, a feeling he had a name for only because he had been angry at someone else for a similar omission, and the anger was still recent enough to make him clumsy in the other direction. Danielle did not congratulate him on the candour. She said, “Okay,” and underlined something, and he understood that he had handed her a fact she would now have to carry up the building, where it would not help him, and that she was a person whose job was carrying facts up buildings. He felt a brief, unwelcome warmth toward her for not performing delight, and at the same time a calculation he disliked: if he was going to give her hard facts, he should also give her sentences she could use. The two impulses arrived together. He did not sort them.

“The site makes it sound larger,” she said.

“The site is doing a job it isn’t very good at.”

She glanced up. “Whose sentences are they?”

“Mine.”

“All right.” She turned a page. “Helen said you were recommended. I have Farid Mansouri in the notes. How do you know him?”

He looked at the cup. Farid was the reason the hour existed, and Farid was also the reason he had almost written to Helen to cancel it, on the night the emails went around, before he understood that cancelling would have been a way of punishing Farid and not a way of protecting anyone.

Farid had been in the back room at Elise Navarro’s office on a Thursday in February, a room with a window onto a parking lot and a printer that jammed when you asked it for more than ten pages. He was not there for Tower. He was there because Elise’s bookkeeper had quit and the bank feed had been wrong since November, and Farid Mansouri, who did books for a handful of small professional firms and sometimes told people he “did systems” when he meant spreadsheets with opinions, had been hired for a month. Wale was there because the morning job had spiked, and he had come in person rather than send a note, which was a rule he had made after the first time a flag was wrong and he had tried to explain it by email while Ruth was already on the phone.

The job had applied a rule version published that morning to facts that belonged to a period ending the month before. The valid time of the new rule started that day. The files did not. Twenty-two matters came up as if their windows had closed. Ruth Peng, who had been a caseworker for eleven years and did not trust software as a class, had trusted this queue because it had been dull and right for weeks, which is the only way a queue earns trust. She had called one client before Wale reached the office. She had not sent the letters. The letters were still drafts. He had frozen the queue from his phone on the streetcar, thumb wet on the glass. At seven-forty, at his own table, he had hovered over a different fix: exclude that program from the outbound job, let the run go green, talk about it later. He had not done it. He still remembered the wanting, which had been physical, a wish for the red row to become a quiet row before Elise read it.

In the back room Ruth would not sit. Elise stood with her coat still on and said, “If I cannot trust the flag, I cannot use you.” She did not raise her voice. Farid, at the side table, had a stack of statements and the particular stillness of a person who has understood he is going to hear something he was not hired to hear. Wale said the flag was wrong and the letters had not gone out. He did not say the system was, in general, right. He opened the twenty-two rows on Ruth’s monitor and they went through them. It took three hours. One of the matters was the man Ruth had called. They drafted, together, the second call, which was worse than the first because it had to be true. Farid said, once, not looking at Elise, “You could exclude that program from the emails. Just while you sort it.” Wale said, “That would hide the next one.” Elise looked at Farid as if he had offered to hide it from her. Farid’s ears went dark. Wale said, “It’s the first thing I wanted to do this morning.” Nobody thanked him for the sentence. Ruth made the second call from the hall. Elise paid the next invoice when it came, without a note. The trust was not repaired in the room. It was repaired, if that was the word, by the following month of flags being dull again, and by a constraint that made the illegal assessment fail instead of queue.

Farid had asked for the write-up in March. He said he wanted to understand what he had been sitting beside. Wale sent it. It was four pages, sanitized, with the firm unnamed and Ruth unnamed and the constraint named in full, because the constraint was the part that mattered: an exclusion on the matter and the valid-time range, so two assessments could not both claim the same stretch of a life. He had written, carefully, that the operator acted on a flag the system should not have produced. He had been proud of the care, which was a warning he did not hear.

Wale had the front half of this from Farid, later, on the phone, told defensively and, he thought, truly. A month in the year before last, Arbour’s finance team had hired Farid to reconcile a botched export of engagement statuses. Helen had been the engineer sent to say why his spreadsheet could not be loaded. Farid had mapped closed and dormant into one value because they looked the same in the cells. She had not written the finance director. She had sat with him for an hour, made him split them, and said, at the end, “Don’t send me a version that flatters the mess.” Farid had been ashamed, and grateful, and had experienced both as loyalty. In the spring one of his clients, a small accounting firm already on Arbour, received two notices about a year that was filed. Support replied with a paragraph about unexpected communication. Farid wrote to Helen instead of answering the paragraph. She called him from a stairwell — this was the detail Wale had almost not believed, and then had, because Farid was not inventive about rooms — and told him what the worker had actually done, more than support was allowed to say. She remembered the spreadsheet. She did not want the official sentence to be the only one in circulation. Farid did not fully understand the rollback. He understood that a notice had gone twice, and that she hated the wording she was supposed to use.

In August he read Wale’s note and recognized the family of the trouble, and recognized, with a feeling he would not have called opportunism and would not have been able to swear was not, a chance to hand Helen something that was not a complaint. He wrote to her. Wale saw the email only afterward, forwarded back to him with a defensive line at the top — this is all I said — because Wale had been short with him on the phone.

Helen,

A person I met through a client’s office. Not a pitch. He runs a system that monitors immigration files for a firm I was doing the books for. In February it raised a bad flag, the caseworker almost wrote to a bunch of clients, and he shut the queue off and sat with the rows. I attached the note he wrote.

I’m not saying hire him. The problem in the note looked like the one you told me about in the spring, the notices that went twice. He won’t sell himself. If you write to him he may answer slowly. He was restoring a database when I tried to talk to him and he would not leave it.

Farid

Farid had not said that he and Wale were friends. They had spoken four times. He had not said anything about a degree, which Wale did not have, or about the hour at seven-forty, or about the fear, which had been obvious to anyone looking at Wale’s hands on Ruth’s desk and which Farid, to his credit or his taste, had not turned into an anecdote. He had also not said that the caseworker had done the human thing. The email made Ruth the near-disaster and Wale the person who sat with the rows. The note itself was better than the email. The email was what a reader would remember.

Wale had sent a second message the next morning, to Helen, with Farid copied, against Farid’s request that he “not make it a thing.”

Helen,

Farid forwarded me what he sent you. One correction. The caseworker did what the flag asked her to do. The failure was that the flag existed. If the note reads otherwise, it reads wrong. I wrote the note, so the fault in the reading is mine to fix.

If you want the migrations I can send those. I will not send client data.

Wale Omotayo
Omcoda

Farid did not speak to him for six days. When he did, he said, “You could have told me I was wrong without putting me on the letter.” Wale said, “You put the caseworker on the letter first.” Farid said he had been trying to help. Both of these were true, and the truth of them did not make a friendship, and did not cancel the fact that Farid had, months earlier, watched a frightened person refuse a convenient lie and had wanted one particular engineer to see it. Helen’s reply had been a single line. Send the migrations. If he was willing, she would look at a sandbox. She had not mentioned the correction. She had not mentioned Farid.

“He was doing the books at a firm that uses Tower,” Wale said to Danielle. “He saw a bad morning. He knows Helen. He sent her a note I wrote about it. I sent her a correction of his email. That’s the whole relationship.”

Danielle’s pen stopped. “You corrected the recommendation.”

“Yes.”

“How did he take it?”

“Badly, and not without reason. I could have called him first. I didn’t, because I thought he would ask me not to send it, and I already knew I was going to send it, and I didn’t want the phone call where I pretended otherwise.”

She looked at him for a moment as if he had placed an object on the table that was not on her list and she had to decide whether it was hers. “That’s either going to read as high integrity or as someone who creates process problems. I’m not deciding which in this half hour.”

“All right.”

“Tell me about that morning anyway. The version you’d tell Helen. Short.”

He told it short. The rule’s valid time, the facts’ period, the twenty-two rows, the call already made, the letters not sent, the three hours, the constraint. He did not tell the seven-forty hover. He heard himself not telling it, and the not-telling sat next to the late invoice he had volunteered, and the two did not match. He was not consistent. He was a person who could add a stain in one place and keep one in another, and the choice of which was not a principle he could have recited. The hover shamed him more than the late invoice. That was the reason. It was not a good reason to use on himself and withhold from the room. He put his hands flat on his knees under the table, where she could not see them, and said, “Earlier, before I went to their office, I almost suppressed the program in the job so the run would go quiet. I didn’t. I’m telling you because I left it out of the version I just called the version.”

Danielle stared at the notebook. Then she wrote, longer than the sentence had been. “Why didn’t you?”

“Because the next run would have done it again, and Ruth would have been the one to find out, and she would have been right not to trust any of it after that.”

“Ruth is the caseworker.”

“Yes.”

“She doesn’t work for you.”

“No. She works for the firm. If she stops trusting the queue, the firm is right to stop paying, and the people in the files are back to whatever the firm can remember by hand.”

Danielle nodded once, the nod of a person fitting a story into a competency she had been told to listen for. “So you influenced the stakeholder without authority, and you prioritized the downstream user.”

He felt the sentence land a few degrees off the events. “Ruth isn’t a stakeholder I influenced. She was angry. Elise — the consultant — told me she couldn’t use the system. I agreed the flag was useless. We redid the morning. I don’t think I managed them. I think I owed them the rows.”

“In our language,” Danielle said, and she was not sarcastic, “that’s still a conflict with a customer. I’m trying to put it somewhere I can defend. If I write ‘he disagreed with the framing,’ Martin will ask me what the business outcome was.”

“The business outcome was that one client got a second phone call, twenty-one didn’t get a wrong letter, and the firm is still a customer. Elise didn’t feel managed. She felt exposed, and then she watched the thing get narrower.” He heard the slight heat in his own voice and pulled it down. “If the box needs a different sentence, say I stopped a send and stayed until the list was checked. That’s the part I can stand behind.”

“I’ll use both,” she said. “Mine and yours. He can ask.”

She asked why he wanted the job. He had known the question was coming and still found it difficult, because the true answer was not one thing. He wanted, badly, a person who could refuse a migration of his. He had never had that. He wanted an income that did not depend on Elise’s patience. He was afraid that if he came into a building like this, the morning check on Tower would become a thing he did with half his mind, and that Ruth would feel the halfness before he admitted it. He also wanted, in a way he did not like to look at directly, to know whether the work he had been doing was the same kind of work as the work in this room, or whether he had only been a careful man with a database and three firms. He did not say all of that. He said he wanted to work on a system where the state was the product, inside a team, on a domain that was not his alone, because the failures he knew how to see were the failures he had already caused. Danielle asked him what he would do with Omcoda. He said he would keep operating it, and that he would not pretend the morning check took nothing. She wrote that down with a face that said the sentence had made her job harder. He did not take it back.

She asked where he saw himself in five years. He said he did not know, and then, because the answer was too small for the trouble she had gone to, he said he expected to still be close to a system that could not be allowed to lie about time. She let the silence after that be short.

“Degree?” she said.

“I’ve started one. Part-time, evenings, Toronto Metropolitan. First term. It isn’t why you’d hire me. I can work in Canada without sponsorship.”

“The posting says equivalent experience.” She said it as information she was glad to have, and also as a line she would be asked to repeat. “I’ll note the enrolment. Some people upstairs treat ‘started’ as ‘doesn’t have.’ I won’t hide it, and I also won’t lead with it.”

“Thank you.”

She looked at the clock on her laptop. The hour had a seam in it. “I have one more that I hate, and I’m going to ask it because the form has a field and if I leave the field blank someone fills it in for you. What would you say is your superpower?”

He did not answer. The van in the lane shut its flashers off and pulled away. He could hear, through the glass, someone laughing in the kitchen across the hall, a laugh that had nothing to do with them. Danielle waited. She did not fill the silence, which raised his opinion of her at the exact moment he was about to disappoint her.

“I think the question wants a trait you can put in the paragraph,” he said. “I don’t have one that would be true on its own. If you need a sentence, I can try, and you can tell me if it’s the wrong kind of sentence.”

“The form has a box,” she said. Her ears had coloured, a small neat pink. She was embarrassed and also interested, and she was doing him the courtesy of not pretending she was only interested.

“Then,” he said, “I notice when a system is about to say something a person will act on. I don’t always notice it soon enough.”

She typed it. He watched the words become a line in a document he would not see, and he felt the grease of having shaped them for her, for the upstairs he had not met, for Martin who did not want a founder pitch. The sentence was still true. The shaping was also true. He did not add a joke to make the grease feel like ease. She looked relieved to have a line, and slightly as if she had been handled, which she had been, a little, by someone who had seen that she would have to walk this hour into another room. He saw that she saw it. Neither of them said so.

“Compensation,” she said, and the relief of a concrete subject entered her voice. “The band for this level is one twenty-five to one fifty-five, base, plus a small option grant I can explain if we get there. Where do you sit?”

“One thirty-five,” he said. “I don’t have a salary to anchor to. The retainers are the comparison, and they’re lumpy, and I’ve already told you the number. I don’t know how to price the options. If you explain them later I’ll have questions a person has on their first time. I won’t have a counter-offer.”

“That’s a clean answer,” she said, surprised enough that the surprise showed, and then annoyed at herself for the surprise. “People usually dance for a minute.”

“I don’t know the dance,” he said. “I’d rather not invent one in front of you.”

She closed the notebook. Behind him, the door opened.

Helen Ivers was older than the voice he had constructed for her from the one-line email. Forty, or a little past it. She had a lanyard she didn’t look at and a sweater with a pulled thread at the cuff, and she came in already talking, not rudely, the way people talk when they have left one conversation and the next one has started in their head on the stairs. “Sorry. The call was the firm in Hamilton who thinks a notice is a suggestion. Danielle, do you want to stay or are you escaping?”

“I’ll stay for five,” Danielle said. “Then I’ll let you.”

Helen sat. She looked at Wale once, directly, as a person looks at a machine they have already opened and are now seeing with the cover on. “You sent the migrations. I read them. I made a tenant in the sandbox on a Tuesday night and I tried to mark a matter eligible and not, in the same cycle. Your API told me no. It named the two assessment ids. That’s why you’re here, more than Farid is why you’re here. Farid got me to open the note.” She turned to Danielle. “He isn’t an AI immigration startup. I want that said in the room so your notes don’t grow a second product on the way upstairs.”

Danielle’s mouth tightened, not at Helen exactly, but at being spoken of as a route by which notes decayed. “I have him as Omcoda, one product, three firms. I do not have him as an AI company.”

“Good,” Helen said. She had not heard the tightness, or she had heard it and was already past it. “Wale, before we go further — Danielle, anything he should correct that I would otherwise inherit?”

It was a generous question and a dangerous one. Wale saw Danielle’s hand rest on the notebook. She had inherited, ten minutes ago, a correction of Farid that had cost a silence of six days. She was waiting to see if he would do it to her in front of a staff engineer.

“One thing,” he said. “If the summary says I’m looking to go in-house because the company is too small, that isn’t what I said. I said I want a review I can’t give myself, and that I would keep operating Tower, including the morning check. Danielle has the late invoice. That’s right. And the degree is started, not held.”

Danielle looked at him. Something in her face eased by a degree and did not become warmth. “That’s what I have.”

“Then I don’t need to fix it,” he said.

Helen nodded. “Stay as long as you want, Danielle. I’m going to talk about the constraint.”

Danielle stayed. Wale understood that she was staying now for a different reason than the five minutes. She had been almost summarized out of the interview, and she had decided not to be. He was glad. He also saw Helen notice the decision and file it somewhere. Helen’s face did not comment. She pulled a folded page from her pocket, a printout, his migration, with a line circled in pen.

“btree_gist,” she said. “You create the extension in the same migration as the exclusion constraint. I would have split them. At the bank we weren’t allowed to install extensions from a migration at all. A DBA did it, once, on a Thursday, and then pretended it had always been there. So when I read this I assumed you’d faked the overlap with a trigger, because that’s what people do when they can’t get gist equality on a scalar. You didn’t. You installed the extension. Then I thought: they let him? There isn’t a they. You let you. Which is either the best argument for your working alone or the worst.”

“Both,” he said. “I split them the first time. The extension migration ran, and on my machine the constraint passed, because I’d already created the extension by hand months earlier and forgotten. Staging had it too. I only saw the fresh-database failure when I restored a backup into an empty instance. The restore is a drill I do. It isn’t heroic. I had skipped it for a while and the skip is why the ordering bug lived. I merged the statements after that. If someone else runs the migrations on a clean Postgres, they get the extension whether they remember it or not.”

Helen sat back. The pulled thread on her cuff brushed the paper. “The drill is the part I believed before I believed the constraint. Anyone can name an exclusion constraint after they’ve read a post. The empty instance is harder to fake.” She tapped the circle. “Tell me what it actually rejects.”

“Two assessments for the same matter whose valid-time ranges overlap. Not whose transaction times overlap. We were writing ‘the rule that applies’ as a mutable foreign key. The morning in February, a new version became the rule, and the job evaluated old periods with it. Now a version has a range, and an assessment stores the range it used, and the exclusion stops two of them from claiming the same stretch. Transaction time is just when we wrote the row. I keep that column. I don’t let it decide.”

“And if the range is unbounded on the right?”

“I don’t allow that on insert. Not with the exclusion, with a check that the upper bound is present. I had an open-ended version in development eat a weekend. Across the three firms I don’t have the volume that would make the gist index the interesting problem. A bit over a thousand matters. I haven’t load-tested it. If you tell me what it does at fifty thousand I won’t pretend I’ve seen it.”

Helen made a small sound that was not quite agreement. “Ours are closed intervals too. Filing periods. Someone still inserted a null end, and the constraint didn’t save us, because exclusion constraints treat nulls as not equal. Two nulls don’t conflict. You know this. I’m asking whether you have the other hole.”

He saw the page in his mind, the second index, the one he had felt virtuous about. Unique on matter, rule version, and superseded_at. He had described it in the note as a belt beside the suspenders. The model had suggested the unique index. He had understood it well enough to keep it. He had not understood, or had not stayed with, the fact that superseded_at was null on the open row, and that a unique index would allow many open rows if the nulls did not count.

“I have a unique index,” he said, and his voice had already slowed, because he could hear the hole approaching in his own sentence, “on matter, rule version, and superseded_at. The open assessment is the one where superseded_at is null. The application sets superseded_at when a new assessment replaces an old one. So there should only be one open—”

He stopped. The should was the same should as the tenant predicate he was going to have to talk about if she had looked at the report query. He put the cup down. Both hands were on his knees again. He brought them up onto the edge of the table, flat, because he did not want to finish the sentence in a tidy voice.

“The application sets it. That’s not a constraint. Two rows can have a null superseded_at if their ranges don’t overlap, and the exclusion won’t care, and the unique index won’t care, because the nulls don’t collide. Both of them look current to the job if the job treats null as current. I don’t have a partial unique index on the open row. I knew partial indexes existed. I didn’t put one there. The model drafted the unique index. I drafted the exclusion, after February, from the Postgres docs, because I didn’t trust myself to have the overlap right. I trusted the unique index because it looked like a second lock. It isn’t. Not for this.”

The room was quiet in the particular way of a room where a person has corrected themselves and the others are deciding whether to help them recover. Danielle’s pen had stopped in the air. Helen did not help him recover. She also did not make the moment larger than it was.

“Partial unique index where superseded_at is null,” she said. “One open row per matter per rule. You can write it tonight. I’m not impressed that you can write it. I’m interested that you heard it while you were saying the other thing. Most people hear it in the hallway afterward and then decide the interviewer was being pedantic.”

“I might still decide that,” he said. “On the streetcar. I don’t think I will. It’s the same shape as a bug I do have, and I should tell you that one while you’re already here.”

Helen’s eyebrows moved. “Go on.”

“Tenant isolation is a predicate. Every query is supposed to carry the firm. I have tests on the handlers. I don’t have row-level security. A report endpoint forgot the predicate. It was mine. I found it because a log line showed a count that was larger than the firm, not because a customer saw another firm’s files. Nobody crossed. I added a test. I didn’t add RLS. The test binds the handler I fixed. It doesn’t bind the next query someone writes by hand. There is no someone. The next person is me on a Friday. That’s worse, not better. I have not done the RLS. I half-decided it was extra for three tenants. The half-decision is the part I’m not willing to say in a nicer way.”

Danielle wrote. Helen looked at the erased whiteboard as if a diagram might still be there.

“I would have hired the test and called it a mitigation,” Helen said. “I have done that, here, on a report nobody was supposed to export. Then a firm exported it. So I’m a poor person to absolve you. RLS is the right next lock. It’s also annoying, and you’ll fight the session variable, and you’ll curse it, and it’s still the right lock. If you tell me you’ve done it by the time we talk again, I’ll assume you did it for me, and I’ll weight that less. If you tell me you haven’t, because you looked and the session design is wrong for your deploy, I’ll weight that more. Don’t perform the patch for the interview.”

“I wasn’t going to have time tonight anyway,” he said. “Ruth has a document-type question I haven’t answered, and the job runs at six-thirty whether I’m proud of myself or not.”

Helen looked at him for a second longer than the sentence required. Then she turned the printout over.

“The sandbox,” she said. “The 409 named both ids. We return a generic conflict. Our caseworkers open a ticket. Yours can find the other row. Did you design that, or did someone ask?”

“Ruth asked. After February she didn’t want a message that said conflict. She wanted the other assessment’s id so she could open it without searching. I put the ids in the body. It isn’t a philosophy. If I’m vain about it, the vanity is that I listened to her once and I want it to count as a trait.”

“It’s a good body,” Helen said. “Take the praise. It was a specific decision and it was the right one, and you don’t have to launder it through humility. She asked. You did it. The body is still good.”

He felt the praise in his face, a heat that was not pleasant and not false. He did not know where to put his eyes. He put them on the printout. “Thank you,” he said, and the thanks was too small and he left it, because enlarging it would have been worse.

Danielle stood. “I should go. Helen, I’ll send you the notes before five. Wale, you’ll get a note from me either way. If it’s a next conversation, it won’t be a culture screen. Helen has already threatened me out of that.” She said it lightly. The lightness was a repair, offered to the room. At the door she paused. “The sentence you gave me for the box. I’ll use it. If you hate it tomorrow, write me a different one before I send the packet. After that it’s in Martin’s head, and I can’t chase it.”

“I won’t hate the sentence,” he said. “I might hate that I made it for the box. That’s mine to keep.”

She considered him. “All right,” she said again, and left.

Helen waited until the door had shut. “She is better than that form,” she said. “The form is how this building defends a hire to itself. If you treat her as the form, you’ll miss the person who just offered you a revision window. I don’t think you missed it. I’m saying it because founders sometimes come in here and perform sincerity at the recruiter and save the real talk for me. You did a little of that. The correction you made in front of me was careful. It put her back in possession of the facts. It was also a social act. I noticed. I don’t hold it against you. I want you to know I noticed, so you don’t leave thinking the room was simpler than it was.”

He was quiet. The heat from the praise had somewhere to go now, and it went toward embarrassment, which was more familiar. “I saw her get left out when you said the notes decay. I didn’t want the hour to be about me repairing that. I also did want her to have the right facts. Both.”

“Good,” Helen said. “Hold on.”

Her phone had lit, face down, and it buzzed once against the table, the particular cadence of a page and not a message. She turned it over. Her eyes left him. They left the room. He felt, absurdly, a small drop, as if he had been in the middle of being understood and the understanding had stood up and walked out. He sat with it. She typed three words and put the phone down.

“You went somewhere,” he said. “I thought I’d lost you. It felt like losing the room. That’s my vanity, not your emergency. If something is on fire, take it.”

Helen’s mouth did a thing that was almost a smile and was mostly fatigue. “You did lose me, for a second. Production. Hamilton again. A notice went to a client whose engagement is closed, which is our version of your flag. My on-call has it. I told him the one place to look. If I open the laptop I’ll stay in it, and you came downtown.” She set the phone face down again, a little farther from her hand. “You could have pretended not to mind. People do. They think it’s professional. I would have liked you less. Not because of the honesty prize. Because I can’t work with someone who smiles through a thing they’ve noticed. We have enough of that between engineering and product.”

“I don’t know your product organization,” he said.

“You will, if this continues. You’ll get a request to send a reminder that isn’t backed by a state. ‘Just a nudge.’ Friendly. The firms like nudges until a client calls and asks why they were nudged about a year that’s filed. I have been in that argument since March.”

She told him the March incident without being asked, which he understood as a cost she was choosing. A status had moved from Filed back to In Review when a rejection came in late. The reminder worker had treated the new transition as a fresh not-filed and had sent a second, harder notice to clients at three firms. There was a send ledger now. There was still no required reason on the backward transition, so the worker inferred. She had wanted a reason code. The product manager, whom she did not name at first and then did — Chris — had said the firms would never see it, and that plumbing was not the quarter. Helen said this without inviting him to despise Chris. She said Chris was answering to renewal calls in which owners asked why the software felt like homework. Chris was not stupid. Chris was optimizing for a sentence the owner would repeat. She was optimizing for a notice that could be defended on a Tuesday. Those were different jobs. She was tired of translating between them.

Wale listened. He did not offer Tower as the place where this problem had already been solved. It had not been solved. It had been narrowed.

“If I worked here,” he said, “I would get the nudge request too. From Chris, or from a firm, or from my own wish for the red row to go away. I’ve already wanted the quiet run. I told Danielle. The constraint doesn’t remove the wanting. It makes the wanting lose.”

Helen was still. In the kitchen someone started the microwave. The beep at the end went on too long, the way it does when the person has walked away.

“Farid said you wouldn’t sell yourself,” she said. “He was right, and he was also selling you, a little, by saying it. I’m not going to pretend I called you in only from curiosity. I am tired. I want a person who can hold a domain without me in the room. Your thinking, on the part we share, is real. Your scale is three firms and a morning job. Our scale is ugly in ways you have not met — not heroic scale, accounting-firm scale, fifty offices, a partner who exports a report to Excel and then argues with the Excel. You have not been reviewed by anyone who could block you. That is the gap I care about. Not the degree. Danielle has to care about the degree because Martin will perform caring about it. I care that the partial index wasn’t there and that you live alone in the codebase. Those can be fixed by work. A person who needs the system to remain his cannot.”

“Tower would still be mine,” he said. “I won’t sell it into this job, and I won’t let it rot as a hobby and call that balance. The morning check is real. On a week I’m on your pager, your pager wins if both are ringing. I’m telling you the order so you don’t have to discover it. If the order is disqualifying, it’s better disqualifying now.”

“It’s the sentence that makes me not want to hire you,” Helen said, “and the sentence that makes me believe the others. I don’t know which one wins yet. Martin wants one hour and a recommendation. My recommendation, if I send it, will be a working session, not an offer. Two hours, on our obligations schema, with a change I’m actually afraid of. You’d be wrong about some of it. I’d be wrong about some of it. Danielle would call that a technical deep dive and book a room. I want a board and a failure.”

“I’d come,” he said.

“Don’t agree yet.” She pulled the page back and wrote nothing on it. “You looked at the screen in reception.”

He had, for a second, the absurd idea that she could see the fourth floor from wherever her call had been. She could not. “Megan mentioned it,” Helen said. “I asked her what you did while you waited. People tell me they were thinking. She said you watched the same slide twice.”

“Ready for review,” he said. “It can mean at least three things. I didn’t know which. I didn’t tell the receptionist.”

“We’ve had a ticket open since July,” Helen said. “Chris wants the pill because the demo reads cleanly. The preparers want a different word when the client has uploaded. I have not won. You saw it in eight minutes. That doesn’t make you a prophet. It makes the pill bad. I was curious whether you’d save it up to impress me.”

“I saved it up,” he said. “I didn’t know if there would be a place to put it that wasn’t a performance. You’re giving me the place. The ticket takes the impressiveness off. I’m a little disappointed. I’ll survive the disappointment.”

She laughed once, short, not because he had been funny in the way the kitchen was funny, but because the disappointment had been named at the right size. Then she stopped laughing and looked at the phone again, not turning it over. “Ask me something you actually want to know. Not a question you prepared to show range.”

He had prepared three. He set them aside. They had been about deployment and on-call rotation and the shape of the team, and they were not dishonest, and they were not what he wanted while the March story was still in the air.

“When a CRA form changes a date,” he said, “what is the path from the PDF to a migration? Who is allowed to update a due date in production without a migration? And if Chris ships a nudge that isn’t a state transition, where does it live — in the worker, or in a table someone can query later when a client says they never got it, or in a tool you don’t own?”

Helen’s face changed. The fatigue stayed. Something more precise came in over it. “The path is bad,” she said. “A manager on the research side reads the form, writes a ticket, an engineer updates a seed, sometimes a migration if we’re good, sometimes a row in a table called deadline_overrides if we’re in a hurry. Who is allowed: anyone with production write, which is more people than I want and fewer than Chris wants. The override table has no valid time. It has an updated_at. You can see the problem. If we are in a hurry in March, the hurry becomes the rule until someone remembers. I have been the person who remembers. I have also been the person in the hurry.”

“And the nudge?”

“It would live in the campaign tool,” she said. “The one we bought. It has its own templates and its own idea of a person. It does not read the send ledger. I have said this in writing. I have not yet refused the integration. Refusal is expensive here in a way it isn’t expensive for you. You refuse a brief and the cost is a contract you didn’t take. I refuse a tool and the cost is a quarter of argument and a note in a review about partnership.”

“I’m not above that cost,” he said. “I haven’t paid it. I pay a different one. Elise can leave, and the leaving is a sentence in a ledger, and I told your recruiter about a late invoice because I was angry at a man for smudging a story. That’s not the same courage. It’s a smaller room.”

Helen folded the printout along the old crease. “You keep handing me the smaller room,” she said. “Stop auditioning with it. I believe the room is small. I believed it when I counted your firms in the schema comments. You had written the counts in a comment, by the way, which you should remove. Not because they’re shameful. Because a comment that exists to defend you against an imagined reviewer is a comment for this interview, and it was in the migration from June, before Farid wrote to me. You’ve been imagining the reviewer for a while. That’s fine. It’s also a tell. The work is the indexes, not the apology in the comment.”

He felt the comment as if she had read a diary. He had written the tenant counts above the RLS decision he had not made. Three firms. Do not build a cathedral. He had thought he was talking to his future self. He had also been talking to her, before he knew her name, a woman on a Tuesday night who might think he was inflating. The heat came back into his ears.

“I’ll take the counts out,” he said. “Not tonight, for you. Because they’re doing a job I don’t want a comment to do.”

“Good.”

She asked him, then, about a thing she was actually afraid of. A partner firm kept its own books on a cash basis and the software on accrual, and a deadline had been stored as a single timestamp in America/Toronto. Across a daylight change the firm had been marked late while the local calendar still said the return was due, and a week had gone into the argument about whose clock was real. She laid it out quickly. He started an answer about storing the civil date separately from the instant. Halfway through he heard himself assume the lost week had been a CRA payroll remittance with one national due date. She corrected him. It had been a WCB annual return for a firm in Alberta, a civil date in America/Edmonton plus that province’s holiday rule, sitting beside a separate CRA remitter calendar, quarterly or accelerated, set by the firm’s average monthly withholding. He had flattened two calendars into the timestamp. He said so, out loud, and restated the object: not an instant, and not one date, but a jurisdiction, a holiday roll, and the rule for what happened when the date landed on a day the agency was closed. Helen said, “Yes. That. We stored the timestamp because the first customer was in Ontario and we were embarrassed to have a date type in a stack that wanted instants. The embarrassment shipped.” She was stronger than he was on this. He felt the difference as a relief that surprised him. He did not have to be the person who had already solved the room. He asked what the holiday table looked like, and whether a holiday could be added after a notice had been scheduled, and what the worker did then. She told him. One of the answers was incomplete on her side — she could not remember whether the cancel path wrote a row or deleted one — and she said she could not remember, and she did not decorate the gap. He liked her for that more than for the praise, and was wary of the liking, because liking an interviewer was a way of becoming less accurate.

Near the end she said, “You used a model. You said it drafted the index. What else?”

“A migration, once, that I threw away. It added a nullable column it described as flexibility. I don’t want flexibility in that table. And a letter template, which Ruth rewrote until it sounded like her. The model is useful when I already know what the row is allowed to be. It is expensive when I don’t, because it will give me a plausible row and I will feel finished. The unique index is the expensive case. I felt finished. I was not finished. I don’t have a method more respectable than that. I read the constraint. I did not read the index implementation. I can tell you what the exclusion rejects. I cannot tell you how the gist page splits. If you need that person today, it isn’t me.”

“I don’t need that person today,” Helen said. “I need a person who can hear the nulls while he’s talking. Gist page splits we can read together if you ever work here, and you will be slower than I want, and I will be impatient, and that is an ordinary problem. The extraordinary problem is a person who cannot be wrong in the room. You were wrong twice. You corrected one before I did. I corrected the other. The room survived. Don’t look so grim about it. Grim reads as performance too.”

“I’m grim because the index is still wrong in production,” he said. “Not because of the theatre of being wrong. The job runs at six-thirty. It probably won’t hit the hole tomorrow. It can hit the hole. I’ll write the partial index after Ruth’s question. I’ll do it because the hole is real. You told me not to perform the patch. I’m telling you I’m going to do this one anyway. RLS I’ll leave until I’ve thought about the session. That’s the division. You can think it’s obedience or fear. It’s the open row.”

Helen stood. She did not offer a handshake at first. She looked at the lane, where the van had been. “Come to the session if Danielle sends it. If she doesn’t, it will be because Martin wanted a no and she couldn’t find a no that wasn’t ‘he made the hour more precise than the form.’ She may still find one. The degree. The three firms. The morning check. Any of them can be a no in a different mouth. If she says no, you should believe the no is about this building’s stomach, and you should also look at the partial index as if no one is coming to inspect it. Those aren’t contradictory.”

“I know,” he said.

At the elevator she did put her hand out. Her palm was dry. The thread on the cuff brushed his wrist. “Farid,” she said. “He did a real thing and a smudged thing. You don’t have to reconcile them for me. If you write to him, don’t make the letter a verdict. He’ll forward it to someone. He can’t help it. It’s how he loves a story.”

“I’ll call him,” Wale said. “He hates the phone. That’s the form that won’t become a letter.”

“Good.”

The elevator doors opened on the lobby. The receptionist looked up and said, “Have a good one, Wally,” cheerful, already past the moment. He said, “You too,” and heard himself miss the second chance, and this time the shame was smaller because Helen was still beside him and the day had spent the larger share of it. He did not correct the woman. He would not construct a virtue out of sparing her. He had spared himself a scene. That was all.

Danielle was at the desk, talking to the receptionist about a sticker that would not print. She saw him and stepped aside, and walked him the ten feet to the door because that was the kind of finish the building expected. “Helen will follow up,” she said. “I liked the hour. You were easy to talk to.” She heard it as it left, and he saw her hear it, because it was not true in any way that would help her, and a flicker of irritation at herself crossed her face. “That’s not what I mean. I can write the packet without inventing you. I don’t do that with everyone. Don’t thank me. Go answer your caseworker.”

“I will,” he said.

On the sidewalk the light was the flat light of a Toronto afternoon after rain, the kind that makes the streetcar tracks shine and does not warm anything. He walked to King because he wanted the walk, and because the phone was in his pocket with Ruth’s question still unanswered, and he did not want to answer it standing outside Helen’s building like a man continuing the interview for an audience of one.

He called Farid from the corner, with the walk signal counting down and then starting over. Farid answered as if the number had been a bill.

“If this is about the email,” Farid said, “I was trying to help you. You know that. You knew it when you copied me.”

“I know,” Wale said. “I also know you put Ruth in the wrong chair. Both of those can be true. I shouldn’t have done it in her inbox. I’m sorry I did it that way. I’m not sorry I said the flag was the failure. Helen saw both messages. She made up her own mind. You don’t have to defend the introduction to me.”

Farid was quiet. A streetcar passed, loud enough that Wale missed the first half of the next sentence. The second half was, “—not a child.”

“I know you aren’t,” Wale said.

“She called me, you know. In the spring. About her own mess. I didn’t fully understand it. I understood enough to think of you. That’s all I had. If you go work there, don’t tell them I don’t understand things. I understand the books. I understood the room in February better than you think. You were scared. I didn’t put that in the email. You’re welcome.”

“Thank you for not putting it in,” Wale said. He meant it. The other sentences were available — you used me, you smudged her, you want the dinner story — and they did not feel like information Farid lacked. Farid already knew. Saying them would have been for the pleasure of having Farid know that he knew. He kept the phone call from becoming a second letter. “If they ask me back, it will be to sit with a schema. If they don’t, the index is still wrong. Either way I’m glad she saw the note. You were right to send it. The correction stands. We don’t have to have a meeting about it.”

“I hate meetings,” Farid said, which was not true; Farid liked meetings in which he was the person who had found the discrepancy. He sighed. “Go home. Your voice sounds like a hallway.”

Wale took the next car. He stood because the seats were wet. At Dufferin he got off and walked the last blocks with the phone in his hand, writing Ruth where she could see a draft if she looked, then deleting, then writing again.

The document type she wanted was a police certificate that was sometimes required and sometimes not, depending on a country code and on whether the person had lived there more than six months. She had asked if it could be “just a checkbox the job ignores.” He wrote: It can be a document on the pathway with a condition, country and duration, and the job will treat it as required only when the condition holds. A checkbox the job ignores will be true on a file where it is not true, and someone will act on it, including me on a morning I’m rushing. If the condition is more complicated than country and six months, tell me the case that breaks it. He deleted the phrase including me on a morning I’m rushing. It was about him. She needed the condition. He sent the shorter note. Then he sent a second line, because the shorter note was so compressed it might sound like a refusal of her judgment. If you have already seen a file where six months is the wrong cut, I will use your cut. You see them and I see the job.

At home the kitchen table had the morning’s cup still on it, a ring dried into an ellipse. He opened the laptop. The monitoring run for the next day was queued and quiet. One row in the schema comment still recited the tenant counts, defending him against a reviewer who had, this afternoon, told him to stop. He deleted the comment. The deletion was a three-line diff and it felt larger than it was, which he distrusted, so he did not commit it alone. He wrote the partial unique index in the same migration file he had open, read it back, and found he had named the index as if he were naming a moral. He renamed it to the table and the predicate. He ran the tests he had. They passed for the reason tests pass when they were written beside the hole and not against it. He added one that inserted two open rows and expected the second to fail. It failed in the way he wanted. He did not feel finished. He felt the particular thin relief of a door that now latched, and under it the unchanged fact that RLS was still a conversation he had postponed, and that Helen had told him not to perform a patch, and that he had performed one, and that the performance and the latch were the same diff.

He made tea. He did not open Danielle’s form in his head and rewrite it. The sentence he had given her — he noticed when a system was about to say something a person would act on, and he did not always notice soon enough — sat on the table beside the cup, neither a boast nor a confession, only a thing he had said in a glass room to a woman who had needed a box filled. It was accurate enough to leave alone. The seven-forty hover was still outside it. He would tell Helen in the working session, if there was a working session. If there was not, he would still have to live with it, which he had been doing since February, at no particular cost except the cost of knowing what he was willing to almost do.

His phone buzzed. Not a page. Danielle.

Packet goes up tomorrow. I used your sentence. I also used the late invoice and the degree and the morning check. Helen asked for a working session. Martin will say yes or he will say the hour was enough. I’ll tell you which when I know. Thank you for not making me invent the company.

He typed three words and deleted the thanks and sent: I’ll wait. If it’s a no, the no is fair to send plainly.

He sat for a while with the laptop open and the index in front of him, listening to the fridge and to a neighbour’s television through the wall, a laugh track that arrived half a second after the joke it belonged to. He thought of Helen’s hand on the phone, and of Danielle’s ears going pink, and of Ruth, who would read his note in the morning and might still be annoyed, because a condition was more work than a checkbox, and her annoyance would be about the work and not about his character. That seemed bearable. He thought of Elise’s coat, still on, in a room in February. The constraint existed now. Her sentence had not expired. He closed the laptop. The six-thirty job did not need him to watch it sleep. He washed the cup. The ellipse on the table remained, dried past the point where water would lift it, and he left it, and he turned the kitchen light off, and in the dark the phone lit once more, Ruth, not a reply to his note but a forward of a client’s question that could wait until morning, and that she had sent to him anyway, because that was the arrangement, and the arrangement held.
