The Interview

He checked the address again at the corner of Adelaide and Simcoe, not because he had forgotten it but because the number on the brass plate was smaller than the number had been in his mind all morning. 212 Adelaide Street West. A brown brick front, a dental-supply wholesaler on two, a freight broker on three, Northwater on four. The plate listed them in the same type as the freight broker. He stood out of the sidewalk closure — a plywood tunnel, a compressor somewhere he couldn't see — and watched a woman with a badge on a lanyard shoulder the door with her elbow because her hands were full of a bowl with a lid. The lid was not quite on. She did not look like a person about to conduct anyone's future. He followed her in before the door decided whether it was going to close.

The lobby smelled of the soup and of wet wool, though it had not rained. There was a rubber plant in a square planter and a directory under glass that still had a company on it he was fairly sure had ceased to exist, because he had looked the building up the night before and then, ashamed of looking the building up, had looked up the failed tenant too. A man on a stool behind a short counter was watching a phone propped against a mug. He glanced at Wale and held up a finger, finished a sentence in a language Wale did not catch, and turned the phone face down as if that completed a professional act.

"Who are you here for?"

"Northwater. Wale Omotayo. I have a ten-fifteen with Denise Colbourne."

The man looked at a printed sheet, not a screen. His finger went down the page and came back up. "I don't have a Wale."

"It might be under Omotayo."

The finger went again. The man turned the sheet around, which was either courtesy or a small surrender, and Wale saw his own name written as Walle Omotoyo, with the time right and the company right and everything else slightly off, the way a person writes a word they have only heard. He felt the familiar small heat at the back of his neck and did not correct the page. Correcting a receptionist’s paper in a lobby was a kind of action he had watched other people take when they wanted a room to know they were precise. He said, "That's me. The ten-fifteen."

"Fourth floor. Elevators are slow before ten-thirty. Sign."

He signed the version of his name that was already there, which bothered him more than the mishearing, and went to the elevator. In the brushed door he looked like a person who had thought about his shirt. He had. The blazer was at a cleaner on Roncesvalles with a ticket in his wallet he had meant to use yesterday and had not, because a restore of Diane Okeke's database had taken the afternoon and then the evening had been the write-up of nothing, a log line he did not trust, and he had sat with it until the cleaner was closed. So he had come in the shirt. In the elevator he decided he would not mention the blazer if anyone apologized for the office being casual. No one was going to apologize for that. The decision sat in him anyway, unused, taking up the space a better thought might have used.

Northwater's door was glass, frosted from the waist down. A woman on the other side saw him and misjudged which of them was going to open it. They both reached. She laughed, one short note, and let him. Inside there was no desk, only a bench, a coat tree, and a screen on the wall cycling photographs of people in a kitchen he assumed was somewhere on this floor. The woman — she had the soup — told him Denise would be out, and disappeared through a second door with her shoulder again. He sat. The bench was lower than a chair. His knees came up. He took his notebook out of his bag and did not open it, because he had a superstition he would not have admitted to, that reading his own notes in a waiting area was a way of becoming a candidate, and he did not yet know if that was what he was.

He had not applied. That fact had been in his head on the streetcar in a form that kept changing shape. Sometimes it was a relief: nothing had been asked of him except to come. Sometimes it was the other thing, that he was here on the strength of a sentence Grace Lin had said on a Sunday while she was cooking, a sentence he had not heard, whose exact wording he had been trying not to invent. Inventing it would have been a way of flattering himself or injuring himself, and he did not trust either motive.

What he knew was the text. It had come on a Sunday night three weeks ago, while he was standing in the aisle of the No Frills on Dundas with a basket he had not wanted to carry and was carrying anyway. Grace: My old roommate works at a software company downtown. They are hiring for the part that is basically what you already do with us. She asked if you have something she can read that is not the website. You can say no. Her name is Priya Raman. She is not in sales.

He had put the phone back in his pocket and picked up a tin of tomatoes and put it down again. Grace did not ask him for things. She asked the screen for things, which was different. In eight months of the Hassan office using Tower she had requested three changes, all of which were about a person being able to see a fact without guessing, and he had made two of them part of the product and refused the third because it would have required the system to pretend a document had been received when all that had happened was that someone had said, over the phone, that it was coming. She had said, "All right," in the voice of a person who has been refused by institutions often enough to recognize a refusal that is at least about something. He did not want to repay that with a performance of availability.

He had also, standing in front of the tomatoes, felt the plain animal interest. A staff engineer wanted to read. He walked to the back of the store where the light was worse and read the text again. Not the website. Grace had remembered that much. He had said once, in her office, that the website was a door and not the building, and she had nodded in the way people nod when they intend to use your sentence later for their own purposes, and he had been glad she had any purpose at all.

He answered her four hours later, after he had gone home and opened the incident document and closed it and opened it again. Tell her she can write to me. I will send the July incident. It is four pages. If she is hiring a student I am not that.

Grace replied in less than a minute. She is not hiring anybody, she does not run the place, she builds the deadline part. Do not lead with what you are not. And then, as if she regretted the instruction: Do what you want. Nabil does not know I wrote you.

That last line was the one that made him send the document cleanly. Nabil's not knowing meant Grace had spent something of her own. He was not going to make her a courier for a file that contained her office's mistake as well as his. He wrote to Priya directly.

Grace said you asked for something to read. Attached is an incident from July, at one of the two offices that pay for the system. The system is called Tower. I run it through Omcoda. I am the only person on it. I do not have a computer science degree. If this is not what you meant, you don't need to reply.

He read the line about the degree three times before he sent it. It was true, and it was also a preemptive crouch, and he could see that it was a crouch, and he sent it anyway because the alternative was to let her discover the absence later and believe he had arranged the discovery. He did not know which of those was more respectful. He knew which one he could stand to have written.

She replied the next evening, late enough that he assumed she had read the four pages at her kitchen table or not at all.

This is what I meant. I am going to ask Denise in recruiting to put a conversation on the calendar. She will ask you things I will not ask you. I saw the sentence about the degree. Don't spend the hour repairing it.

He had not known what to do with the last instruction. It might have been kindness. It might have been a person telling him not to be tedious about a subject she had already priced in. He had written three answers and deleted them and sent: All right. I'll come.

Now he was on the bench, and the hour had not started, and the instruction was already difficult. A man came out of the inner door, saw him, said "You're not the cable guy," and went back in. Wale said, "No," to the closing door. He opened the notebook then, not to the incident, but to a blank page, and wrote the three case-file identifiers he had been afraid of forgetting, the ones that still violated the constraint. He remembered two. The third had been in his head on the streetcar and was not in his head now. He wrote a question mark and closed the book. His hands felt slightly ridiculous. He put them on his knees.

Denise Colbourne came out at ten-nineteen with a laptop under her arm and an expression of someone who had been late in a hallway and had decided not to arrive breathless. She was older than he had expected, or rather she was a specific age he had not pictured, early forties, with her hair in a clip that was losing. She looked at him and said his first name in a way that rhymed with sale.

He stood. "Wale. Like the start of wallet."

"Wale. I am sorry. I had it wrong in my mouth all morning. Denise. Thank you for coming in." She shook his hand. Her hand was dry and the shake was the correct length, which he noticed because he had, in other rooms, let go either too soon or not soon enough. "We're in the small room. The nice room has a leak. Can I get you water?"

"I'm all right."

"Get it anyway. The heat in there is a personality."

He took the water. The hallway was narrower than the lobby had suggested, carpeted in a grey that had given up, with doors open onto people wearing headphones. No one looked up. He was grateful. On a whiteboard in one of the open rooms, half erased, someone had written DEADLINE FANOUT and under it, circled hard enough to dent the board, DO NOT SHIP FRIDAY. He turned his head and read the rest of the line as he passed — owner Priya, the word owner written in a different colour — and felt an absurd small easing, as if a sign in his own language had appeared in a station where he had expected only advertisements.

The small room had a table, three chairs, a dead succulent, and a window onto a brick wall four feet away. Denise sat with her back to the brick. He took the chair that let him see the door, then wondered if that was a tell of some kind, then stayed in it because moving would have been a larger tell. She opened the laptop and also a paper notebook, which surprised him. The notebook had his name at the top, spelled correctly. She had fixed it since the lobby. Or the lobby had never asked her.

"You found us," she said. "Adelaide is a mess. They've had that sidewalk closed since August."

"I walked from St. Andrew. The plywood goes to the next door, so you come back."

"Every day. I have started to feel affectionate about it, which I resent." She smiled and let the smile end. "So. Thank you for making the time. This conversation is mine. Priya will come after. She is running a few minutes behind, which she told me and which I am telling you so you don't sit in the kitchen at eleven wondering if we forgot you. I am going to ask you the things I ask everyone, and a few I don't, because you did not come in through the ordinary door. Is that a fair way to start?"

"Yes."

"How did you hear about the role?"

He had known the question would arrive and had still, until it did, kept the history in a pile instead of a line. He set the cup down.

"I didn't hear about the role. A woman who uses my system knows Priya. She asked if Priya could write to me. Priya read an incident note and asked you to put this on the calendar. I looked at the posting after I said I would come."

Denise's pen hovered. He could see her deciding whether to write the long version or a short one she could say later to someone else. "The woman who uses your system. Is she a client?"

"She manages the office. The client, if that's the word, is her boss. Hassan Settlement Services, in Mississauga. They pay monthly. Grace Lin is the person who actually works in it."

"And she and Priya are friends."

"They were roommates. I don't know more than that. Grace said old roommate. She also said I could say no."

Denise wrote something short. He had the impression, not yet examined, that she was relieved by the modesty of the connection and also disappointed by it. A referral from a staff engineer's former roommate was not a referral from a staff engineer. It was a side door with a draft. He did not help her upgrade it.

What he did not tell her, because he had not been in the room where it happened and did not want to counterfeit it, was the part Priya had told him later, in two sentences, in the same email in which she had sent the calendar invitation. Grace had called about a cat. Priya had said she was spending her Saturdays interviewing people who had built projects. She had used a phrase Priya would not repeat, only that it had been unkind and that Grace had then said there was a person who would not build her boss a website. Priya had asked what he would build. The rest of the email was logistics. Wale had read those two sentences on a Tuesday night with the laptop on his knees and had understood that he had entered this building, three weeks later, as the answer to a complaint. He did not resent the complaint. He had his own. He resented, mildly, that a cat had been the occasion, and then the resentment embarrassed him, because occasions were allowed to be small. People did not owe him a dignified origin for a conversation.

He also knew, because Grace had told him on the phone the day after Priya's first reply, what Grace had chosen to leave out. She had told him in a rush, the way a person confesses a social crime. "I didn't tell her about July. The emails. I didn't think it was mine. And I didn't tell her you came off cold, except I did say Nabil thinks you're rude, because if you come off cold I don't want her ringing me. I'm sorry. That's me covering me." He had said he would rather she had said the rude part than the other part. She had gone quiet, and then she had said, "You don't have to keep being grateful to me for using the thing. It is my job to use the thing." He had said, "I know." They had both pretended that settled it.

Denise said, "I'll be honest with you, because the other way wastes the half hour. When Priya asked me to schedule this, my first answer was no. Not to her face. In my head. We have a posting that says degree or equivalent practical experience, and I live inside the word equivalent, and it is a word that has gotten me in trouble. I put Priya's name in the sponsor column before I ever wrote to you. If this goes badly, the exception is hers. I am telling you so that you don't spend any charm trying to make me feel that I discovered you. I didn't."

He had not been preparing charm. The information landed somewhere more useful. "All right. What does the sponsor column oblige her to do?"

Denise looked up. The question was not one of the ones on her page. "It obliges her to say, in a meeting I am also in, that she has seen the work and wants the process to continue. It does not oblige anyone to hire you. Chris Boily, who manages the team, is on leave until the week after next. He will read my notes and he will talk to Priya. If he wants a degree conversation, he will have it. Priya may have told you not to bother. She does that. She is not the only vote."

"She told me not to spend the hour repairing it. She didn't say there would be no other hour."

"Good. Then I don't have to unwind her." Denise sat back. The chair clicked. "Tell me what you actually do. In sentences I can say to Chris without lying and without borrowing your vocabulary. He will borrow it if I write it down, and then he will be annoyed at both of us."

Wale felt the shape of the request and was grateful for it. She was asking him to be portable. He looked at the brick beyond her shoulder and then back, because looking away while he found a sentence sometimes read as evasion, and he did not want to spend the correction later.

"I run a small system called Tower. It watches immigration files for two offices. It tells them when an eligibility window is about to close, or when a file has gone quiet long enough that the person should be called again, and it keeps a record of what was true on the day the notice went out. Omcoda is the name I sell that under. I don't take a brief from an office and build them their own software. If they need something that only they need, I usually say no. If they need something the other office will also need, I put it in the product. There is no team. I write it, I deploy it, I answer when it breaks. The offices pay a monthly fee. It is not a large fee."

Denise wrote. Her handwriting was faster than he expected and less tidy. "You said usually say no. When did you say yes?"

"Diane Okeke, the second office, asked if the monitor could watch conditions on a study permit, not only the expiry. Both offices have those files. I added it. Nabil Hassan asked for a client portal with his name on it, a quote calculator, and a login for his brother-in-law. I said no to all three."

"And he is still a customer."

"He is still a customer. He was angry. He remains somewhat angry. Grace is the one who decided to stay, practically. He signs the transfer."

Denise nodded in a way that did not mean she agreed with the decision. "Why is that a thing you want me to know? It would have been easier to describe a happy client."

"Because if Chris hears startup, he will think I take work and ship features. The refusals are the job. The monitoring is the job. The rest is me not doing a different job."

She looked at her page. He saw, upside down, that she had written SaaS in the margin earlier and had drawn a line through it, not a clean line, a line that left the word readable. She noticed him noticing. A flicker of irritation crossed her face, not large, the irritation of a person whose private notes have been read by the subject of them. He could have looked away and preserved the fiction that he had not seen. He did not, and then he had to do something with the fact that he had not.

"If that word stays in the margin," he said, "Priya will spend the first ten minutes taking it back out of me. There isn't a round. There isn't a pitch. There are two invoices."

The irritation stayed a moment longer, long enough for him to feel that he had been rude in exactly the register Nabil meant, and then Denise let out a breath that was almost a laugh and not quite. "You can read upside down. Fine. I wrote it when you sat down, before you talked, because the email from Priya said founder and my hand knows that word. I will not say SaaS to Chris. I will say two paying offices, monthly, sole operator, product he will not customize." She struck the word a second time, harder. "Do not do that with him, by the way. The looking. He will mind more than I do."

"All right."

"I'm not insulted. I'm being specific." She turned the page, a small reset. "Tell me about a time you had a conflict with someone you were delivering work to."

He had disliked this species of question all his life, the request that a person carve a clean incident out of a month that had not been clean, and he disliked it less when the incident was already sitting in the room between them. July had happened. Pretending to search for a preferable story would have been a vanity of its own.

"In July the system sent a notice twice to fourteen people at Nabil's office, and one of those people should not have been written to at all. He had a lawyer. The lawyer had told Nabil not to contact him directly. The flag was on the file. My query checked the primary contact and not the secondary, and he was the secondary on a family file. The job also sent the batch, crashed before it recorded the send, and sent it again on the retry."

Denise waited. She was good at waiting. He understood that the shape she needed had a middle.

"Nabil met me in the parking lot of the plaza. He did not want the caseworkers to hear him. He said I had made him look like he did not know his own files. He said I would not put his name on a page but I would email his clients. I wanted to explain the join. He was not talking about the join. He was talking about being ashamed in front of a lawyer who already thought his office was small."

"What did you say?"

"I said the second email should not have gone out, and the man with the lawyer should not have been on it, and those were my failures. I said the page with his name on it was a different question and I was not going to build it. Then I stopped talking. We went upstairs. I showed Grace the two rows. She saw it faster than he did. I patched it from a chair in their office and I stayed until a notice went out once, to a test address she controls, and the row recorded it."

"And the relationship now?"

"He pays. He does not recommend me. Grace does, apparently, to her old roommate. When something breaks he writes to her and she writes to me. That is worse for her and more accurate about who knows the files."

Denise tapped the pen once. "If I write, he separated the client's humiliation from the feature request and did not use the outage to concede scope, Chris will think you are proud. If I write, he apologized and rebuilt the relationship, I will be lying. Give me the sentence you can live with."

He was quiet long enough that she picked up her water. The quiet was not strategy. He was hearing Nabil's sentence again — you will not build the page but you will email my clients — and feeling the same inability to answer it inside its own terms. In the parking lot the asphalt had been soft at the edges. A cart had rattled behind the grocery that anchored the plaza. Nabil had been in shirtsleeves, a folder under his arm that he was not using, carried because a folder made a hard conversation feel like business. Wale had sweated through the back of his shirt on the bus from Kipling and had hated that this was part of the scene, the body arriving already at a disadvantage. He had felt, under the apology he did owe, a hard small refusal to let Nabil fuse two complaints into a single accusation of character. The fusion was understandable. It was also not true. He had stood with his hands open, which he had only noticed afterward, a posture he distrusted in himself because it looked like patience.

"He was right about the mail," Wale said. "He was wrong about what the mail meant about the page. I said both. I did not say them unkindly. I also did not make him feel better, and I was not trying to."

Denise wrote that, or wrote something shorter that she believed was that. "A lot of people in that parking lot would have promised the portal and then quietly not built it."

"I would have had to build it or be a person who lies about building it. The second one is more expensive. He would have told people the portal was coming."

"All right." She glanced at the clock on the laptop, not theatrically. "I'm going to ask you about money. People get delicate. Don't. The band for this role, product engineer on Priya's group, is one hundred and fifteen to one hundred and thirty-five, depending on what Chris decides you are. It is not a senior title. The posting says two years. You do not have two years inside a company. You may have two years of something else. I don't set the number. I need to know whether the number is a reason to stop."

He did the arithmetic in the open, where she could see it, because hiding it would have produced a false smoothness. The two invoices together were a little under two thousand a month, and the server and the mail and the error tracker and the accountant he used once a year came out of that, and the room on Sorauren came out of that, and some months Nabil paid on the twelfth and some months on the twenty-seventh. A hundred and fifteen thousand was a different life. It was also a life in which Tower had to be either a night job or a thing he handed to the two offices with a date on it. He had not decided which of those he was willing to say in a room. The not-deciding was the fact.

"The number is not a reason to stop. I don't know what I would do with the two offices in the first month. I haven't told them I'm here. If I told you I was ready to shut it down for a salary, I'd be deciding it in front of you to make the conversation easier."

Denise's face did a small, competent thing. She did not look pleased and she did not look maternal. She looked like a person adding a risk to a list she already had a column for. "If you get to an offer, that sentence becomes the conversation. It cannot be a surprise in week two. I am not going to coach you out of it. I am going to write flight risk with a question mark, and Priya is going to argue with my question mark, and Chris is going to ask you for a plan. You should have a plan before Chris, even if the plan is a date you will tell Grace."

"That's fair."

"It is also my job. Don't thank me for it." She said this without heat. "Work authorization. The form asks. Are you legally able to work in Canada without sponsorship?"

"Yes. I'm a citizen."

She ticked a box. The speed of the tick was its own small mercy; she did not make him narrate a family. "The next part is the part people perform. I'd like you not to. Why Northwater? And if the answer is because Priya wrote to you, say that, and then tell me what you saw when you looked, because you did look."

He had looked the same night, at the kitchen table, the laptop pushed against a plate he had not cleared. For a second, sitting across from Denise, he saw himself reading the posting on the streetcar, as if he had been coming home from something with a schedule. He had not been. He had been in the room on Sorauren with the window open. The other picture was neater, and he set it down. The posting was six paragraphs. Four of them could have belonged to any company on Adelaide. Two of them could not. One described workers that computed limitation dates and permit expiries for firms that missed them by doing the computation in a spreadsheet on a Friday. The other said the person in the role would sit with the operations staff of a customer often enough to be embarrassed when the screen was wrong. He had believed the second paragraph conditionally. Companies wrote such paragraphs and then hired people who were never allowed near a customer. The whiteboard in the hall, Priya's name in another colour, had raised the probability a little. It had not settled it.

"Because Priya wrote to me, first. When I read the posting, the part that was real to me was the deadlines, and the claim that the engineer hears about the screen. I don't know if the second part is true here. It's true in my work because there is no one to put between Grace and me. You might have someone whose job is to stand in that gap and be pleasant. If you do, the posting is advertising."

"We have a customer-success team," Denise said. "They are pleasant. They are also tired of being the only ones who hear it. Priya's group has a rule that a production error in deadlines gets a note the customer can read, written by the person who fixed it, passed through success so that the tone doesn't start a fire. She fought for the rule. She is sentimental about it and also correct. You can ask her about the fight. Don't ask her in a way that makes her perform the fight."

He nodded. He wanted, suddenly, to ask Denise whether she liked Priya. The want was not interview tactics. He had been in the building less than an hour and already the picture of Priya was a composite of other people's precautions: don't repair the degree with her, don't make her perform, she puts her name on exceptions, she is not the only vote, she told Grace something unkind about candidates and then went looking for a person a roommate could stand. A composite was not a person. He did not ask. The question would have forced Denise to be disloyal or bland, and he could see she had a talent for neither, and he was not going to spend it for his own comfort.

She asked him how he worked with people who were not engineers. He told her about sitting beside Grace on a Tuesday in May while she processed a reactivation list, and about the moment she clicked a file into "monitoring" when the file was already in "notice sent," and the screen allowed it. He had not said, in the moment, that the screen was wrong. He had asked her what she thought the click had done. She had said, "It puts them back in the queue so I don't lose them." She had been trying to save a person from being forgotten. The software had called that corruption. He had gone home and made the illegal click impossible, and had added a separate action called keep visible, which did what she had actually wanted. The next week she had used it without comment. He had wanted a comment. He mentioned the wanting, because it was part of the truth and because it made him sound less like a person who altered systems from a pure love of invariants. Denise wrote kept visible and, beside it, wanted thanks? He saw it and let it stand. She was not wrong.

She asked what he was bad at. He said he had never had his work read by someone who would be woken up by it. He said his tests were thinner than his incident notes. He said he would sometimes explain a thing past the point where the other person had understood it, because the explanation was still satisfying a doubt in him and not a doubt in them. Denise said, "I have not seen the third one yet." He said, "The conversation has been easy to answer." She smiled with her mouth closed, which was the first time he thought she might remember him as himself and not as a risk with a sponsor.

At the end she closed the laptop and did not close the notebook.

"Priya is in the kitchen. I'll walk you. If you and she run long, that is her decision and the calendar's problem. If you want to stop, you can say so. People forget they can say so."

"I don't want to stop."

"I know. I still say it." She stood, and then, with her hand on the door, she did a thing she had not done in the half hour. She spoke without the page in front of her. "You made my notes harder. That's not an insult. Most people make them easy and then I spend the afternoon taking adjectives out. I will remember that you made me strike a word. Try not to be proud of it."

"I wasn't going to be."

"Everyone is, a little. Come on."

The kitchen was the kitchen from the photographs, smaller in life, with a dishwasher running and a note above the sink that said the sponge was new and should be allowed to be new. Priya Raman was standing at the counter reading her phone with her mug held at an angle that would have spilled if there had been more tea in it. She was not what he had built. He had built someone taller, and older, and more armed. She was about his age, perhaps a year or two past it, in a grey sweater with a small hole at the cuff she had clearly decided was not worth mending, her hair in a knot that was coming undone at the nape. She looked up when Denise said her name, and the look she gave him was quick and unadorned, the look of a person checking a fact against a photograph and finding that there had been no photograph.

"Wale. I'm late. There was a page. It was our monitor deciding a queue was on fire because a customer uploaded a spreadsheet with a date in American order. I am telling you so you don't think lateness is how we do this."

"Was the date the third of April or the fourth of March?"

She stopped with the mug halfway to the counter. Then she set it down properly. "The third of April. Which is not a date we had, so the worker treated it as the future and tried to schedule a reminder in a year, and the reminder fan-out has a guard that pages when a single file produces more than fifty jobs. It produced one job. The guard was wrong. I have just spent nine minutes discovering that, and I am going to spend twenty later being angry at the guard. Hello."

"Hello."

Denise looked between them with an expression Wale recognized from Grace, the expression of a person who has brought two live wires into a room and is interested in the outcome and unwilling to be blamed for it. "I am going to leave you. Small room is free until noon. Priya, he reads margins. Wale, she has not had lunch. Neither fact is a strategy." She left. The dishwasher entered a louder phase, as if it had been waiting for the audience to thin.

Priya poured the rest of her tea into the sink. "Do you want anything?"

"I have water."

"Then come. If I stay in here I'll start on the guard."

They walked back past the whiteboard. She saw him see it. "That was last Friday's almost-disaster. We didn't ship. The circle is me being dramatic so that the drama is on the board and not in the deploy. It works about half the time."

In the small room she took Denise's chair, then stood up again and took the chair beside the table instead, so that she was not across from him like a panel. She had a paper notebook, thicker than Denise's, with a rubber band around it. She did not open a laptop. He noticed that and decided not to be flattered by it. People had preferences. Some of them were even about him.

"Denise has the process," she said. "I asked her to keep it, including the parts that will irritate both of us. What I want is narrower. I read your July note. I have questions about whether the note and the system are the same object. I also want to know whether you can sit in a group that already has opinions. You have never had to. That is not an accusation. It is the risk I am actually hiring for, if I am hiring. Chris will have a different risk. He is not here."

"Denise told me he's back the week after next."

"Good." She folded her hands, unfolded them, and put them on the notebook. She seemed, for a moment, not to know how to begin without a script, and the not-knowing made her more present rather than less. "Start anywhere in the July incident that you think I may have misunderstood. I will tell you what I thought I read."

"Tell me what you thought. I'll know faster."

She nodded. "A notice job hands a batch to the mail provider. The provider accepts. The process dies before the database marks the notices sent. The retry has no record of the acceptance, so it sends again. Separately, a do-not-contact flag on a secondary person is invisible to the query because the query only knows the primary. You fixed the retry by writing an attempt row before the send, with a key the retry can find, and you fixed the flag by making the suppression look at every contact on the family file. You deployed the same afternoon from their office. The note says a check constraint now rejects illegal file statuses, so a file cannot be in monitoring and notice-sent at once. And it says you are the only person who reviewed any of this."

"The last part is true. The attempt row is true. The suppression is true. The check constraint is the part I need to correct."

He heard himself say it and felt a thin bright relief that he did not admire. The relief was the relief of putting down a bag he had been carrying so carefully that the care had become a second falsehood. He also felt the loss of the version of himself the four pages had constructed, a person whose database enforced what his mouth claimed. Priya did not move to fill the pause. She had heard the clause. She was going to make him finish it.

"The constraint is in production as NOT VALID," he said. "I added it that night. Validating it would have failed on three rows that were already in illegal statuses. I didn't want to invent a clean status for them at eleven at night and I didn't want to leave the migration half-run. NOT VALID let the migration finish. New writes are checked. Those three rows are still there. I special-cased them in a report in August so the report wouldn't crash. The note doesn't say NOT VALID. It doesn't mention the three rows. I wrote the sentence about the constraint before I had decided not to validate it, and after I decided, I changed the date on the note and not the sentence."

Priya's eyes stayed on him. Whatever she had been expecting, it had not been the specific grammar of this. He could see her adjusting, not from belief to contempt, but from one picture to a sharper one. "So if I had asked your database, the night I read the note, whether every row satisfied the constraint, it would have said no."

"It would have said the constraint existed and was not validated. If you had asked the application, it would have looked cleaner, because the application doesn't surface those three files. Grace can't click them. I can see them if I query."

"Do you remember the identifiers?"

He almost laughed, and did not. The laugh would have been panic. "Two of them. I wrote them down in your lobby. The third I had on the streetcar and lost. I'll have to look."

"Don't look now." She was quiet. The dishwasher, even here, was a rumour in the wall. "When you wrote the sentence, did you know you were going to leave it untrue?"

"Not when I wrote it. When I updated the date, yes. I told myself I would validate it the next night and the sentence would become true retroactively. I didn't do it the next night. Diane had a failed backup. After that the sentence was already in a document I had sent to no one, so the urgency dropped. Then I sent it to you. The urgency should have come back. It didn't. I wanted the note to be the system a competent person would have."

"And you knew I was reading it to decide whether to spend an exception."

"Yes."

She sat with that. He did not add a softening clause. The softening clauses were all forms of asking her to manage his shame, and she had not offered, and he was not going to invoice her for it.

"All right," she said. "I'm angry at the sentence. I'm less angry than I would have been if you had let me praise it. Don't take that as absolution. Take it as sequence." She opened the notebook at last and wrote a line. "Walk me through the attempt row. Slowly. I want the transaction, not the moral."

He was grateful for the change of subject in the way a person is grateful for a task after a confession, and he distrusted the gratitude, and he did the task anyway. He drew on the back of a page she tore out for him. There was a notices table. There was an attempts table. The worker began a transaction, inserted an attempt with a key derived from the file, the notice type, and the cycle date, and committed that insert before it called the provider. If it died, the retry found the attempt and, if the attempt said unconfirmed, asked the provider for the message by the same key. He stopped.

"I'm describing a provider that will answer that question," he said. "Postmark will not, not in the way I just said. I don't store their message id unless the call returns. If the process dies after they accept and before I store the id, the retry cannot ask them whether they took it. What the retry does instead is refuse to send if an attempt exists in the unconfirmed state, and it raises a row I have to look at. Fourteen people getting two emails was worse than fourteen people getting none until I look. That is the actual trade. The note is cleaner than that. The note sounds as if the key makes the send idempotent. It doesn't. It makes the second send my problem instead of the client's."

Priya had been following with her finger on his drawing. She stopped at the word unconfirmed. "The first version of your sentence just now was the fantasy. You corrected it in the middle. Do you know you did that?"

"Yes. Halfway through I heard it go past the part I have."

"Good. Leave the correction in, when you talk to Chris. People sand them out. It makes me trust the rest less." She tapped the drawing. "Two workers. Both pass the check for an existing attempt before either insert commits. Both insert. Your unique key is file, type, cycle date. One of them fails the unique constraint. Which one sends?"

"The one that commits the insert. The other rolls back and, on its retry, sees the attempt." He heard the neatness and looked for the hole himself before she could, because he would rather find it than be shown it, and then he recognized that preference as vanity and kept looking anyway. "Unless the first one commits the attempt, sends, and dies before marking confirmed, and the second one, having failed the insert, retries, sees unconfirmed, and also sends, because I just told you the unconfirmed state does not prove the provider never got it. I don't have a lock that covers the send. The send is outside the transaction on purpose. So the unique key stops two attempts. It does not stop two sends if I treat unconfirmed as permission to try again after a crash. And I do treat it that way if I look at the row and get impatient."

"Do you get impatient?"

"In July I waited. I went to Mississauga and I used a test address. On a Tuesday in August, with Diane's office, a notice sat unconfirmed for ninety minutes and I re-sent it by hand after I decided the first one had not gone. It had gone. It went to her, not to a client. She wrote to me and said she had received two, and was the system all right. I said there had been a duplicate to her only. That was true and also a way of making the duplicate small."

Priya leaned back. The chair complained. "You keep handing me the smaller version after I have already accepted the larger one. I want you to notice the habit. It is not the same thing as honesty. Honesty would have been the larger version first. The smaller one afterward is you managing my opinion of the larger one."

He felt the blood in his face. The description was accurate, and the accuracy had no cruelty in it, which made it harder to turn aside. She was not enjoying herself. She was looking at the drawing.

"You're right," he said.

"I know. Sit with it for a second. I am going to get this wrong on purpose and I want to see what you do." She said it so plainly that the test was almost not a test. "You went with a queue. SQS, or something like it. The attempt row is a message."

"No. There is no queue service. There is a table of jobs in Postgres. A worker selects them with SKIP LOCKED, does the work, and marks the job. The attempt row is not the job. The job says send this notice. The attempt row says I have already tried, or I have not finished trying. I didn't want a second system to operate. I can barely operate the one."

"SKIP LOCKED means the second worker cannot see the locked row."

"It means the second worker skips it. If the first worker is still inside the transaction, yes, the row is invisible to the second. If the first worker commits the lock away and the job is marked done, the second never had a claim. The dangerous window is the one I drew, where the dangerous part isn't the job row. It's the email, which is not in the database while it's happening."

She nodded. She had known. The deliberate wrongness had been thin, and he had answered it without triumph, and he saw her notice the absence of triumph and decide, quietly, to put down a weapon she had been prepared to need. He felt the decision more than he saw it. It was in the way her shoulders dropped a fraction.

"All right," she said. "My turn to be actually wrong, not strategically. I told Denise, after I read your note, that you had probably overbuilt the state machine to compensate for being alone. People alone do that. They make cathedrals because no one is there to tell them the parish only needed a lock on the door. I got to your office in my head and it was full of states. Then I read it again this morning. You have, what, six statuses on a file."

"Five. And the three rows are in a sixth that the constraint doesn't admit. I should count that. So six, if I'm being the person I was ten minutes ago."

"Five or six is not a cathedral. The cathedral, if there is one, is the note. You wrote four careful pages about a system that fits on a diagram you drew from memory." She looked at him. "Why was the note that careful? Don't say because the work is serious. Work can be serious and the note can be three lines."

He thought of the night he had written it. The window in the room on Sorauren had been open. Someone downstairs had been frying fish. He had written the note because July had frightened him in a way the fright of the parking lot did not cover. The fright was that he had a TODO above the query, secondary contacts shaky, and he had shipped past it because Nabil had wanted reactivation calls going out before a long weekend, and Wale had wanted to be a person who could move quickly when a customer had a real deadline. The speed and the principle had been on opposite sides of the week and he had chosen the speed and called it responsiveness. The note had been a place to put the system back under a standard after he had already dropped it. Care, after the fact, performed at a length no one had asked for.

"I wrote it the week after," he said, "when no one was asking for it. Grace had already gone back to work. Nabil had already decided to keep paying. I think I wrote four pages because three lines would have made it look like an accident, and I needed it to look like a thing I had understood. Some of the understanding was real. The length was for me."

"Did an assistant draft any of it?"

The question was the one he had been waiting for without knowing he was waiting. He had used a model on the migration. He had not used one on the narrative. The distinction mattered, and it would sound like a distinction manufactured to matter, and it was still the truth.

"The note, no. I typed it. The migration, the first draft, yes. I asked it for a check constraint on status and a way to get the bad rows into a legal state. It gave me the constraint and a backfill that set the bad rows to a status called needs review. I understood the constraint. I did not want the backfill. needs review would have appeared on Grace's screen as if it were a real condition of the file. It wasn't. It was a bucket I would have been inventing so the migration could be perfect. I deleted the backfill. I added NOT VALID myself. So the part a tool wrote was stricter than what I shipped, and the lie in the note is mine. The tool doesn't know about the note."

Priya was silent again. She had a habit, he was learning, of letting a finished answer remain finished. Denise filled. Priya stored. He did not know yet which of them he found more exposing.

"Would you have told me that if I hadn't asked?" she said.

"I don't know. I had decided to tell you about NOT VALID before I came in. The tool I was going to tell you if we talked about the migration. We did. So I didn't have to find out."

"That is a more precise answer than yes." She rubbed the hole in her cuff with her thumb, noticed herself doing it, and stopped. "I'm going to tell you what I saw in the note before I knew any of this, because you should hear the impression you actually created, not the one you're now dismantling. The attempt-before-send was the part I trusted. Not because it is ingenious. Because the trade was visible in it, even through the cleaner wording. You chose a human delay over a duplicate. I have watched people choose the duplicate because the delay shows up in a dashboard and the duplicate shows up in a complaint they can call user error. The refusal to build the portal was not in the note. Grace told me. I believed her because it made her life harder and she told me anyway. Those two things are why you are in this room and not a PDF in a pile. The constraint sentence is why I might walk you out. I have not decided. Do not help me decide by becoming abject."

"I wasn't going to."

"You might not call it that."

He drank the water. It was warm. He could feel the morning accumulating in his shoulders, the bench, the lobby signature, the word he had made her strike, the third identifier he could not remember. He looked at the brick wall. A pigeon moved on a ledge and was gone.

"You pulled me past a screen," he said. "Denise told me she would have said no, and that your name is on the exception. So this conversation is you checking whether you were stupid. I would be checking the same thing. The degree isn't the reason you were. The sentence might be. I don't think I can help you with the verdict by talking around it."

Priya's face went still. Not closed. Still, the way a person goes still when a calculation they had kept private is said back to them in ordinary words. He had a moment of fear that he had humiliated her, and he looked at the fear, and under it he did not find the wish to humiliate. He found the wish to stop pretending the hour had a shape other than the one it had. If she was angry, he would wear it. He had been angry at sentences of his own often enough to know the difference between being seen and being used.

She said, "Denise shouldn't have told you she said no."

"She told me so I wouldn't spend the meeting being grateful to the wrong person."

"That sounds like her." Priya looked at the dead succulent as if it had contributed. "Yes. I am checking whether I was stupid. I don't like that you could see it. I like it more than I would have liked discovering, next week, that you had seen it and decided to be tactful. Tact is how this team gets sold work it shouldn't build. You would not be doing me a favour." She met his eyes again. "For the record, I was not being charitable. The req has been open since early July. I am tired. Chris is away. A person who had already been punished by a double send was more interesting to me than a person with a tidy transcript. Interest is not a compliment. It is a need."

"I know."

"Grace needs the software to keep existing. She did not say that either. I am saying it because it is obvious, and because if you take a job here and Tower falls over in October she will have spent her roommate on nothing. I am not your conscience about that. I am telling you I noticed."

"She told me I don't have to be grateful to her for using it. She uses it because it is her job."

"She can believe that and still have the other motive. People are allowed to have both." Priya pulled the notebook closer. "I'm going to ask you about something you don't know. I'd like you not to invent a bridge."

"All right."

"When two of us change the same area of the schema, we put the migrations in a queue and one of us rebases. Last month I rebased over a colleague who had added a column, and my migration assumed the column wasn't there, and the deploy failed in the afternoon when a customer was trying to record a filing. We recovered. It took forty minutes. The customer wrote a sentence I deserved. Here is the question. You have never rebased over a living person. What do you think you will do the first time someone tells you your migration has to wait because theirs is in?"

He opened his mouth and closed it. The honest picture was not flattering. He would feel the wait in his body as a kind of incorrectness. He would want to explain why his change was smaller, or safer, or already promised to Grace, and Grace would not even be in that story, he would be importing her as a justification. He might comply and then be cold in the channel afterward. He might comply badly, by editing their migration instead of his, and tell himself he was helping.

"I think I will argue once," he said, "and the argument will be disguised as a clarification. If the person is sure, I will wait, and I will be worse at hiding the resentment than I think I am. I don't have a practice of being read. I have a practice of being the only one who is right in the repository, which is an easy practice to confuse with being right."

Priya wrote. "That is the best answer you have given that wasn't a correction of yourself. Remember it. Chris will ask you a version of it in the language of collaboration, and if you answer him in the language of collaboration I will know you have sanded this out."

"You keep giving me lines to keep."

"I keep meeting people who can say a true thing once and a professional thing forever after. I am selfish about the first one. Let me be." She glanced at the clock on the wall, a clock he had not noticed, shaped like a leftover from a school. "We have twenty minutes before I have to go be angry at my guard. I want to spend them on a weakness that is mine, because I don't want this to be a hearing. Last spring I shipped a constraint that was correct. A filing could not be marked served before the document existed in the system. Our largest firm asked us to loosen it. Their clerks mark served on the phone and upload the affidavit later, sometimes the next morning. The correct constraint was making them lie, or making them wait, and they were lying. I loosened it. I added a state, served pending document, with a job that screams if the document is not there by noon. Half of engineering thought I had given in to a customer. The customer thought the new state was an accusation. Both were a little right. The firm still uses it. Two of the clerks started uploading the same day because the scream was worse than the extra minute. One of them will never forgive the word pending."

She stopped. He understood he was being given something that had cost her a meeting, perhaps several, and that the gift was also a probe. She wanted to see what he did with a story in which the invariant had lost.

"What did you want the clerks to do?" he asked.

"I wanted them to upload first. That was the correct order and it was not their morning. Their morning is a phone and a lineup. I wanted the product to teach them a virtue they didn't have time to learn. The scream is a compromise I don't love. It works. Working has not made me love it."

"Grace would have clicked past your first constraint," he said. "Not to lie. To keep the person from being lost in a queue she couldn't see. I built her a separate action so the constraint could stay. You didn't have that option, or you had it and it wasn't enough, because the firm's morning is the product. I don't know which. I'm not saying you gave in. I'm saying the clerk who hates the word pending can see the accusation even if the job is right."

Priya looked at him for a long moment. "Grace showed you that, or you saw it."

"She said the click put them back so she wouldn't lose them. I had been about to tell her the click was invalid. She would have agreed and she would have kept her workaround in a spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet would have become the real system. I have a prejudice against that. It is the same prejudice that makes me write notes that are cleaner than the database."

"Yes." She did not write this down. He had the sense she was going to remember it without paper, and that this was not entirely comfortable for either of them. "If you come here, you will meet that clerk. Not metaphorically. Her name is Laila. She works for a firm in Brampton and she has my email. You will want to explain the state machine. She will hear a lecture from a person who does not answer her phone. If you can hear her before you explain, you will be useful. If you can't, you will be a second copy of me, and one is expensive enough."

"I might explain anyway and notice too late."

"Then notice too late out loud. She can work with that. She cannot work with a person who finishes the lecture because he has started it."

He thought of Nabil in the lot, of the join he had wanted to unfold into the heat, of the decision to stop. He had stopped for Nabil and had explained, later, to Grace, who could use it. The skill was not silence. The skill was knowing which of them was in front of him. He did not have the skill as a trait. He had two memories and a hope of a third. He did not say this in a way that asked for credit. He said, "In the parking lot I stopped. Upstairs I explained. I think those were the right ways around. I have one example. You're describing a job."

"Yes. A job is a great many mornings." She closed the notebook. The rubber band had left a ridge in the cover. "Ask me what you actually want to ask. Not a question you prepared to demonstrate curiosity. If you don't have one, we can stop. Stopping is allowed."

He had several prepared, and they fell through him like cards, and what remained was duller and more real. "When the deploy failed for forty minutes, who wrote the sentence to the customer?"

"I did. Success passed it through. They changed one adjective. I had written that we had made an error in the order of migrations. They wrote that we had a brief interruption in recording filings. I let them. I have not forgiven myself for the adjective, which is absurd, because the customer needed the plain time more than they needed my purity. I am telling you so you know the rule on this team is not a poem. We write the note. Sometimes it gets sanded. I decide which sanding I can live with. Some weeks I decide badly."

"Did the customer believe the sanded version?"

"The customer believed the forty minutes, which were true in both versions. The operations manager wrote back and said thank you for the note and please don't let it happen on a Monday. It was a Wednesday. She was making a general request." Priya's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You would have liked her. She did not care about our constraint. She cared that a clerk was standing at a desk with a person in front of her and could not type."

He nodded. He could see the desk. He had seen Grace's, which was laminate, with a photograph of a child in a paper frame and a list of extensions written in ballpoint on the wood itself, where the laminate had chipped. He had a question under the question, and he asked it before he could make it elegant.

"Do you want someone who will argue with the sanding, or someone who will learn when you have already decided?"

"Both, on different days, which is a rotten answer and the true one. If you argue every adjective you will exhaust me and I will start sanding you. If you never argue I will have hired a person who lets a sentence go the way you let NOT VALID go. I am trying not to hire my own failure in a different body." She stood, not to end the meeting, but because the chair had finally defeated her. She stayed standing, one hand on the back of it. "There is a thing I should not hold until Chris. I am going to say it. If we continue, he may still want the degree in the first ten minutes. I told you not to repair it with me. That instruction was for my interview, because I had already decided it wasn't the interesting risk, and I didn't want my hour spent on a ritual. His hour is not mine. If he needs the ritual, you will have to decide whether you can stand it. I can't spare you. Spending the exception did not make me his manager."

"If he needs it, he needs it," Wale said. "I can say I haven't finished a degree because I haven't started one. I was going to start in January and I didn't, because the two offices and the incidents were the work, and the work was teaching me the parts I would have been trying to learn in order to be allowed to do the work. That sentence is true. It is also a little too pleased with itself. If I say it to him I should say the second part too."

Priya looked at him with something that was not warmth and was not approval. Recognition, maybe, of a kind of sentence she had also had to prune. "Say both, if you say either. He is not a fool. He is a person who has been hired, in the past, to make sure a team can be explained to a director. Directors ask about degrees when they are anxious about risk they can't see. He may be anxious. You don't have to treat the anxiety as a puzzle to win."

"All right."

"What are you going to do tonight, about the three rows?"

The question was so close to his own private end of the day that he answered without arranging it. "Look up the third id. Decide whether I can put them into real statuses without inventing one. If I can, validate the constraint. If I can't, write to Grace and tell her the three files are outside the rule I told her the system had. I won't mention this room. She doesn't need your name on my mistake."

"Don't confess a client into a panic at nine in the evening to prove to yourself that you were honest with me. Look first. If the files are dormant, the morning will do. If one of them is active, then the evening is hers, not yours." Priya picked up the notebook. "I'm going to tell Denise that I want you to talk to Chris. I am also going to tell her that the document I sponsored had a false sentence in it, and that you corrected it before I praised it, and that I still don't know whether you will be able to wait for someone else's migration. She will write several question marks. She is allowed."

"Will the false sentence end it?"

"Not by itself. It will be in the room with Chris. So will the parking lot, in whatever sentence Denise managed to live with. So will the money, and the fact that you didn't pretend to have a plan for Grace. If you want to withdraw before that room exists, tell Denise today. She can close an exception more easily than she can open one."

"I don't want to withdraw."

"Then go home on the streetcar you came on, and do not write me a follow-up that repairs the hour. There is nothing to repair. There is a constraint to validate or a client to tell. Those are not for my inbox."

He stood. The water cup was empty and he did not know where to put it. She took it from him and set it on the table, a small domestic motion that undid some of the ceremony he had been about to add. At the door he remembered the whiteboard, and the guard, and the American date, and he almost asked what the guard ought to have counted. He swallowed the question. It was a real question and it was also a way of leaving as a colleague. She had not offered him that. She had offered him a second room, with a man who was not here, and a list of risks with his name beside them.

In the hall she walked him as far as the frosted door. Denise was on the bench, laptop open, talking to no one, her notes in her lap. She looked up.

"We're done," Priya said. "I want him to see Chris. I'll write you the reasons, including the ugly one. Not in the hallway."

Denise looked at Wale, and he saw her updating a model in real time: the sponsor had not cooled, the candidate had not become glossy, something ugly existed and was going to be written down. She did not ask him to soothe the update. "I'll send you the next conversation if Chris agrees to have it. It will not be tomorrow. He is changing a diaper and not reading my mail, if he is sane. If you need to tell me you are out, tell me before I write the pack."

"I'm not out."

"All right." She glanced at Priya. "Eat something. You get precise and mean when you don't, and he can apparently hear precise, so the mean will be wasted."

"I'm not mean," Priya said, without force. "I'm late for a guard that pages on one job." She turned to Wale. "The pronunciation. I had it wrong until Denise said it in the kitchen. I'll say it correctly to Chris. It will not be the thing that matters, and it should not be a thing you have to spend."

"Thank you."

He left them there, Denise already turning a page, Priya already half turned toward the inner door and the American date. The elevator was slow, as promised. In it he looked at his hands and not at the door. He had the two identifiers in the notebook and a question mark where the third should be. He had said the sentence. He had been seen saying the smaller version second, and the seeing had been fair, and the fairness sat in him with a sting that was not injury. On Adelaide the sidewalk was still closed. He went around. The compressor was louder than it had been in the morning. He did not put his headphones on. He wanted the city at the volume it actually was, because the room had been so quiet.

At the corner he took out his phone and did not write to Priya, and did not write to Grace, and did not write to Nabil. He opened the note where he kept production oddities and typed the two identifiers and then, under them, the names of the statuses he believed those files were really in. He was sure of one. He was not sure of the second. The uncertainty was smaller than the missing third and, in its way, worse, because it was the kind he could have checked in a minute if he had been willing to open a tunnel on the sidewalk and stare at a production query in the glare. He put the phone away. The morning would do, unless, when he looked, one of them was active. Priya had been right about the evening. He could accept a rightness that was not his without giving it a speech.

The streetcar west was crowded. He stood and held the bar and watched a boy do homework on a phone with his mother correcting him in a voice too low for the words to carry. At Strachan the car stalled in a way that meant nothing, a pause, a bell, a continuation. He thought of Laila, whom he had never met, marking a thing served because a person was on the phone and the affidavit was still in a scanner. He thought of the word pending, and of how a correct word could accuse. He thought of Chris, who would ask about a degree, and of the two sentences he would have to say if he said either. He did not arrange them. Arrangement was how the note had gone false: a sentence written in the evening, a date corrected in the morning, the middle left to become true later.

When he got off at his stop the light was the light of late September, generous and already thinner than it had been at noon. He walked toward Sorauren with his bag on one shoulder. In the bag the notebook knocked against the laptop at each step, a small practical sound. He was already, against Priya's instruction and also beneath it, composing the query he would run. He stopped composing. There would be a chair and a lamp and the actual rows, which would not care what kind of person he had been in a room with a dead plant. Two offices would still be paying him in the morning. A woman in Mississauga would still be trusting a screen he had allowed to be cleaner than its table. He had been recommended by a sentence he had not heard, sponsored by a person who was tired, remembered by a recruiter for a word he had made her strike. None of that had validated the constraint. He climbed the stairs. The third identifier was somewhere in a table, sitting in an illegal state, patient, and unimproved by the fact that he had finally said so.
